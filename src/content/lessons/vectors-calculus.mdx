## Components do the work

Olympiad problems are almost never solved with vectors as arrows; they are
solved by picking axes and writing components. Any vector splits as
$A_x = A\cos\theta$, $A_y = A\sin\theta$, and two products cover essentially
every use:

$$\vec A \cdot \vec B = AB\cos\theta, \qquad |\vec A \times \vec B| = AB\sin\theta$$

The dot product answers "how much of this is along that": work, flux, and
checking perpendicularity. The cross product answers "how much of this is
perpendicular to that": torque, angular momentum, magnetic force. If the dot
product vanishes the vectors are perpendicular; if the cross product vanishes
they are parallel.

## Choosing axes

The single highest-return decision in a mechanics problem is where to point
the axes. On an incline, tilt them along the slope so the acceleration lies on
one axis and only gravity needs splitting. In circular motion, point one axis
at the center. Good axes turn two coupled equations into one.

## The calculus you actually need

F=ma is written to be solvable without calculus, but calculus shortens
half of it. Derivatives of polynomials and trig functions, the chain rule, and
simple integrals are enough. The one approximation you will use everywhere:

$$\sin\theta \approx \theta, \qquad \cos\theta \approx 1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2}, \qquad (1+x)^n \approx 1 + nx$$

valid for small $\theta$ and small $x$.

<Aside label="Reading the problem">
  "Small oscillations", "slightly displaced", "to first order": all of these
  are instructions to expand and throw away the higher terms. Recognizing the
  phrasing is most of the work.
</Aside>
