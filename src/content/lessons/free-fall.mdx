Drop a stone down a well and count. Three seconds later you hear the splash.
How deep is the water? The counting is easy; turning it into a number is not
obvious, because part of those three seconds was stone falling and part was
sound coming back up, and nothing in the problem tells you where the split is.

That question, and most of the ones in this unit, come down to a single fact
about the world: near the ground, with air resistance out of the picture, every
object falls with the same downward acceleration. Not the same speed — the same
acceleration. A hammer and a feather dropped together on the Moon land together,
and the astronauts of Apollo 15 filmed it to prove the point. Once you accept
that the acceleration is a known constant, the graphs from the
[previous lesson](/guide/motion-graphs) do the rest of the work.

## The one number and the three equations

Call the downward acceleration $g$. Near the Earth's surface $g = 9.8$ m/s²,
which means a falling object gains 9.8 m/s of downward speed every second. After
one second of falling from rest it is moving at 9.8 m/s, after two seconds
19.6 m/s, after three seconds 29.4 m/s. That is the whole answer to "a wrench is
dropped and falls for 3.0 s, how fast is it going".

$g$ is a positive number, always. Whether gravity speeds an object up or slows
it down is decided by the sign you attach to it, not by $g$ itself. Choose a
positive direction first and write it on your paper. From here on,
**up is positive**, so the acceleration is

$$a = -g = -9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$$

and it is $-9.8$ on the way up, at the very top, and on the way down. Gravity
does not know or care which way the object happens to be moving.

Now recall the three constant-acceleration results from the previous lesson.
Velocity changes at a steady rate, so it is linear in time. Position is the area
under the velocity graph, which is a trapezium, so it is quadratic in time. And
eliminating $t$ between those two gives a relation with no time in it at all.
Written with $y$ for height, $y_0$ for the starting height and $v_0$ for the
starting velocity:

$$v = v_0 - gt$$

$$y = y_0 + v_0 t - \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2$$

$$v^2 = v_0^2 - 2g(y - y_0)$$

Read them in words. The first says the velocity falls steadily from whatever it
started at. The second says the height is where you started, plus how far you
would have drifted at the old velocity, minus the sag that gravity adds. The
third is the one to reach for whenever a problem mentions two heights and two
speeds but never mentions time.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  $v_0$ is a velocity, not a speed: it carries a sign. Thrown up at 20 m/s means
  $v_0 = +20$; thrown down at 20 m/s means $v_0 = -20$. Getting that one sign
  wrong is the most common way these problems go bad.
</Aside>

## Three graphs that never change shape

Every vertical-motion problem here has the same three graphs behind it. The acceleration graph is a flat line at $-9.8$. The velocity graph is a
straight line sloping down at that rate, crossing zero at the top of the flight.
The height graph is a parabola whose peak sits directly above that crossing.

Two facts fall straight out of the velocity line. First, the object reaches its
highest point when $v = 0$, which by $v = v_0 - gt$ happens at

$$t_{\text{top}} = \frac{v_0}{g}$$

A ball thrown up at 14.7 m/s takes $14.7/9.8 = 1.5$ s to reach the top. Second,
the velocity line is symmetric about that instant: 1.5 s before the top the
velocity was $+14.7$, and 1.5 s after the top it is $-14.7$. So an object passes
every height twice, with the same speed both times, opposite in direction. A
ball thrown up from the ground at 25 m/s takes $25/9.8 = 2.55$ s to rise, and
the same again to come back, giving 5.1 s in the air.

Put $v = 0$ into the third equation and the maximum rise above the launch point
is $v_0^2/2g$. For $v_0 = 20$ m/s that is $400/19.6 = 20.4$ m. Notice it goes as
the square of the launch speed: throw twice as fast and you go four times as
high.

### Worked example 1: speed partway down

A ball is thrown straight up at 15 m/s from the top of a 50 m tower. How fast is
it moving when it is 20 m above the ground?

The problem gives two heights and one speed and asks for another speed. No time
is mentioned anywhere, so use the third equation. Put the origin at the ground,
so $y_0 = 50$ m and $y = 20$ m, with $v_0 = +15$ m/s.

$$v^2 = (15)^2 - 2(9.8)(20 - 50)$$

$$v^2 = 225 - 2(9.8)(-30) = 225 + 588 = 813$$

$$v = \sqrt{813} = 28.5\ \mathrm{m/s}$$

The square root is what makes this work: $v^2$ does not care about the sign of
$v$, so the answer covers both the upward and the downward pass, and here only
the downward one is below the tower top. The question asked for a speed, so
28.5 m/s is the answer. Had it asked for velocity, the answer would be
$-28.5$ m/s.

Also notice that the launch direction dropped out. Thrown *down* at 15 m/s from
the same tower, the ball passes 20 m at exactly the same speed, because $v_0$
appears only as $v_0^2$.

## When you cannot avoid the quadratic

Sometimes a problem insists on a time, and then you have to solve
$y = y_0 + v_0 t - \tfrac12 g t^2$ for $t$, which is a quadratic. That is not a
sign you have gone wrong. It is the normal state of affairs whenever the object
starts and ends at different heights, because the flight is no longer symmetric.

### Worked example 2: thrown up from a roof

A ball leaves a 25 m roof at 18 m/s straight up. When does it hit the ground?

Ground level is $y = 0$, and the release point is $y_0 = 25$ m with
$v_0 = +18$ m/s. Set $y = 0$:

$$0 = 25 + 18t - 4.9t^2$$

Rearrange so the $t^2$ term is positive:

$$4.9t^2 - 18t - 25 = 0$$

$$t = \frac{18 \pm \sqrt{(-18)^2 - 4(4.9)(-25)}}{2(4.9)} = \frac{18 \pm \sqrt{324 + 490}}{9.8}$$

$$t = \frac{18 \pm 28.53}{9.8}$$

The two roots are $t = 4.75$ s and $t = -1.07$ s. The negative root is real
mathematics, not a mistake: it is when the ball *would have* left the ground if
the whole parabola were extended backwards. It is not the answer here, so
discard it and keep $t = 4.75$ s.

The landing speed comes free: $v = 18 - 9.8(4.75) = -28.5$ m/s, so the ball
lands at 28.5 m/s. Compare that with the 28.53 hiding inside the square root a
moment ago. That is not a coincidence — the discriminant contains
$v_0^2 + 2gh$, which is precisely $v^2$ at the ground.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Sign errors, almost always, and they come from mixing conventions halfway
  through. If up is positive then $g$ enters every equation as $-9.8$, a
  downward throw has a negative $v_0$, and a point below the origin has a
  negative $y$. Write "up +" at the top of your page and check each substitution
  against it. The other frequent slip is using a distance fallen where the
  equations want a change in height: falling 30 m means $y - y_0 = -30$ m, not
  $+30$.
</Aside>

## Two objects, one clock

A problem with two moving objects looks twice as hard and usually is not,
provided you write a position equation for each with the *same* $t$ and the
*same* origin, then say in algebra what the question says in words.

Take two balls released at the same instant: one dropped from 60 m, one thrown
up from the ground at 30 m/s. Where do they meet?

$$y_1 = 60 - 4.9t^2, \qquad y_2 = 30t - 4.9t^2$$

Meeting means $y_1 = y_2$. Subtract:

$$60 - 4.9t^2 = 30t - 4.9t^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; 60 = 30t \;\Longrightarrow\; t = 2.0\ \mathrm{s}$$

The $4.9t^2$ terms cancelled, and that cancellation is the real content of the
problem. Both balls are pulled down identically, so relative to each other
gravity does nothing at all: the gap between them closes at the constant
30 m/s the thrown ball started with. Sixty metres at 30 m/s is two seconds.
Then put $t = 2.0$ into either equation:

$$y = 60 - 4.9(2.0)^2 = 60 - 19.6 = 40.4\ \mathrm{m}$$

The same cancellation solves problems that look unrelated. Two stones dropped
from the same bridge one second apart: the moment the second is released, the
first is already moving at 9.8 m/s and is 4.9 m below. After that the gap grows
at a steady 9.8 m/s because both accelerate alike. Two seconds later — three
seconds after the first release — the separation is
$4.9 + 9.8(2.0) = 24.5$ m.

## Motion in stages

Many of the harder problems are not one motion but two glued together. The rule
is the same every time: solve the first stage completely, then use its final
position and final velocity as the starting conditions of the second. Do not try
to write one equation across the join, because the acceleration is different on
each side of it.

A model rocket climbing at 4.0 m/s² for 20 s is a clean case. At burnout it has
reached $v = 4.0 \times 20 = 80$ m/s and a height of
$\tfrac12 (4.0)(20)^2 = 800$ m. Only now does free fall begin, from 800 m with
$v_0 = +80$ m/s, adding $80^2/(2 \times 9.8) = 326.5$ m. Maximum height:
1126.5 m. A reader who forgets the 800 m, or who uses $g$ during the burn, will
be out by hundreds of metres.

A bouncing ball is the same idea. Dropped from 5.0 m it hits at
$\sqrt{2(9.8)(5.0)} = 9.90$ m/s after $\sqrt{2(5.0)/9.8} = 1.01$ s. If it
rebounds with 80% of that, it leaves the floor at 7.92 m/s, and a throw up at
7.92 m/s returns to the floor after $2(7.92)/9.8 = 1.62$ s. Second impact at
$1.01 + 1.62 = 2.63$ s.

### Worked example 3: the depth of the well

Back to the stone and the splash, 3.00 s after release, with sound at 340 m/s.

Two stages again, but this one has a twist: you are told the total time and
neither piece of it. Name the unknown depth $d$ and write each stage in terms of
it. The fall takes $t_1$ with $d = \tfrac12 g t_1^2$, so
$t_1 = \sqrt{2d/g}$. The sound comes back up at constant speed, taking
$t_2 = d/340$. Their sum is what you heard:

$$\sqrt{\frac{2d}{9.8}} + \frac{d}{340} = 3.00$$

That is awkward in $d$, so use $t_1$ as the unknown instead, since
$d = 4.9t_1^2$:

$$t_1 + \frac{4.9t_1^2}{340} = 3.00$$

$$0.014412\,t_1^2 + t_1 - 3.00 = 0$$

$$t_1 = \frac{-1 + \sqrt{1 + 4(0.014412)(3.00)}}{2(0.014412)} = \frac{-1 + \sqrt{1.17294}}{0.028824}$$

$$t_1 = \frac{0.08302}{0.028824} = 2.880\ \mathrm{s}$$

$$d = 4.9(2.880)^2 = 40.7\ \mathrm{m}$$

Check it against the crude answer. Ignoring the sound entirely gives
$4.9(3.00)^2 = 44.1$ m, and the true depth is a little less than that, as it
must be, since some of the three seconds was spent on the return trip. The sound
leg took $40.7/340 = 0.12$ s, which is small but not nothing.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Whenever a problem hands you a *total* time or a *total* distance covering two
  different motions, name the shared unknown, write both legs in terms of it, and
  add. The same move solves the ball that passes a 1.50 m window in 0.120 s: the
  window crossing fixes the speed at the top of the window, and from that speed
  you run $v^2 = 2gh$ backwards to find how far above it was released.
</Aside>

## When the acceleration refuses to stay constant

Everything so far assumed no air. Real falls do not keep accelerating forever;
a skydiver levels off near 55 m/s and a raindrop near 9 m/s. Drag pushes back
against the motion and grows with speed, so the net downward force shrinks as
the object speeds up. Write the slow-speed case, where drag is proportional to
speed with some constant $b$:

$$m\frac{dv}{dt} = mg - bv$$

At $v = 0$ the acceleration is the full $g$. As $v$ climbs, the right-hand side
shrinks. It reaches zero at the **terminal speed**, where the object stops
accelerating and falls forever at a fixed rate:

$$v_t = \frac{mg}{b}$$

You do not need calculus to find $v_t$ — set the net force to zero and solve.
Getting the whole history $v(t)$ does need calculus, and the result you will be
given is

$$v(t) = v_t\left(1 - e^{-gt/v_t}\right)$$

Check the two ends. At $t = 0$ the bracket is $1 - 1 = 0$, as it should be. For
small $t$, $e^{-x} \approx 1 - x$, so $v \approx v_t (gt/v_t) = gt$, the
no-drag answer — drag barely matters at the start. For large $t$ the exponential
dies and $v \to v_t$ from below. The object never overshoots.

With $v_t = 6.0$ m/s, the speed at $t = 1.20$ s is

$$v = 6.0\left(1 - e^{-(9.8)(1.20)/6.0}\right) = 6.0\left(1 - e^{-1.96}\right)$$

$$e^{-1.96} = 0.1409, \qquad v = 6.0(0.8591) = 5.15\ \mathrm{m/s}$$

The marked time <i>v</i><sub>t</sub>/<i>g</i> is where the fall is 63% of the way to terminal.</>}>

Constant acceleration would have predicted $9.8 \times 1.20 = 11.8$ m/s, nearly
twice as much. For fast, blunt objects the drag goes as $v^2$ instead, terminal
speed becomes $v_t = \sqrt{mg/c}$, and the useful form is in terms of distance
fallen $y$ rather than time:

$$v^2 = v_t^2\left(1 - e^{-2gy/v_t^2}\right)$$

Reaching 40 m/s with $v_t = 50$ m/s needs
$1600 = 2500(1 - e^{-2gy/2500})$, so $e^{-2gy/2500} = 0.36$, giving
$2(9.8)y/2500 = \ln(1/0.36) = 1.0217$ and $y = 130$ m. Both formulas have the
same skeleton: a terminal value, and an exponential that measures how much of
the way there you are.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Terminal speed is not a property of the material, it is a balance of two
  forces, so it depends on mass. A heavy ball and a light ball of the same shape
  have the same $b$ but different $mg$, and the heavy one falls faster. That is
  why a hammer beats a feather in air and ties with it in vacuum.
</Aside>

## Recognising a free-fall problem

The tell is that gravity is the only thing acting and the motion is along one
vertical line. Words like dropped, released, thrown straight up, falls freely,
neglect air resistance all point here. Once you are sure, three questions pick
the equation for you.

Does the problem mention time, or ask for it? If yes, you need
$v = v_0 - gt$ or the quadratic $y = y_0 + v_0 t - \tfrac12 gt^2$. If time is
never mentioned in either the given data or the question, use
$v^2 = v_0^2 - 2g(y-y_0)$ and save yourself a step.

Is there more than one moving object, or more than one phase of motion? Two
objects means two position equations sharing one clock, then set them equal or
subtract them — and expect the $t^2$ terms to cancel. Two phases, like a rocket
burn then a coast, or a fall then a bounce, means solving the first stage fully
and feeding its end into the start of the second.

Does the problem mention air resistance, terminal speed, or give you an
exponential? Then constant acceleration is dead and you are plugging into the
drag formulas above. The giveaway is any phrase about a speed that stops
increasing.

One last habit worth building: before solving, estimate. A drop of a few metres
takes about a second and ends near 10 m/s; a ten-storey fall takes about two and
a half seconds. If your algebra returns 300 m/s for a stone off a bridge, the
sign of $g$ is probably wrong somewhere.

Every motion so far has been along a single vertical line. Next you keep
the same $-g$ but give the object a sideways velocity too, and find that the
horizontal and vertical halves of the motion never talk to each other. That is
[two-dimensional and projectile motion](/guide/projectiles).
