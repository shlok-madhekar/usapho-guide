## One photon, one electron

Shine light on a clean metal surface and electrons come off. The wave picture
predicts that a dim beam should eventually accumulate enough energy to free an
electron, given time, and that a brighter beam should free faster electrons.
Neither happens.

What actually happens is that light arrives as discrete quanta of energy

$$E = hf$$

and each ejected electron is freed by exactly one of them. That single
assumption explains everything the wave picture got wrong.

An electron near the surface is bound. The minimum energy needed to pull it out
is the **work function** $\phi$, a property of the metal — about 2.3 eV for
sodium, 4.7 eV for copper. Whatever the photon carries beyond that becomes
kinetic energy:

$$K_{max} = hf - \phi$$

The subscript matters. Electrons deeper in the metal lose energy on the way out
and emerge with less, so $K_{max}$ describes the luckiest ones.

## Why brightness is the wrong knob

Set $K_{max} = 0$ to get the **threshold frequency** $f_0 = \phi/h$. Below it,
no photon in the beam has enough energy to free anything, and no amount of
intensity helps — you are delivering more photons, each still too weak. The
emission stops abruptly at $f_0$, not gradually.

Above threshold, intensity controls how many photons arrive per second, so it
controls the current. Frequency controls what each photon carries, so it
controls the energy. The two knobs are independent, which is exactly what a
wave theory cannot produce.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Doubling intensity does not double $K_{max}$, and halving intensity does not
  introduce a delay before emission. Emission begins within nanoseconds even at
  the faintest intensity, because the electron only ever needed one photon.
</Aside>

## Reading the graph

Measure $K_{max}$ by applying a reverse voltage until the current just stops.
At that stopping potential $V_s$,

$$eV_s = hf - \phi$$

Plot $V_s$ against $f$ and you get a straight line. The slope is $h/e$ — the
same for every metal, which is the striking part. The horizontal intercept is
$f_0$, and the vertical intercept is $-\phi/e$. Changing the metal shifts the
line sideways without tilting it.

This is a real measurement of Planck's constant with a voltmeter, which is why
the experiment settled the argument.
