## Describing a wave

A travelling wave carries energy without carrying matter. Its basic relations:

$$v = f\lambda, \qquad y(x,t) = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$$

with $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ and $\omega = 2\pi f$. The speed is set by the medium,
not by the source: on a string $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$, so tightening a string
raises its pitch by making waves travel faster along it.

## Superposition

Waves add. Everything interesting in this topic follows from that.

Two waves of equal frequency travelling in opposite directions form a standing
wave, with nodes fixed in place. For a string fixed at both ends, only
wavelengths that fit produce a standing pattern:

$$\lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n}, \qquad f_n = \frac{nv}{2L}$$

A pipe open at both ends behaves the same way. A pipe closed at one end needs
a node at the closed end and an antinode at the open one, so only odd
harmonics survive and the fundamental is an octave lower than an open pipe of
the same length.

Two waves of slightly different frequency produce beats at $|f_1 - f_2|$,
which is how a piano is tuned by ear.

## Doppler

When the source or observer moves, the observed frequency shifts:

$$f' = f\,\frac{v \pm v_o}{v \mp v_s}$$

Rather than memorize the signs, reason about the direction: approaching always
raises the pitch, receding always lowers it. Pick the signs that produce that,
then compute.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  A moving source and a moving observer are not symmetric. The source changes
  the wavelength in the medium; the observer changes the rate at which
  unchanged wavefronts arrive. At low speeds the results nearly agree, but the
  exact expressions differ, and olympiad problems choose speeds where it shows.
</Aside>
