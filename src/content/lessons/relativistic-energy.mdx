## A stationary object with energy in it

In the last lesson you pushed clocks and rulers around: [moving clocks run
slow, moving rulers are short](/guide/sr-kinematics), and every frame disagrees
about when and where. Nothing there said anything about energy. But something
has to give. If you push a particle with a constant force forever, Newton says
its speed grows without limit, and relativity says it can never reach $c$. The
force is still doing work the whole time. Where is that work going?

The answer is that energy and momentum have to be rebuilt from scratch, and
when you do the rebuilding you find something nobody expected: an object that
is not moving at all still has energy, and the amount is fixed by its mass. A
gram of anything, sitting on a table, holds about $9 \times 10^{13}$ joules.
That is the fact this lesson is built around, and the rest of it is learning to
compute with it.

## Total energy, rest energy, kinetic energy

Write $\gamma$ for the Lorentz factor you already know,

$$\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$$

where $v$ is the object's speed and $c = 3.00 \times 10^8$ m/s. At rest
$\gamma = 1$; at $v = 0.866c$, $\gamma = 2$; as $v \to c$, $\gamma \to \infty$.

The **total energy** of a free particle of mass $m$ turns out to be

$$E = \gamma m c^2$$

and the reason to believe it is this. Set the particle at rest, so $\gamma = 1$,
and you get $E_0 = mc^2$, the **rest energy** — the energy the thing has just
for existing. Anything above that floor is energy of motion, so define the
**kinetic energy** as the excess:

$$K = E - E_0 = (\gamma - 1)mc^2$$

That is a definition, not a new law, and it has to agree with $\tfrac12 mv^2$
when the particle is slow or the whole of mechanics you already learned would
be wrong. Check it. For small $v/c$ the binomial expansion gives
$\gamma \approx 1 + \tfrac12 v^2/c^2$, so

$$K \approx \left(1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\frac{v^2}{c^2} - 1\right)mc^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$$

The $c^2$ cancels and Newton reappears. The classical formula was never a
different theory; it was the first term of this one.

Notice what $K = (\gamma - 1)mc^2$ says about that constant force. As
$v \to c$, $\gamma \to \infty$, so the kinetic energy runs off to infinity while
the speed creeps towards a wall. The work you do is not lost. It goes into
$\gamma$, not into $v$.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Three numbers cover most of this topic. An electron's rest energy is
  $0.511$ MeV, a proton's is $938.3$ MeV, and a neutral pion's is $135.0$ MeV.
  A 1 MeV electron is wildly relativistic. A 1 MeV proton is barely moving.
</Aside>

## Momentum with no ceiling

Momentum gets the same treatment. The relativistic momentum of a particle is

$$p = \gamma m v$$

Again the low-speed limit is the check: $\gamma \to 1$ and you recover $p = mv$.
And again the factor $\gamma$ is what removes the ceiling. Speed is capped at
$c$, but $\gamma$ is not capped at anything, so momentum has no maximum. This
is why an accelerator is rated in GeV and never in metres per second — past a
few MeV every electron in the machine is moving at essentially $c$ and the speed
stops carrying information.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Do not write $K = \tfrac12 \gamma m v^2$, and do not invent a
  "relativistic mass" $\gamma m$ that grows with speed. Both survive in old
  books and both will cost you points, because mass is the one quantity every
  observer agrees on. Whenever $\gamma$ appears it belongs to the energy or the
  momentum, never to $m$.
</Aside>

## The relation that does the real work

You now have two equations, $E = \gamma mc^2$ and $p = \gamma mv$, each carrying
the unknowns $\gamma$ and $v$. In a problem you usually know neither. So get rid
of them. Divide the second by the first:

$$\frac{pc}{E} = \frac{\gamma m v c}{\gamma m c^2} = \frac{v}{c}$$

That is already useful: the ratio $pc/E$ *is* the speed in units of $c$. Now
square both originals and subtract:

$$E^2 - (pc)^2 = \gamma^2 m^2 c^4 - \gamma^2 m^2 v^2 c^2 = \gamma^2 m^2 c^4 \left(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)$$

The bracket is exactly $1/\gamma^2$, so the $\gamma$'s cancel and you are left
with

$$E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2$$

No $\gamma$, no $v$. This is the single most useful equation in the subject.
Read it as a right triangle: the legs are $pc$ and $mc^2$, the hypotenuse is
$E$. Different observers see different $E$ and different $p$ — the triangle
changes shape — but the leg $mc^2$ is the same for all of them. The combination
$E^2 - (pc)^2$ is an **invariant**.

Set $m = 0$ and the triangle collapses onto one leg:

$$E = pc$$

A photon has momentum even though it has no mass, and its speed is
$pc/E = c$ exactly, as it must be. Combined with $E = hc/\lambda$, this is what
lets light push on a solar sail.

## Units that keep the arithmetic short

Never put joules and kilograms into these formulas unless a problem forces you.
Use the electronvolt: $1$ eV is the energy an electron picks up crossing $1$
volt. Then measure mass in MeV/$c^2$ and momentum in MeV/$c$. The point of
those odd units is that the $c$'s cancel wherever they appear. The invariant
becomes a statement about three numbers all in MeV:

$$E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2 \quad \longrightarrow \quad E^2 = p^2 + m^2 \ \text{(all in MeV)}$$

**Worked example 1.** An electron has kinetic energy $2.00$ MeV. Find its total
energy, its momentum, and its speed.

Start from the definition of kinetic energy. Total energy is rest energy plus
kinetic energy:

$$E = mc^2 + K = 0.511 + 2.00 = 2.511 \ \text{MeV}$$

For the momentum use the invariant, rearranged:

$$(pc)^2 = E^2 - (mc^2)^2 = (2.511)^2 - (0.511)^2 = 6.305 - 0.261 = 6.044$$

so $pc = \sqrt{6.044} = 2.459$ MeV, that is $p = 2.459$ MeV/$c$. For the speed,
use $v/c = pc/E$:

$$\frac{v}{c} = \frac{2.459}{2.511} = 0.979$$

Three questions, one triangle, no $\gamma$ anywhere. If you had wanted $\gamma$
it is $E/mc^2 = 2.511/0.511 = 4.91$.

## Collisions and decays: what is conserved and what is not

In any reaction — a collision, a decay, a particle absorbing a photon — two
things are conserved:

- total energy $E$, summed over all particles before and after;
- total momentum $\vec{p}$, each component separately.

Mass is **not** conserved. Kinetic energy is **not** conserved. A pion at rest
vanishes into two photons: $135.0$ MeV of mass before, zero mass after, with
every bit of it now carried as photon energy. This is not mass "turning into"
some other substance. Rest energy and kinetic energy are the same currency, and
a reaction is free to move the total between the two columns as long as the sum
stays fixed.

That also explains the Sun. It radiates $3.85 \times 10^{26}$ W, and by
$E = mc^2$ that outflow of energy is an outflow of mass at
$P/c^2 = 3.85 \times 10^{26} / 9.00 \times 10^{16} = 4.28 \times 10^9$ kg every
second.

**Worked example 2.** A charged pion at rest ($139.6$ MeV) decays into a muon
($105.7$ MeV) and a neutrino you may treat as massless. Find the muon's kinetic
energy.

Write down conservation first, before touching any numbers. The pion is at
rest, so total momentum before is zero, so the muon and neutrino come out
back-to-back with equal momentum magnitudes $p$. Energy conservation says

$$m_\pi c^2 = E_\mu + E_\nu$$

The neutrino is massless, so $E_\nu = pc$. The muon obeys the invariant, so
$E_\mu = \sqrt{(pc)^2 + (m_\mu c^2)^2}$. One unknown, one equation. Isolate the
root and square:

$$m_\pi c^2 - pc = \sqrt{(pc)^2 + (m_\mu c^2)^2}$$

$$(m_\pi c^2)^2 - 2 m_\pi c^2 \, (pc) + (pc)^2 = (pc)^2 + (m_\mu c^2)^2$$

The $(pc)^2$ cancels on both sides — that cancellation is the whole trick and it
happens every time one product is massless. Solve:

$$pc = \frac{(m_\pi c^2)^2 - (m_\mu c^2)^2}{2 m_\pi c^2} = \frac{139.6^2 - 105.7^2}{2(139.6)} = \frac{19488 - 11172}{279.2} = \frac{8316}{279.2} = 29.78 \ \text{MeV}$$

Then the muon's total energy is $E_\mu = 139.6 - 29.78 = 109.8$ MeV, and its
kinetic energy is what is left after you take out its rest energy:

$$K_\mu = 109.8 - 105.7 = 4.1 \ \text{MeV}$$

Small, because the muon is nearly as heavy as the pion and there was not much
spare mass to convert. The same method handles any two-body decay from rest,
including one where both products have mass: you would just carry two square
roots instead of one.

## Invariant mass, thresholds and colliders

Here is the technique that cracks the hard problems. Take a whole system of
particles, add up their energies to get $E_{\rm tot}$ and add up their momentum
vectors to get $\vec{p}_{\rm tot}$. Then the quantity

$$s = E_{\rm tot}^2 - (p_{\rm tot}c)^2$$

is invariant: every observer computes the same number for it, because energy
and momentum transform together exactly the way $E$ and $p$ of a single
particle do. And $\sqrt{s}$ has a plain meaning — it is the total energy the
system would have in the frame where its net momentum is zero, the
**centre-of-momentum frame**. That is the energy budget genuinely available for
making new particles, because anything left over as bulk motion is momentum you
are still required to conserve.

A reaction is **at threshold** when the products are made with nothing to
spare: in the centre-of-momentum frame they all come out at rest, moving
together. So the threshold condition is

$$\sqrt{s} = \sum (\text{rest energies of the products})$$

**Worked example 3.** A proton beam hits protons at rest to make neutral pions,
$p + p \to p + p + \pi^0$. With $m_p c^2 = 938.3$ MeV and
$m_\pi c^2 = 135.0$ MeV, find the minimum kinetic energy of the beam proton.

Compute $s$ twice — once after, once before.

*After*, at threshold, in the centre-of-momentum frame the three products sit
still, so $\sqrt{s}$ is just their rest energies added:

$$\sqrt{s} = 2(938.3) + 135.0 = 2011.6 \ \text{MeV}$$

*Before*, work in the lab. The beam proton has energy $E$ and momentum $p$; the
target has energy $938.3$ and no momentum. So

$$s = (E + m_p c^2)^2 - (pc)^2 = E^2 - (pc)^2 + 2E\,m_p c^2 + (m_p c^2)^2$$

and $E^2 - (pc)^2 = (m_p c^2)^2$ for the beam proton, leaving the tidy result

$$s = 2(m_p c^2)^2 + 2 E \, m_p c^2$$

Set the two expressions for $s$ equal:

$$2011.6^2 = 2(938.3)^2 + 2E(938.3)$$

$$4.0465 \times 10^6 = 1.7608 \times 10^6 + 1876.6\,E$$

$$E = \frac{2.2857 \times 10^6}{1876.6} = 1218 \ \text{MeV}$$

That is total energy, so subtract the rest energy for the kinetic energy the
question asked for:

$$K = 1218 - 938.3 = 280 \ \text{MeV}$$

You had to supply $280$ MeV of motion to make $135$ MeV of pion. The surplus is
not waste — it is the kinetic energy the products are forced to keep, because
momentum going in was not zero and cannot come out zero.

That surplus is why colliders exist. Fire two beams of energy $E$ head-on and
the total momentum is zero already, so $\sqrt{s} = 2E$: every joule counts. In a
fixed-target machine $s$ only grows like $2E m c^2$, so $\sqrt{s}$ grows like
$\sqrt{E}$ — doubling the reach costs four times the beam energy. Two $7.00$ GeV
electron beams give $\sqrt{s} = 14.0$ GeV, and matching that with a fixed target
would take a beam of roughly $s/(2m_e c^2) = (14000)^2/(2 \times 0.511)$ MeV,
which is about $1.9 \times 10^{5}$ GeV. No one is building that.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  At threshold the products are at rest in the centre-of-momentum frame, not in
  the lab. Setting the lab kinetic energy of the products to zero is the most
  common wrong move in this whole topic, and it gives $135$ MeV instead of
  $280$ MeV above. Also watch the conserved quantum numbers: you cannot make a
  lone antiproton, so the cheapest reaction is
  $p + p \to p + p + p + \bar p$, and the products' rest energy in the threshold
  condition is $4 m_p c^2$, not $3 m_p c^2$.
</Aside>

## Boosting a decay: the opening angle

One more manoeuvre, because the hardest problems use it. Solve the decay in the
frame where it is simple — usually the parent's rest frame — then transform the
answers to the lab.

A neutral pion at rest sends out two photons of $67.5$ MeV each, back to back,
at some angle $\theta^\*$ to whatever axis you like. Now let the pion fly with
Lorentz factor $\gamma$ and speed $\beta c$. The photons' directions swing
forward. The angle each one makes with the flight direction in the lab comes
from transforming its momentum components:

$$\tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta^\*}{\gamma(\cos\theta^\* + \beta)}$$

The denominator carries the $\gamma$, so every lab angle is squeezed towards
zero as $\gamma$ grows. The two photons are still opposite in the pion's frame
but they are no longer opposite in yours.

**Worked example 4.** A neutral pion with total energy $10.0$ GeV decays into
two photons. Find the smallest possible angle between them in the lab.

First get $\gamma$, which is total energy over rest energy:

$$\gamma = \frac{10000}{135.0} = 74.07$$

The opening angle $\Theta$ turns out to be smallest for the symmetric case,
$\theta^\* = 90^\circ$, where both photons make the same lab angle with the
axis. Put $\theta^\* = 90^\circ$ into the formula: $\tan\theta = 1/(\gamma\beta)$
for each photon, so $\Theta = 2\theta$ and, tidying the trigonometry,
$\sin(\Theta/2) = 1/\gamma$. Then

$$\sin(\Theta/2) = \frac{1}{74.07} = 0.01350 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \Theta/2 = 0.774^\circ$$

$$\Theta = 1.55^\circ$$

At high energy the two photons arrive almost on top of each other — which is
exactly the problem detector builders have to solve.

## Recognising when this is the tool

Some signals, roughly in order of how reliable they are.

**Rest energies are quoted in MeV or GeV.** If a problem hands you $938.3$ MeV
instead of a mass in kilograms, it wants relativistic energy accounting and
almost certainly wants the invariant $E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2$.

**Speeds near $c$, or a $\gamma$, or an accelerating voltage in megavolts.** An
electron through $5.00$ MV has picked up $5.00$ MeV, ten times its own rest
energy, so $\gamma = (0.511 + 5.00)/0.511 = 10.8$ and any classical formula is
useless.

**Particles appear or disappear.** Decay, annihilation, pair production,
fusion. Conserve $E$ and $\vec p$, never mass and never kinetic energy. Compute
the mass difference and multiply by $c^2$ — four hydrogen nuclei losing
$0.0287$ u becomes $0.0287 \times 931.5 = 26.7$ MeV.

**The words "minimum", "threshold", or "just barely enough".** That is the
invariant-mass method: set $\sqrt{s}$ before equal to the sum of product rest
energies.

**Anything asking for an angle or an energy in a different frame.** Solve it in
the frame where momentum is zero, then boost.

The habit worth building is to stop reaching for $\gamma$ and $v$ at all.
Compute in $E$ and $p$, use the triangle to convert between them, and pull out
$v/c = pc/E$ only at the very end if a problem insists on a speed. Next you will
meet the photon acting on something solid: [the photoelectric
effect](/guide/photoelectric), where the $E = pc$ you just derived stops being a
formal curiosity and starts knocking electrons out of metal.
