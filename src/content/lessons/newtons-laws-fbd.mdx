In the last lesson you were changing your mind about which frame to watch a
motion from, and the velocities all shifted around depending on who was doing
the looking. Accelerations did not. That is the hint this lesson is built on:
acceleration is the thing that every non-accelerating observer agrees about,
and forces are what produce it.

Here is the question. A crate sits on a table. The table pushes up on it, and
the crate does not move. Now you put the same crate in a lift and the lift
starts up, and a scale under the crate reads more than it did before, even
though nothing about the crate changed. Something is deciding, moment by
moment, how hard the floor pushes. What decides it? The answer is never a
formula you look up. It is a bookkeeping discipline: find every force acting
on one chosen object, add them as vectors, and set the sum equal to $ma$. The
hard part is the finding, not the adding.

## The three laws, said carefully

**First law.** An object with no net force on it keeps a constant velocity.
Not "stays still" — constant velocity, which includes staying still as the
special case where that constant is zero. The law's real content is that
motion needs no cause. Only *change* of motion needs a cause. A puck gliding
across frictionless ice needs nothing pushing it forward.

**Second law.** The net force on an object equals its mass times its
acceleration:

$$\sum \vec F = m\vec a$$

That is a vector equation, and a vector equation is really several scalar
equations at once. Pick axes and it becomes

$$\sum F_x = m a_x, \qquad \sum F_y = m a_y$$

The symbol $\sum$ means "add up all of them". $m$ is the mass in kilograms,
$a$ the acceleration in m/s², and force is then in newtons: one newton is one
kilogram-metre per second squared. Read the equation as a statement about
*cause and response*: forces are the input, acceleration is the output, and
mass is how stubbornly the object resists. Double the force, double the
acceleration. Double the mass instead, halve it.

**Third law.** If A pushes on B, then B pushes on A with the same magnitude in
the opposite direction. The two forces in such a pair always act on *different
objects*. That last clause is the whole point, and it is where most of the
confusion about this law lives.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  The crate on the table feels a downward weight $mg$ and an upward normal
  force $N$, and here $N = mg$. People call that a third law pair. It is not.
  Both of those forces act on the *crate*, so they are two entries in one free
  body diagram, and they happen to cancel because the crate is not
  accelerating. The real partner of the crate's weight is the crate's
  gravitational pull on the Earth. The real partner of $N$ is the crate
  pushing down on the table. Put the lift under the table and $N$ stops
  equalling $mg$ immediately, which would be impossible if they were a third
  law pair.
</Aside>

## The procedure

Four steps, in this order, every time.

1. **Choose one body** and draw it alone, as a box or a dot. Nothing else on
   the page — no table, no rope, no other block.
2. **Draw every force acting on it.** On it, from outside it. Never a force it
   exerts on something else, and never an internal force.
3. **Choose axes.** Usually one axis along the known direction of the
   acceleration, because that makes the other component zero.
4. **Write $\sum F_x = ma_x$ and $\sum F_y = ma_y$** and solve.

Step 2 needs a catalogue, so here is the short list of what can appear.

*Weight*, $W = mg$, always straight down, always present, magnitude $mg$ with
$g = 9.8$ m/s² near the Earth's surface. *Normal force* $N$, from any surface
in contact. The word normal here means perpendicular, not usual: it points
perpendicular to the contact surface, away from it. Its magnitude is whatever
is needed to stop the two objects passing through each other, which is
frequently not $mg$. *Tension* $T$, from any rope or cable, pulling along the
rope, away from the object. A rope can only pull. *Applied forces*, whatever
the problem says someone is pushing with. And friction, which gets its own
lesson next.

Nothing else. If you find yourself drawing an arrow labelled "the force of
motion" or "centrifugal force", stop: neither exists in this framework.

## One diagram per object

The moment there is more than one body, the discipline pays off. Two blocks of
mass $m_1 = 2.0$ kg and $m_2 = 3.0$ kg sit touching on a frictionless floor,
and you push the first with $F = 20$ N so the pair slides together.

<Figure caption={<><b>Figure 1.</b> The applied force only touches the first block. The second one is accelerated entirely by the contact force between them. Slide the masses around and watch the contact force change even though F does not.</>}>
  <Sim id="newtons-laws-fbd-pair" />
</Figure>

Because they stay in contact they have the same acceleration. Treat the pair
as one object of mass $m_1 + m_2 = 5.0$ kg. The only external horizontal force
is $F$, since the contact force between them is internal to this choice of
system and cancels out of the sum:

$$a = \frac{F}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{20\ \text{N}}{5.0\ \text{kg}} = 4.0\ \text{m/s}^2$$

Now, to get the contact force $C$, you have to break the system apart, because
$C$ was invisible while the blocks were one object. Draw the second block
alone. The only horizontal force on it is $C$, pushing forward:

$$C = m_2 a = (3.0\ \text{kg})(4.0\ \text{m/s}^2) = 12\ \text{N}$$

Check it against the first block, which feels $F$ forward and $C$ backward by
the third law: $20 - 12 = 8.0$ N, and $m_1 a = (2.0)(4.0) = 8.0$ N. It agrees.
That cross-check is worth doing while you are learning, because it catches a
sign error instantly.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Combine bodies into one system when you want the acceleration and do not
  care about the force between them. Split them apart when the force between
  them is what you were asked for. Most two-body problems are one of each,
  done in that order.
</Aside>

## Choosing axes: the ramp

A $12$ kg box rests on a ramp tilted at $25°$. What is the normal force?

The trap is answering $mg$. The box is not accelerating, true, but the normal
force is perpendicular to the ramp and the weight is vertical, and those are
no longer the same line. Tilt your axes to match the ramp: $x$ down the slope,
$y$ perpendicular to it. Then $N$ lies entirely along $y$ and only the weight
needs splitting. The weight makes an angle $\theta$ with the perpendicular —
the same $\theta$ as the ramp, because their arms are mutually perpendicular —
so its component along $y$ is $mg\cos\theta$ and along $x$ is $mg\sin\theta$.

With no acceleration perpendicular to the surface, $N - mg\cos\theta = 0$:

$$N = mg\cos\theta = (12)(9.8)\cos 25° = 117.6 \times 0.906 = 106.6\ \text{N}$$

Less than the $117.6$ N you would get on flat ground, which makes sense: the
ramp only has to support part of the weight, and the rest goes into pulling
the box down the slope. Along the slope, if nothing holds the box,
$mg\sin\theta = ma$ gives $a = g\sin\theta$, independent of mass.

</Figure>

That second case is the one worth chasing, so work it in full. A $2$ kg block
sits on a frictionless $40°$ incline, held in place by a purely *horizontal*
force. Take $g = 10$ m/s². What is the normal force?

Tilted axes are now a poor choice, because both the weight and the applied
force would need splitting. Use horizontal and vertical axes instead, and
split $N$. The normal is perpendicular to a surface tilted at $40°$, so $N$
itself is tilted $40°$ from the vertical: its vertical component is
$N\cos 40°$ and its horizontal component is $N\sin 40°$. The block is in
equilibrium, so each direction sums to zero.

Vertical: $N\cos 40° - mg = 0$, so

$$N = \frac{mg}{\cos 40°} = \frac{(2)(10)}{0.766} = 26.1\ \text{N}$$

Horizontal: $F - N \sin 40° = 0$, giving $F = 26.1 \times 0.643 = 16.8$ N,
which is also $mg\tan 40°$.

Notice that $N$ came out *larger* than $mg$, not $mg\cos\theta = 15.3$ N. The
horizontal hand is squeezing the block into the ramp. Reaching for
$mg\cos\theta$ here, out of habit, gets you a number that is wrong by 70%.

## Apparent weight

Back to the lift. A $65$ kg student stands on a bathroom scale in a lift that
accelerates upward at $1.5$ m/s². What does the scale read?

A scale reads the force it is pushed with, which by the third law equals the
normal force it pushes back with. So the question is really "what is $N$?"
Draw the student alone: weight $mg$ down, normal force $N$ up, nothing else.
Take up as positive. The acceleration is $+1.5$ m/s², so

$$N - mg = ma \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N = m(g + a)$$

$$N = 65(9.8 + 1.5) = 65 \times 11.3 = 734.5\ \text{N}$$

Against $mg = 637$ N at rest. The same formula handles downward acceleration
by making $a$ negative: at $a = -2.0$ m/s², $N = 65(9.8 - 2.0) = 65 \times 7.8
= 507$ N. And if the cable snaps, $a = -g$ and $N = 0$. That is weightlessness
— not the absence of gravity, but the absence of anything pushing back.

## Ropes, pulleys, and ropes with mass

A rope pulls along its own length, away from whatever it is attached to. If
the rope is massless, the tension is the same everywhere in it, because a
piece of rope with zero mass needs zero net force no matter how it
accelerates. An ideal pulley changes the direction of the rope without
changing the tension.

That gives the Atwood machine in three lines. A $3$ kg and a $2$ kg block hang
over a frictionless pulley, $g = 10$ m/s². Take down as positive for the heavy
block and up as positive for the light one, so a single $a$ describes both.
Heavy block: $3g - T = 3a$. Light block: $T - 2g = 2a$. Add the two equations
and $T$ disappears:

$$3g - 2g = 5a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a = \frac{(3-2)(10)}{5} = 2\ \text{m/s}^2$$

Adding the equations is the same move as treating the two blocks plus rope as
one system, where the tension is internal.

Now let the rope have mass, and "tension" stops being a single number. A $10$
kg block hangs from a uniform rope of mass $4.0$ kg, and the whole thing is
hauled straight up with acceleration $2.0$ m/s². Find the tension at the
midpoint of the rope.

Cut the rope at the midpoint, in your head, and ask what the upper half is
holding. Everything below the cut: the block, $10$ kg, plus the lower half of
the rope, $2.0$ kg. That is $12$ kg. Draw that sub-system alone. Two forces:
tension $T$ at the cut, pulling up, and its own weight, $12g$ down.

$$T - (12)(9.8) = (12)(2.0)$$
$$T = 117.6 + 24 = 141.6\ \text{N}$$

The cut is the whole technique. Any point inside any extended object can be
made into the boundary of a free body diagram, and the internal force there is
just whatever it takes to give everything on one side of the cut its share of
the acceleration. A hanging $6.0$ kg chain, one third of the way up from the
bottom, supports $2.0$ kg of chain below it at rest: $T = (2.0)(9.8) = 19.6$
N. A $5.0$ kg rope on a frictionless floor pushed with $12$ N accelerates at
$12/5.0 = 2.4$ m/s²; the internal force $1.0$ m from the pushed end is what
drives the remaining $2.0$ m, mass $(2.0/3.0)(5.0) = 3.33$ kg, so it is
$(3.33)(2.4) = 8.0$ N.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  A constraint is an extra equation you get for free from the geometry.
  Blocks in contact that stay in contact have the same acceleration. Two ends
  of an inextensible rope over a pulley move with equal speeds, so their
  accelerations are equal in magnitude. Something that "does not slip" on a
  surface has the same acceleration as the surface. Write the constraint down
  explicitly before you start solving, and count: you need as many independent
  equations as unknowns.
</Aside>

## Recognising the technique

Almost every mechanics problem that is not about energy or momentum wants this
method, so the useful question is not *whether* to draw free body diagrams but
*how many* and *around what*.

Draw one per object, always, if the problem names more than one object — two
blocks, a block and a wedge, a painter and a platform. Do not try to hold them
in your head.

Reach for the **combined system first** when the question asks for an
acceleration and the bodies clearly move together. Reach for the **split**
when the question asks for a contact force, a tension, or "the force the
platform exerts on the painter" — any force that lives at the boundary between
two objects. Those words are the signal that the boundary has to become the
edge of a diagram.

Make a **cut** when the object is extended and the question asks about a point
inside it: a rope with mass, a chain, a stack of bricks. The cut turns "inside"
into "outside".

Choose **tilted axes** when the acceleration is along a slope and the only
awkward force is gravity. Choose **horizontal and vertical axes** when the
awkward forces point in several directions at once, or when the object is in
equilibrium so neither axis is special — hanging signs on two cables, blocks
held by horizontal pushes.

And when a problem says "frictionless", it is telling you the catalogue is
short: weight, normal, tension, applied. Next comes the force that fills in
the missing entry, and the reason the word "frictionless" was ever needed:
[friction](/guide/friction).
