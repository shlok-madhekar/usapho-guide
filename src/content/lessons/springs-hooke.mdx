## The restoring force

Stretch a spring and it pulls back; compress it and it pushes back. For small
enough deformations the force is proportional to how far you moved the end from
its natural length:

$$F = -kx$$

Here $x$ is measured **from the relaxed length**, not from the floor or from
wherever the mass happens to sit, and the minus sign says the force opposes the
displacement. The constant $k$ is the stiffness in newtons per metre; a large
$k$ is a stiff spring.

Hooke's law is an approximation, good while the material stays elastic. Pull
hard enough and a real spring yields, after which it neither returns its energy
nor its original length.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Measuring $x$ from the wrong origin. In $F = -kx$, $x$ is the extension of the
  spring, not the position of the block in your coordinate system. When a block
  hangs at rest and you then displace it, it is usually cleaner to redefine the
  origin at the hanging equilibrium — see below for why that works.
</Aside>

## Series and parallel

Two springs side by side, both attached to the same block, are in **parallel**.
They share the displacement and their forces add:

$$k_{\text{par}} = k_1 + k_2$$

Two springs end to end are in **series**. Now they share the force — the same
tension runs through the chain — and the extensions add:

$$\frac{1}{k_{\text{ser}}} = \frac{1}{k_1} + \frac{1}{k_2}$$

Series is always softer than either spring alone. The reason is that the same
pull stretches both, so you collect two extensions for the price of one force.
Cutting a spring in half doubles its stiffness, which is the same fact read
backwards: the original was two half-springs in series.

Note the arithmetic is opposite to resistors, so do not pattern-match. Decide
by asking which quantity is shared: shared displacement means add the $k$'s,
shared force means add the compliances $1/k$.

## A hanging spring

Hang a mass $m$ from a vertical spring. At rest, the spring force balances
gravity:

$$k x_0 = mg \;\Longrightarrow\; x_0 = \frac{mg}{k}$$

Now displace the mass a further distance $y$ below that point. The spring force
is $k(x_0 + y)$ upward, gravity is $mg$ downward, and the net force is

$$k(x_0 + y) - mg = ky$$

Gravity has cancelled completely. Measured from the hanging equilibrium, a
vertical spring behaves exactly like a horizontal one — same restoring law,
same $k$. Constant gravity shifts the equilibrium and changes nothing else,
which is why you can drop $mg$ from these problems the moment you put your
origin in the right place.
