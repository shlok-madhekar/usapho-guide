## Momentum that goes around

A rigid body spinning at $\omega$ carries angular momentum

$$L = I\omega$$

but that formula is a special case. The general definition works for anything,
spinning or not: a particle of momentum $\vec{p}$ at position $\vec{r}$ from a
chosen origin has

$$\vec{L} = \vec{r} \times \vec{p}, \qquad L = mvr_\perp$$

where $r_\perp$ is the perpendicular distance from the origin to the line of
motion. A car driving in a straight line past you has angular momentum about
you — constant, nonzero, and often exactly the quantity a problem needs.

## When it is conserved

Torque changes angular momentum the way force changes momentum:

$$\vec{\tau}_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\vec{L}}{dt}$$

So with no net external torque, $L$ is fixed. Since $L = I\omega$ and $I$ can
change while $L$ cannot, a skater pulling her arms in must speed up. Her kinetic
energy $\tfrac12 I\omega^2 = L^2/2I$ *rises* as $I$ falls — she does work
against the outward pull of her own arms. Angular momentum conservation does not
imply energy conservation, and in a rotational collision it usually rules it out.

The same argument runs a collapsing gas cloud up to a pulsar spinning hundreds
of times a second.

## Pick the point first

Angular momentum is only defined relative to an origin, and it is conserved
about some points and not others in the very same problem. Choose deliberately.

The rule: pick the point through which the forces you cannot evaluate pass. A
bullet embeds in a door hinged at one edge. The hinge exerts a large, unknown
impulsive force during the impact, so linear momentum is *not* conserved — but
that force acts at the hinge, contributing zero torque about it. Take $L$ about
the hinge and the unknown disappears:

$$m v r_\perp = \left(I_{\text{door}} + m r^2\right)\omega$$

One equation, one unknown. Every "object strikes a pivoted rod" problem is this
problem.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Do not switch origins partway through. Compute $L$ before and after about the
  *same* point, and include every mass, moving object included. The bullet's
  contribution to $I$ after impact is $mr^2$; leaving it out is the standard
  slip.
</Aside>
