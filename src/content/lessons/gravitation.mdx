## The inverse square law

Any two point masses attract each other along the line joining them:

$$F = \frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}, \qquad G = 6.674\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/kg^2}$$

Here $r$ is the distance between the centres, not between the surfaces. That
matters because of a result Newton had to invent calculus to prove: a uniform
spherical shell attracts an outside body exactly as if all its mass sat at the
centre. So a solid sphere — a planet, a star — can be replaced by a point at its
centre for anything outside it. Inside a shell, the pull is exactly zero.

Near the surface of a planet of mass $M$ and radius $R$, this reproduces the
familiar constant:

$$g = \frac{GM}{R^2}$$

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Saying an astronaut in orbit is weightless because gravity is absent. At
  400 km up, $g$ is about 89% of its surface value. What is absent is the
  normal force: the station and everything in it are in free fall together,
  accelerating toward Earth at the same rate. Weightlessness is a statement
  about the FBD of a body in free fall, not about gravity switching off.
</Aside>

## Circular orbits

A satellite in a circular orbit of radius $r$ has gravity supplying exactly the
centripetal force it needs:

$$\frac{GMm}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r} \;\Longrightarrow\; v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$$

The satellite's own mass cancels — a bolt and a space station in the same orbit
move identically. The period follows from $v = 2\pi r / T$:

$$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}\,r^3$$

Two consequences worth internalizing. Higher orbits are *slower*, since
$v \propto r^{-1/2}$. And to catch up with something ahead of you in the same
orbit, you must fire your engine to drop lower, speed up, and come back — the
counterintuitive fact at the heart of orbital rendezvous.

## Energy and binding

Take the potential energy to be zero at infinite separation. Then

$$U = -\frac{GMm}{r}$$

is negative everywhere: you must do positive work to pull the two apart. To
escape, a body launched from radius $r$ needs enough kinetic energy to reach
infinity with nothing to spare, so $\tfrac12 mv^2 = GMm/r$:

$$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}} = \sqrt{2}\; v_{\text{orb}}$$

Escape speed is just $\sqrt{2}$ times circular orbital speed at the same
radius, and it does not depend on the launch direction — only on the speed —
since energy is a scalar.

For a circular orbit, $\tfrac12 mv^2 = GMm/2r$, so

$$E = K + U = \frac{GMm}{2r} - \frac{GMm}{r} = -\frac{GMm}{2r}$$

The total energy is negative, which is what "bound" means. It also equals
$-K$, and equals $U/2$. Drag lowers $E$ by making $r$ smaller, and the kinetic
energy *rises* as the orbit decays: friction speeds a satellite up.
