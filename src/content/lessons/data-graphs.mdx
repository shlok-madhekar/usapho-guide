## Make the graph a straight line

A straight line is the only shape the eye judges reliably. You can see an
outlier on a line, and you can see curvature that should not be there. So the
first move with any dataset is to plot it in whatever variables make the
expected relation linear.

A pendulum's period obeys $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$. Plotting $T$ against $L$ gives
a curve that many different laws could fit. Plot $T^2$ against $L$ instead and
the prediction becomes a line through the origin with slope $4\pi^2/g$ — so
the slope measures $g$, and the fact that the intercept comes out near zero is
a check that the model is right.

The general move: rearrange the expected relation into the form
$y = mx + b$, where $x$ and $y$ are things you can compute from the data.
Then the slope and intercept carry the physics.

For a power law $y = Ax^n$ with $n$ unknown, take logarithms:

$$\ln y = \ln A + n \ln x$$

A log-log plot is a straight line whose slope is the exponent and whose
intercept is $\ln A$. For exponentials $y = A e^{-t/\tau}$, plot $\ln y$
against $t$: the slope is $-1/\tau$. Deciding which of the two to use is
itself a result — a power law is straight on log-log, an exponential on
semi-log.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Always ask what the slope and the intercept mean physically before you fit.
  A nonzero intercept where theory predicts zero is not noise to be ignored;
  it usually points at a systematic effect, like a timer offset or an unmeasured
  extra length.
</Aside>

## Uncertainty

Every measurement has a spread. Random error scatters points on both sides of
the true line and shrinks as $1/\sqrt{N}$ when you average $N$ repeats.
Systematic error shifts every point the same way and does not shrink with
repetition — more data will never fix a miscalibrated ruler.

When quantities combine, so do their uncertainties. For products and
quotients, relative uncertainties add in quadrature:

$$\frac{\delta z}{z} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\delta x}{x}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\delta y}{y}\right)^2}$$

For sums and differences, the absolute uncertainties add in quadrature
instead. And a power multiplies the relative uncertainty by the exponent: if
$z = x^3$, then $\delta z/z = 3\,\delta x/x$.

That last rule tells you where to spend effort. In $g = 4\pi^2 L/T^2$, an
error in $T$ counts double. Measure the period carefully — time twenty swings
and divide — before worrying about the ruler.

Quote results as value plus uncertainty, with the uncertainty to one
significant figure and the value rounded to match: $9.7 \pm 0.2\ \text{m/s}^2$,
never $9.6873 \pm 0.2$.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Forcing a fit through the origin because theory says the intercept is zero
  hides exactly the information you want. Fit both parameters, then check
  whether the intercept is consistent with zero given its own uncertainty. If
  it is not, the model is missing something, and that is the finding.
</Aside>
