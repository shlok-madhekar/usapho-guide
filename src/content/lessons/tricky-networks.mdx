## When series-parallel reduction fails

Some networks contain no pair of resistors that is cleanly in series or cleanly
in parallel. The five-resistor bridge is the smallest example. Reduction stalls
immediately, and the three techniques below are what replace it.

## The Wheatstone bridge

Four resistors form two branches from the source, $R_1$ then $R_2$ down one
side and $R_3$ then $R_4$ down the other, with a fifth resistor bridging the
two midpoints. The bridge resistor carries current only if its two ends sit at
different potentials. Each side is a voltage divider, so the midpoints match
when the ratios match:

$$\frac{R_1}{R_2} = \frac{R_3}{R_4}$$

At balance the bridge element is irrelevant. Delete it — or short it, whichever
you prefer — and the remaining four resistors reduce by inspection. Both moves
give the same answer precisely because no current flows and no voltage stands
across it.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  The balance condition is a ratio, not a difference. $R_1 = R_4$ and
  $R_2 = R_3$ does not balance a bridge unless the ratios also match. And the
  condition does not depend on the bridge resistor or the source voltage at
  all, so checking balance is always the first thing to do: it costs one
  comparison and can collapse the whole problem.
</Aside>

## Infinite ladders

A ladder that repeats forever is unchanged by removing one stage. If $R_\infty$
is the resistance of the whole ladder, then one series resistor $R_1$ followed
by a shunt $R_2$ in parallel with *another copy of the whole ladder* must equal
$R_\infty$:

$$R_\infty = R_1 + \frac{R_2 R_\infty}{R_2 + R_\infty}$$

That is a quadratic. Solve it and discard the negative root, since a passive
network cannot have negative resistance. The whole method rests on
self-similarity, so it fails the moment the stages differ — a ladder whose
resistors halve each stage needs a genuine series, not this trick.

## Symmetry and equipotential nodes

If a symmetry of the network maps one node onto another while leaving the input
and output terminals fixed, those two nodes are at equal potential. You may
then merge them into one node or cut any wire between them. Both operations
change nothing measurable, and one of them usually turns the network into
something series-parallel reduction can finish.

For a bridge fed symmetrically, the two midpoints are such a pair. For a
resistor cube entered at a corner, the three neighbours of that corner form
one equipotential set and the three next ones form another.
