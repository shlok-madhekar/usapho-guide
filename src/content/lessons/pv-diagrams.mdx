## Reading the four curves

A PV diagram plots the state of the gas, so every point on it is a legitimate
equilibrium state with its own temperature, fixed by $T = PV/nR$. Curves of
constant temperature are hyperbolas; a point further from the origin is hotter.
That one fact lets you read $\Delta U$ off the picture without computing
anything, since $\Delta U = nC_V\Delta T$.

The four standard legs look like this:

- **Isochoric** — vertical line. $W = 0$.
- **Isobaric** — horizontal line. $W = P\Delta V$, a rectangle.
- **Isothermal** — hyperbola, $PV$ constant. Stays on one temperature curve.
- **Adiabatic** — $PV^\gamma$ constant, so it is *steeper* than the isotherm
  through the same point and crosses to a different temperature.

Steepness is the tell. If two curves leave the same point and one falls off
faster, that one is the adiabat, and $\gamma > 1$ is the reason.

## Work is the area, sign is the direction

$W = \int P\,dV$ is the area under the curve, measured down to the $V$ axis.
Left-to-right motion means expansion and positive work; right-to-left means the
same area counted negative. For a closed cycle the two contributions do not
cancel, and what survives is the area enclosed by the loop:

$$W_{net} = \oint P\,dV = \text{enclosed area}$$

Clockwise gives positive net work — the gas expands at high pressure and is
pushed back at low pressure, which is an engine. Counterclockwise is negative:
work goes in, and the device is a refrigerator or heat pump.

Around any complete cycle the gas returns to its starting state, so
$\Delta U = 0$ and therefore $Q_{net} = W_{net}$.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Reaching for $W = P\Delta V$ on a leg where $P$ is not constant. That form is
  only the isobaric case. On an isotherm the work is $nRT\ln(V_f/V_i)$; on an
  adiabat use $W = -\Delta U = -nC_V\Delta T$, which is far easier than
  integrating $PV^\gamma$ directly.
</Aside>

## Working a cycle

Build a table, one row per leg, with columns $Q$, $W$ and $\Delta U$. Fill in
whichever two of the three the process type hands you free, then get the third
from $\Delta U = Q - W$.

Take a three-leg cycle: isobaric expansion from $V$ to $2V$ at pressure $P$,
then isochoric cooling back to $P/2$, then isothermal compression to close the
loop. Leg one gives $W = PV$ and, from $T$ doubling, $\Delta U = \tfrac{f}{2}PV$.
Leg two gives $W = 0$ with all the heat leaving. Leg three, at fixed
temperature, gives $\Delta U = 0$ and $W = Q = -nRT\ln 2 = -PV\ln 2$.

Sum the work column for $W_{net}$, sum only the positive entries in the heat
column for $Q_{in}$, and check that the $\Delta U$ column sums to zero. That
check catches sign errors before they propagate into an efficiency.
