A car is doing 25 m/s and the driver stands on the brakes. Before the car
stops it travels a certain distance. Double the speed to 50 m/s, brake just as
hard, and the car does not travel twice as far. It travels four times as far.
Nothing in that sentence is about forces or engines or tyres. It follows from
the bookkeeping of position, velocity and time alone.

That bookkeeping is what this lesson is. In the [previous lesson](/guide/data-graphs)
you were reading slopes and areas off graphs and worrying about how much you
could trust a number. Now you get the small set of relations that govern any
object moving along a line, and the habits that turn a wordy problem into two
equations you can actually solve.

## Position, velocity, acceleration

Pick a line to move along and an origin on it. The **position** $x$ is how far
the object is from the origin, with a sign: negative means the other side. It
is measured in metres.

**Velocity** is the rate at which position changes. If the object moves from
$x_1$ at time $t_1$ to $x_2$ at time $t_2$, the average velocity over that
stretch is

$$\bar v = \frac{x_2 - x_1}{t_2 - t_1} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}.$$

Shrink the interval until it is vanishingly small and you get the velocity at
one instant, which is written $v = dx/dt$. That is the derivative of $x$ with
respect to $t$, and if you have not met derivatives, here is all you need for
now: the derivative of $t^n$ is $nt^{n-1}$, and the derivative of a sum is the
sum of the derivatives. So if $x(t) = 3 + 4t - t^2$ in metres, then
$v(t) = 4 - 2t$ in m/s, and at $t = 3.0$ s that is $4 - 6 = -2.0$ m/s. Negative:
the cart is heading back toward the origin.

**Acceleration** is the same operation applied once more. It is the rate at
which velocity changes, $a = dv/dt$, in metres per second per second, m/s².
Note what it is not. Acceleration is not "how fast you are going" and it is not
"speeding up". A car braking from 30 m/s has a negative acceleration while its
velocity is still large and positive. A car reversing and speeding up has
negative velocity and negative acceleration. The two signs are independent, and
the only rule connecting them is this: when $v$ and $a$ have the same sign the
speed grows, and when they have opposite signs the speed shrinks.

<Aside label="Speed is not velocity">
  Velocity is signed; speed is its magnitude, $|v|$. Displacement $\Delta x$ is
  signed; distance travelled is the total path length, which never decreases.
  A ball thrown up and caught again has zero displacement and a very nonzero
  distance. Problems say "distance" or "displacement" deliberately.
</Aside>

## The constant-acceleration equations, derived

Most problems hold $a$ fixed. That single assumption gives you everything.

Constant $a$ means velocity changes by the same amount every second, so after a
time $t$ it has changed by $at$:

$$v = v_0 + at.$$

Here $v_0$ is the velocity at the moment you start your clock. On a
velocity-time graph this is a straight line with slope $a$ and intercept $v_0$.

Now for position. The area under a velocity-time graph is the displacement,
because a thin strip of width $dt$ and height $v$ has area $v\,dt$, which is the
small distance covered in that sliver of time. Add up the strips and you have
$\Delta x$. Under a straight line the region is a trapezoid: two parallel sides
of length $v_0$ and $v$, separated by a base of length $t$. Its area is the
average of the parallel sides times the base, so

$$\Delta x = \frac{v_0 + v}{2}\,t.$$

That is worth a name. The bracket is the **average velocity**, and for constant
acceleration — and only for constant acceleration — it equals the plain average
of the starting and ending velocities.

Substitute $v = v_0 + at$ into it and the trapezoid splits into a rectangle plus
a triangle:

$$\Delta x = \frac{v_0 + v_0 + at}{2}\,t = v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}a t^2.$$

Finally, take $t = (v - v_0)/a$ from the first equation and put it into the
trapezoid form:

$$\Delta x = \frac{v_0 + v}{2}\cdot\frac{v - v_0}{a} = \frac{v^2 - v_0^2}{2a},$$

which rearranges to

$$v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\,\Delta x.$$

That last one has no $t$ in it. Whenever a problem hands you two speeds and a
distance and never mentions seconds, it is asking for that equation. It is also
the answer to the puzzle at the top: stopping from $v_0$ to rest takes
$\Delta x = v_0^2/(2|a|)$, which goes as the square of the speed.

**Worked example 1.** A cyclist is moving at 4.0 m/s and accelerates uniformly
at 1.2 m/s² over the next 60 m. What is her final speed?

Write down what you have: $v_0 = 4.0$ m/s, $a = 1.2$ m/s², $\Delta x = 60$ m,
and $v$ is wanted. No time appears anywhere, so reach for the timeless
equation.

$$v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta x = (4.0)^2 + 2(1.2)(60) = 16 + 144 = 160\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}.$$

So $v = \sqrt{160} = 12.6$ m/s. Taking the positive root is a choice: she was
moving forward and speeding up, so the negative root is not physical here.

## Averages, and the trap in them

Average velocity is always $\Delta x/\Delta t$ — total displacement over total
time. Average speed is total distance over total time. Neither is the average
of the speeds you held, unless you held each one for the same *time*.

Say you drive the first half of a trip's distance at 40 mph and the second half
at 60 mph. Call the total distance $2d$. The first leg takes $d/40$ hours, the
second $d/60$. The total time is

$$\frac{d}{40} + \frac{d}{60} = d\left(\frac{3}{120} + \frac{2}{120}\right) = \frac{5d}{120} = \frac{d}{24}.$$

Average speed is total distance over total time: $2d \div (d/24) = 48$ mph. Not
50. You spend longer on the slow half, so the slow half gets more weight. Same
arithmetic, different numbers: 40 km at 80 km/h then 40 km at 40 km/h takes
$0.5 + 1.0 = 1.5$ h for 80 km, giving 53.3 km/h.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Averaging the speeds is the single most common error in this topic. Equal
  distances at different speeds never give the plain average — the answer is
  always lower. Equal *times* at different speeds do give the plain average.
  Read which one the problem specifies before you write anything down.
</Aside>

## Free fall and the moving platform

Near the ground, ignoring air resistance, everything falls with the same
downward acceleration $g$. Use $g = 9.8$ m/s², or $10$ m/s² when a problem says
so. Mass does not appear. A bowling ball and a marble released together land
together.

The acceleration is almost never the hard part. The initial condition is. An
object released from a moving platform leaves with the platform's velocity — it
does not start at rest just because you let go of it.

**Worked example 2.** A balloon rises at a steady 5 m/s. At a height of 60 m a
sandbag is released. With $g = 10$ m/s², how long until it hits the ground?

Set up axes first, before touching a number: let $y$ be measured upward from
the ground, so $y_0 = +60$ m, the bag's initial velocity is $v_0 = +5$ m/s (it
was moving up with the balloon), and $a = -10$ m/s².

$$y(t) = 60 + 5t - 5t^2.$$

The bag lands when $y = 0$:

$$5t^2 - 5t - 60 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t^2 - t - 12 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (t-4)(t+3) = 0.$$

So $t = 4.0$ s, discarding the negative root, which describes a time before the
bag existed. Notice that the bag first goes *up*: its velocity is zero at
$t = v_0/g = 0.5$ s, at a height of $60 + 5(0.5) - 5(0.25) = 61.25$ m. If you
had set $v_0 = 0$ you would have got $t = \sqrt{12} = 3.46$ s, too small.

## Motion in stages

Real problems rarely hold one acceleration for the whole trip. The method is
always the same: break the motion at every instant where $a$ changes, solve each
stage separately, and pass the final velocity and position of one stage in as
the initial conditions of the next. Draw the velocity-time graph while you do
it; the shape usually tells you the answer.

A rocket sled starting from rest at 8.0 m/s² for 10 s reaches
$v = 80$ m/s having covered $\tfrac12(8)(10)^2 = 400$ m. Stage two starts at
80 m/s and brakes at 4.0 m/s², needing $80/4 = 20$ s and covering
$80^2/(2\cdot 4) = 800$ m. Total: 1200 m. Each stage is a fresh set of initial
conditions.

Sometimes the graph shape alone is enough. A tram accelerates uniformly from
rest, then decelerates uniformly to rest, 200 m in 20 s. Its velocity-time graph
is a triangle of base 20 s and peak $v_{\max}$, so the area is
$\tfrac12(20)v_{\max} = 200$, giving $v_{\max} = 20$ m/s. You never need to know
where the switch happened.

**Worked example 3.** A shuttle must cover 1000 m, starting and ending at rest.
It can accelerate at most 2.0 m/s² and brake at most 4.0 m/s², with no speed
limit. What is the minimum travel time?

Think about what the fastest possible trip looks like. Any moment spent not
accelerating as hard as allowed is a moment you could have been going faster, so
the optimal run is: full acceleration up to some peak speed $v$, then
immediately full braking down to rest. The velocity-time graph is a triangle
with peak $v$.

The accelerating leg covers $v^2/(2\cdot 2.0) = v^2/4$ and takes $v/2.0$. The
braking leg covers $v^2/(2\cdot 4.0) = v^2/8$ and takes $v/4.0$. The distances
must add to 1000 m:

$$\frac{v^2}{4} + \frac{v^2}{8} = \frac{3v^2}{8} = 1000 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v^2 = \frac{8000}{3} = 2666.7,$$

so $v = 51.64$ m/s. The total time is

$$t = \frac{v}{2.0} + \frac{v}{4.0} = 0.75v = 0.75(51.64) = 38.7\ \mathrm{s}.$$

If the problem adds a speed limit, the triangle gets its top sliced off into a
trapezoid: accelerate to the limit, cruise, then brake, and the cruise distance
is whatever is left over after the two ramps.

<Aside label="Two objects, one clock" warn>
  When two objects are involved — a police car chasing a speeder, one ball
  thrown after another — write a position function for each using the *same*
  origin and the *same* $t = 0$, then set them equal. Absorb any head start into
  the formula rather than into your head. A speeder at 30 m/s with a 5.0 s lead
  is at $x_1 = 30(t+5)$ when the cruiser, starting from rest at 4.0 m/s², is at
  $x_2 = 2t^2$; setting them equal gives $t^2 - 15t - 75 = 0$ and $t = 19.0$ s
  after the cruiser starts. Mixing up the two clocks is the usual failure.
</Aside>

## When the acceleration is not constant

The four boxed equations are worthless the moment $a$ changes with time. The
definitions still hold, though, and they are all you need.

If you are given $v(t)$ directly, displacement is the integral — the signed area
under the curve — and distance is the area with every piece counted positive.
Take a cart with $v(t) = 12 - 3t$ m/s over the first 8.0 s. It reverses at
$t = 4.0$ s. Forward leg: a triangle of base 4 and height 12, area
$\tfrac12(4)(12) = 24$ m. Backward leg: from 4 s to 8 s the velocity runs from 0
to $-12$ m/s, another triangle of area 24 m, but travelled backwards. The
displacement is $24 - 24 = 0$. The distance is $24 + 24 = 48$ m. Whenever $v$
changes sign inside the interval, split the integral there or you will report
displacement when the problem asked for distance.

A harder case is acceleration given as a function of velocity. A boat's engine
cuts out at 20 m/s and afterwards $a = -kv$ with $k = 0.50$ s⁻¹. How far does it
coast? Solving $dv/dt = -kv$ needs an exponential, but there is a change of
variable that avoids it. Write acceleration using the chain rule:

$$a = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{dv}{dx}\cdot\frac{dx}{dt} = v\frac{dv}{dx}.$$

That identity is worth memorising; it converts any velocity-dependent
acceleration into a relation between $v$ and $x$. Here it gives
$v\,dv/dx = -kv$, and cancelling the $v$ leaves $dv/dx = -k$: velocity falls off
*linearly with distance*, even though it decays exponentially with time. So
$\Delta v = -k\,\Delta x$, and going from 20 m/s to 0:

$$\Delta x = \frac{20}{0.50} = 40\ \mathrm{m}.$$

The boat takes forever to stop but only ever travels 40 m.

## Recognising what a problem wants

Before you write any equation, list the five constant-acceleration symbols and
mark which you know: $v_0$, $v$, $a$, $t$, $\Delta x$. Three knowns pick the
equation for you.

- **No time mentioned, and none wanted** — use $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta x$.
  "Stopping distance", "final speed over this stretch", "how high does it go".
- **Time is given or asked for, and one velocity is missing** — use
  $\Delta x = v_0t + \tfrac12 at^2$ or, more cheaply, the trapezoid form.
- **Total distance with no way to find the individual stages** — look for the
  area of the velocity-time graph as a whole shape. Triangle and trapezoid areas
  solve a surprising number of problems in one line.
- **The word "maximum" or "minimum" with two acceleration limits** — the answer
  is the bang-bang trip: full acceleration, then full braking, possibly with a
  cruise if there is a speed limit.
- **Two moving objects, or a head start, or a delay** — one origin, one clock,
  two position functions, set equal.
- **Acceleration given as a formula in $t$ or $v$** — the equations do not
  apply. Integrate, or use $a = v\,dv/dx$ if $a$ depends on $v$.
- **"Distance" rather than "displacement", or anything that turns around** —
  find where $v = 0$ and split the calculation there.

Almost every one of these is easier if you sketch the velocity-time graph first.
That is the subject of the next lesson, [Motion Graphs](/guide/motion-graphs),
where slopes and areas stop being a computational trick and become the fastest
way to read a problem.
