## Two laws, two grammars

Friction acts along the contact surface, opposing relative sliding. It comes in
two kinds, and the difference between them is the whole topic:

$$f_s \le \mu_s N, \qquad f_k = \mu_k N$$

Kinetic friction is a number. Once a block is sliding, friction has the
magnitude $\mu_k N$ and points opposite the velocity — nothing to solve for.

Static friction is a budget. It supplies exactly whatever force is needed to
keep the surfaces from sliding, and $\mu_s N$ is only the largest it can
supply. Push a crate with 20 N and it does not move: static friction is 20 N,
not $\mu_s N$. Only at the instant of slipping does the inequality become an
equality, and that is the one moment you may write $f_s = \mu_s N$.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Setting $f_s = \mu_s N$ for an object in ordinary equilibrium. That equation
  is a statement about the threshold of slipping, not about every static
  contact. If the problem does not say "maximum", "minimum", "just barely", or
  "on the verge", treat $f_s$ as an unknown and let the equations determine it.
</Aside>

## Angle of repose

Tilt a plank with a block on it. Along the slope, $mg\sin\theta$ pulls down;
across it, $N = mg\cos\theta$. Slipping starts when the demand hits the ceiling:

$$mg\sin\theta = \mu_s\, mg\cos\theta \;\Longrightarrow\; \tan\theta_{\max} = \mu_s$$

The mass cancels. A heavy block and a light one of the same material slip at
the same angle.

That angle has a geometric meaning. The contact force — normal plus friction,
added as vectors — can tilt away from the surface normal by at most
$\arctan\mu_s$. Sweep that limit around and you get the **friction cone**: any
applied force whose line lies inside the cone can be balanced without sliding,
and anything outside cannot, no matter how hard you push. This is why pressing
a crate harder at a steep downward angle never helps — you add to $N$ and to
the driving component in the same ratio.

## Which surface slips first

Stack two blocks and drag the bottom one, or push a pair of blocks resting on
a table. Each contact has its own $N$ and its own $\mu_s$, so each has its own
budget. The system stays rigid only while *every* contact stays within budget.

The procedure: assume nothing slips, solve for the common acceleration, then
back out the friction required at each surface. Compare each required value to
its own $\mu_s N$. The contact that exceeds its ceiling first is the one that
gives way — and often it is not the one carrying the largest friction force,
because the ceilings differ too. If a contact fails, redo the problem with
$f_k = \mu_k N$ there and independent accelerations for the two bodies.
