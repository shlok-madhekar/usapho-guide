## A rower who cannot win

A river is 150 m wide and flows at 3.0 m/s. A rower can only manage 2.0 m/s
relative to the water. She is slower than the river, so no matter which way she
points the bow, she is going downstream. The question is not whether she drifts.
It is how little she can drift if she chooses her heading well.

You cannot answer that by thinking about speeds alone. Speed is one number, and
this problem has two independent directions in it: across the river and along
it. The whole trick is to stop treating the motion as one thing and start
treating it as two numbers that evolve separately. That is what a vector is —
not an arrow you draw, but a small bundle of independent numbers, one per
direction. The previous lesson had you [checking that the units of an
expression made sense](/guide/units-estimation) before trusting it. This one
gives you the other habit that runs under every later unit: when a quantity has
a direction, break it into pieces along axes you chose on purpose.

## Splitting a vector into components

Put down a pair of perpendicular axes, $x$ and $y$. Draw your vector $\vec A$
starting at the origin, at an angle $\theta$ measured counterclockwise from the
$+x$ axis. Now drop a perpendicular line from the tip of $\vec A$ down to the
$x$ axis. You have made a right triangle whose hypotenuse is $\vec A$, whose
horizontal leg runs along $x$, and whose vertical leg runs along $y$.

That triangle is the whole story. The hypotenuse has length $A$ (the
*magnitude*, a plain positive number with units). Right-triangle trigonometry
says the side adjacent to $\theta$ is $A\cos\theta$ and the side opposite is
$A\sin\theta$. So

$$A_x = A\cos\theta, \qquad A_y = A\sin\theta.$$

$A_x$ is the *$x$-component*: how far the vector reaches in the $x$ direction.
It can be negative, and a negative component just means the vector reaches
backwards along that axis. Going the other way, the two components rebuild the
magnitude and the angle by Pythagoras:

$$A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{A_y}{A_x}.$$

Which function goes with which leg depends on where the angle is measured from,
not on some rule that sine is always vertical. If a problem gives you the angle
from the *vertical*, then the vertical piece is the adjacent one and gets the
cosine. Draw the triangle every single time and read it off. That habit costs
five seconds and removes an entire category of mistake.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  A vector equation is shorthand for one ordinary equation per axis. When you
  write $\vec F_{\text{net}} = m\vec a$ in two dimensions you have written two
  separate scalar equations, and you are allowed to solve them one at a time.
</Aside>

**Worked example 1.** A child pulls a sled with a rope at $35^\circ$ above the
horizontal. The tension is $240$ N. How big is the horizontal pull?

The angle is measured from the horizontal, so the horizontal leg is adjacent,
and it gets the cosine:

$$F_x = (240\ \text{N})\cos 35^\circ = (240)(0.8192) = 197\ \text{N}.$$

The vertical piece comes along free: $F_y = (240)(\sin 35^\circ) = (240)(0.5736)
= 138$ N, upward. That vertical piece is doing nothing to move the sled forward,
but it is lifting some weight off the ground, which is exactly why pulling at an
angle reduces friction. One vector, two jobs, and you only see both once you
split it.

## Adding vectors means adding components

Two vectors add tip to tail: slide the second one so its tail sits at the first
one's tip, and the sum runs from the first tail to the last tip. Geometrically
that is a triangle. In components it is even simpler, because the $x$ journeys
and the $y$ journeys never interfere:

$$\vec A + \vec B = (A_x + B_x,\; A_y + B_y).$$

A hiker who walks 4.0 km east then 3.0 km north has displacement $(4.0, 3.0)$
km, and the net distance from the start is $\sqrt{4.0^2 + 3.0^2} = 5.0$ km — not
7.0 km, because the two legs were never pointing the same way. Magnitudes do not
add. Components do.

Subtraction works the same way with a sign flip, and this is where people slip.
A car going 12 m/s east turns and ends up going 12 m/s north at the same speed.
Its change in velocity is $\Delta\vec v = \vec v_f - \vec v_i = (0, 12) - (12,
0) = (-12, 12)$ m/s, whose magnitude is $12\sqrt2 = 17$ m/s. The speed never
changed, and yet the velocity changed by a lot. That difference is the seed of
everything in circular motion.

Relative velocity is just this addition applied to two observers. The rule is
that velocities add through the middle man: your velocity relative to the ground
equals your velocity relative to the water plus the water's velocity relative to
the ground. For the rower, with $x$ downstream and $y$ across:

$$\vec v_{\text{ground}} = \vec v_{\text{water}} + \vec v_{\text{current}}.$$

Here is the key structural fact. The across-river component decides *how long*
the crossing takes, and the downstream component decides *how far* she drifts
during that time. They are independent. If a swimmer moving at 1.6 m/s points
straight across a 40 m river, her crossing time is set by the 1.6 alone, $t =
40/1.6 = 25$ s, and the 1.2 m/s current carries her $(1.2)(25) = 30$ m
downstream. The current never slows the crossing.

**Worked example 2.** The rower from the opening: $v_b = 2.0$ m/s, current $v_c
= 3.0$ m/s, width $L = 150$ m. Point the bow at an angle $\theta$ upstream of
straight across. What is the smallest possible drift?

Write the two components of her ground velocity. Across: $v_y = v_b\cos\theta$.
Downstream: $v_x = v_c - v_b\sin\theta$. The crossing time is $t =
L/(v_b\cos\theta)$, so the drift is

$$d = v_x t = \frac{L\,(v_c - v_b\sin\theta)}{v_b\cos\theta}.$$

Minimise it by setting $dd/d\theta = 0$. Differentiating the quotient and
clearing the common $\cos^2\theta$ gives $-v_b\cos^2\theta + (v_c -
v_b\sin\theta)\sin\theta = 0$, which expands to $-v_b\cos^2\theta - v_b\sin^2
\theta + v_c\sin\theta = 0$, and since $\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$,

$$\sin\theta = \frac{v_b}{v_c} = \frac{2.0}{3.0} = 0.6667.$$

So $\theta = 41.8^\circ$ and $\cos\theta = \sqrt{1 - 0.4444} = 0.7454$. Now put
the numbers in. Across-speed $v_y = (2.0)(0.7454) = 1.491$ m/s, so $t = 150/1.491
= 100.6$ s. Downstream speed $v_x = 3.0 - (2.0)(0.6667) = 3.0 - 1.333 = 1.667$
m/s. Drift $d = (1.667)(100.6) = 168$ m.

Notice what made this tractable: never once did you think about the resultant
arrow. You wrote two components, built the time out of one and the distance out
of the other, and then did ordinary one-variable calculus.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Do not set up the crossing by pointing the *resultant* straight across unless
  the problem says she lands directly opposite. Heading and course are different
  vectors. "She aims across" fixes $\vec v_{\text{water}}$; "she lands opposite"
  fixes $\vec v_{\text{ground}}$. Read which one the problem pins down, and note
  that when $v_c > v_b$ the second is impossible.
</Aside>

## The dot product: how much of this lies along that

Often you do not want a whole vector, only the part of it pointing some
particular way. The horizontal pull on the sled was exactly that: the part of
$\vec F$ along the direction of motion. Generalise it. Take any two vectors
$\vec A$ and $\vec B$ with angle $\theta$ between them. The length of $\vec A$'s
shadow on the line of $\vec B$ is $A\cos\theta$. Multiply that shadow by the
length of $\vec B$ and you have defined the *dot product*:

$$\vec A\cdot\vec B = AB\cos\theta.$$

It is a plain number, not a vector. It is positive when the two roughly agree in
direction, zero at exactly $90^\circ$, and negative when they oppose. In
components it is even easier to compute, because each axis contributes
separately:

$$\vec A\cdot\vec B = A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z.$$

Having both forms is the point. The component form gets you a number from raw
coordinates; the $AB\cos\theta$ form turns that number into an angle. Set them
equal and you can find the angle between any two vectors:
$\cos\theta = (\vec A\cdot\vec B)/(AB)$.

Three things it buys you. Work: $W = \vec F\cdot\vec d$, since only the part of
the force along the displacement does anything. For $\vec F = (12, -5)$ N and
$\vec d = (3, 4)$ m, $W = (12)(3) + (-5)(4) = 36 - 20 = 16$ J. A
perpendicularity test: $\vec A\cdot\vec B = 0$ with neither vector zero means
they are at right angles, which is how you find the $k$ that makes $(3, k, -2)$
perpendicular to $(4, 5, 1)$ — set $12 + 5k - 2 = 0$, so $k = -2$. And
projection: the component of $\vec F$ along a direction is $\vec F\cdot\hat u$,
where $\hat u$ is a *unit vector*, meaning a vector of length exactly 1 pointing
that way. You build one by dividing any vector by its own magnitude: $\hat u =
\vec d/|\vec d|$.

**Worked example 3.** A bead is threaded on a straight rod pointing along $\vec
d = (1, 2, 2)$. A force $\vec F = (3, 4, 0)$ N acts on the bead. How large is the
part of $\vec F$ perpendicular to the rod?

The direct route — finding the perpendicular piece geometrically — is painful in
three dimensions. The cheap route is to find the *parallel* piece with a dot
product and let Pythagoras give you the rest, since the parallel and
perpendicular parts are the two legs of a right triangle with $\vec F$ as
hypotenuse.

First make the unit vector along the rod. $|\vec d| = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 4} =
\sqrt9 = 3$, so $\hat d = (1/3,\, 2/3,\, 2/3)$. Then

$$F_\parallel = \vec F\cdot\hat d = (3)\tfrac13 + (4)\tfrac23 + (0)\tfrac23 = 1 + 2.667 = 3.667\ \text{N}.$$

The full magnitude is $|\vec F| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$ N. So

$$F_\perp = \sqrt{5^2 - 3.667^2} = \sqrt{25 - 13.44} = \sqrt{11.56} = 3.40\ \text{N}.$$

That 3.40 N is what the rod has to supply to keep the bead on it. Splitting a
vector into "along" and "across" some awkward direction, rather than along $x$
and $y$, is one of the most reusable moves you will learn.

## The cross product: how much of this lies across that

The dot product throws away the perpendicular part. Sometimes the perpendicular
part is the whole point. A wrench does nothing if you push along the handle;
only the sideways push turns the bolt. That calls for a second product, built
from $\sin\theta$ instead of $\cos\theta$:

$$|\vec A\times\vec B| = AB\sin\theta.$$

Unlike the dot product, this one is itself a vector. Its magnitude is the area
of the parallelogram with $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ as sides — base $A$, height $B
\sin\theta$. Its direction is perpendicular to both, given by the right-hand
rule: point your fingers along $\vec A$, curl them toward $\vec B$, and your
thumb points along $\vec A\times\vec B$. Swapping the order reverses it, so
$\vec B\times\vec A = -\vec A\times\vec B$. The cross product of a vector with
itself, or with any parallel vector, is zero, because $\sin 0 = 0$.

In components, with $\hat\imath$, $\hat\jmath$, $\hat k$ the unit vectors along
$x$, $y$, $z$:

$$\vec A\times\vec B = (A_yB_z - A_zB_y,\; A_zB_x - A_xB_z,\; A_xB_y - A_yB_x).$$

The pattern is cyclic: the $x$ entry uses $y$ and $z$, the $y$ entry uses $z$ and
$x$, the $z$ entry uses $x$ and $y$. Write the cycle $x \to y \to z \to x$ in the
margin and you will never misremember a sign.

A wrench pushed with 80 N at 0.25 m from the bolt, at $60^\circ$ to the handle,
gives torque $\tau = rF\sin\theta = (0.25)(80)(0.866) = 17.3$ N·m. Same formula,
different name: torque is $\vec r\times\vec F$, angular momentum is $\vec r
\times \vec p$, and magnetic force is $q\vec v\times\vec B$.

**Worked example 4.** A proton, $q = 1.6\times10^{-19}$ C, moves with $\vec v =
(2.0, -1.0, 3.0)\times10^6$ m/s through $\vec B = (0.050, 0.10, -0.020)$ T. Find
the magnitude of the magnetic force.

Nothing here is perpendicular to anything, so the angle route is hopeless and you
go straight to components. Drop the $10^6$ and restore it at the end. Taking the
three entries of $\vec v\times\vec B$ in turn:

$$x: (-1.0)(-0.020) - (3.0)(0.10) = 0.020 - 0.300 = -0.280$$
$$y: (3.0)(0.050) - (2.0)(-0.020) = 0.150 + 0.040 = 0.190$$

Careful here: the middle entry of the cyclic formula is $A_zB_x - A_xB_z$, which
is what was computed, so $y = +0.190$.

$$z: (2.0)(0.10) - (-1.0)(0.050) = 0.200 + 0.050 = 0.250$$

Magnitude: $\sqrt{0.280^2 + 0.190^2 + 0.250^2} = \sqrt{0.0784 + 0.0361 + 0.0625}
= \sqrt{0.1770} = 0.4207$, so $|\vec v\times\vec B| = 4.207\times10^5$ T·m/s.
Then

$$F = q|\vec v\times\vec B| = (1.6\times10^{-19})(4.207\times10^5) = 6.73\times10^{-14}\ \text{N}.$$

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  $\vec A\cdot\vec B$ is a number; $\vec A\times\vec B$ is a vector. Writing
  $\vec A\cdot\vec B$ with an arrow on top, or taking the magnitude of a dot
  product and expecting a direction, will wreck a problem quietly. Also
  remember $\sin$ and $\cos$ are not interchangeable here: the dot product peaks
  when the vectors are parallel, the cross product peaks when they are at right
  angles.
</Aside>

## Choosing the axes

Nothing forces $x$ to be horizontal. Axes are yours to place, and the single
highest-return decision in a mechanics problem is where to point them. On a
ramp, tilt the axes so one runs along the slope. Then the acceleration lies
entirely on one axis, the normal force entirely on the other, and only gravity
needs splitting — into $mg\sin\alpha$ down the slope and $mg\cos\alpha$ into it.

Try it. A 40 kg crate sits on a frictionless $25^\circ$ ramp while a worker
pushes horizontally into the hill with 200 N. Along the slope, gravity pulls
down-slope with $mg\sin 25^\circ = (40)(9.8)(0.4226) = 165.6$ N. The horizontal
push makes angle $25^\circ$ with the slope surface, so its along-slope
(up-slope) component is $(200)\cos 25^\circ = (200)(0.9063) = 181.3$ N. The net
along-slope force is $181.3 - 165.6 = 15.6$ N up the slope. Two lines, because
the axes were chosen to make the normal force irrelevant.

## Recognising when this is the tool

Some signals to watch for in a problem statement.

Any mention of an angle with a force, a velocity, or a displacement means
components. Write $A\cos\theta$ and $A\sin\theta$ before you do anything else.

Two motions combining — a boat and a current, a plane and a wind, a passenger
walking on a moving train — means relative velocity, which means adding vectors
in components. Ask which component controls the *time* and which controls the
*distance*; they are usually different, and that observation is the solution.

The words "component along", "how much of the force does work", "perpendicular
to", "at what angle", or "for what value of $k$ are these at right angles" are
all dot product. So is finding the perpendicular leftover: get the parallel part
with a dot product, then use Pythagoras.

Torque, angular momentum, magnetic force, the area of a parallelogram or
triangle, the perpendicular distance from a point to a line, and any phrase like
"lever arm" or "impact parameter" are cross product. That last one is worth
keeping: the closest approach of a particle travelling in a straight line, with
position $\vec r$ and velocity $\vec v$, is $|\vec r\times\vec v|/|\vec v|$,
because the cross product already contains the perpendicular distance times the
speed. Two ships on straight courses, an alpha particle flying past a nucleus —
same formula both times. And when a problem hands you three cables or three
supports in 3D, you are being asked for three component equations in three
unknown tensions, each cable's pull written as its tension times a unit vector
along the cable.

Once a quantity can be split into pieces this way, the next natural question is
how each piece *changes* over time. That is where
[derivatives](/guide/derivatives) come in.
