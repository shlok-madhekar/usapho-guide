## Fields from currents

Moving charge makes a magnetic field, and a magnetic field pushes on moving
charge. The force is a cross product, so it is always perpendicular to the
velocity:

$$\vec F = q\vec v \times \vec B, \qquad \vec F = I\vec L \times \vec B$$

Because the force is perpendicular to the motion, a magnetic field does no
work. It bends paths without changing speeds. A charge moving perpendicular to
a uniform field travels a circle of radius $r = mv/qB$.

For the fields themselves, symmetry decides the tool. Ampère's law plays the
role Gauss's law plays in electrostatics:

$$\oint \vec B \cdot d\vec\ell = \mu_0 I_{enc}$$

giving $B = \mu_0 I/2\pi r$ for a long wire and $B = \mu_0 n I$ inside a
solenoid. When the geometry is not symmetric, Biot-Savart and an integral are
the fallback.

## Induction

A changing flux drives an EMF:

$$\mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}, \qquad \Phi_B = \int \vec B \cdot d\vec A$$

Flux changes three ways: the field changes, the area changes, or the
orientation changes. A rotating loop uses the third and produces the sinusoid
that makes AC generation possible.

The minus sign is Lenz's law, and it is a statement about energy rather than a
sign convention. The induced current opposes the change that created it, which
is why a magnet dropped through a copper pipe falls slowly and why generators
get harder to turn as you draw more current from them.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Motional EMF problems ask for force, current and power in a loop that is
  simultaneously being pushed and pushed back on. Solve them in order: EMF
  from the flux rule, then current from the EMF, then the retarding force from
  that current. Trying to shortcut to the force usually loses a factor.
</Aside>
