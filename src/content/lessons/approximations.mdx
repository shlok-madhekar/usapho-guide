## Near a point, everything is a polynomial

Zoom in far enough on any smooth curve and it looks like a straight line —
its tangent. Zoom in less and it looks like a parabola. Taylor's theorem makes
that precise: near $x = 0$,

$$f(x) = f(0) + f'(0)\,x + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(0)\,x^2 + \dots$$

Keep as many terms as the problem needs. Usually one or two is plenty, because
each extra term is smaller by another factor of $x$, and $x$ is small.

The three expansions that appear again and again:

$$\sin x \approx x - \frac{x^3}{6}, \qquad \cos x \approx 1 - \frac{x^2}{2}, \qquad (1+x)^n \approx 1 + nx$$

and $e^x \approx 1 + x$, $\ln(1+x) \approx x$. All valid for $|x| \ll 1$.

The binomial form is the workhorse. It handles any power, including negative
and fractional ones: $1/(1+x) \approx 1 - x$ and $\sqrt{1+x} \approx 1 + x/2$
are the same rule with $n = -1$ and $n = 1/2$.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ requires $\theta$ in radians. In degrees the
  derivative of $\sin$ carries a factor of $\pi/180$ and every expansion above
  is wrong. Convert first, always. This single mistake ruins more pendulum
  problems than any conceptual error.
</Aside>

## Recognising the cue

A problem is asking for an expansion when it says "small oscillations", "for
$x \gg d$", "to first order in", "thin", "slightly displaced", or gives you a
quantity that is obviously tiny compared to another in the problem.

The pendulum is the standard case. The exact equation of motion is
$\ddot\theta = -(g/L)\sin\theta$, which has no elementary solution. Replace
$\sin\theta$ by $\theta$ and it becomes $\ddot\theta = -(g/L)\theta$, the
simple harmonic oscillator, with period $2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$. The approximation is
not laziness; it is what makes the problem solvable at all, and at $10^\circ$
it is accurate to better than 0.2%.

The far-field cue works the same way. A dipole of charges $\pm q$ separated by
$d$, seen from distance $r \gg d$, has potential
$\frac{kq}{r - d/2} - \frac{kq}{r + d/2}$. Factor out $r$, expand each term
with the binomial rule, and the leading $1$'s cancel, leaving $kqd/r^2$. The
cancellation is the point: you needed the first-order term precisely because
the zeroth-order terms killed each other.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Expand to one order past the first surviving term when terms might cancel.
  If you truncate too early in a difference of two nearly equal quantities,
  you get zero instead of the answer.
</Aside>

## How far can you push it

Set a small parameter and track it. If $\epsilon = 0.1$, then first order is
good to about 1%, second order to about 0.1%. Once you know the size of the
first neglected term, you know your error bar.

Judgement matters more than the algebra. Expanding in the wrong variable, or
expanding a quantity that is not actually small, produces a confident and
completely wrong answer. Ask what is small compared to what, name it, and
expand in that.
