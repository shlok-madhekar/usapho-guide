## A force is conservative when the path does not matter

Carry a book from the floor to a shelf. Lift it straight up, or walk it around
the room first and then up: gravity does exactly $-mgh$ either way. Drag the
same book across the floor and back to where it started, and friction has taken
energy every centimeter of the trip. That is the whole distinction.

A force is **conservative** if the work it does between two points depends only
on the endpoints, equivalently if the work around any closed loop is zero:

$$\oint \vec F \cdot d\vec r = 0$$

Gravity, spring forces, and the electrostatic force pass. Friction, air drag,
and the push of your hand do not. Only for a conservative force can you define
a potential energy at all, because otherwise "the energy stored at this point"
would depend on how you got there and would not be a function of position.

## Potential energy is stored negative work

Define $U$ so that the work done by the conservative force is the drop in $U$:

$$W_{\text{cons}} = -\Delta U$$

Near Earth's surface, gravity does $-mgh$ as you rise, so $U_g = mgh$. Far from
a planet the force falls off and the integral gives $U_g = -GMm/r$, negative
because the zero is placed at infinity. For a spring obeying $F = -kx$:

$$U_s = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$$

Every one of these has an arbitrary additive constant. Only differences in $U$
appear in physics, so put the zero wherever it makes the algebra shortest —
usually the lowest point of the motion, or the spring's natural length.

## Read the force off the graph

Turn the definition around and the force is the negative slope of the potential
curve:

$$F_x = -\frac{dU}{dx}$$

This makes a $U(x)$ graph enormously informative. Where the curve slopes upward,
the force pushes back toward $-x$. At a minimum the slope is zero, so the force
vanishes and the point is a **stable** equilibrium — nudge the particle and it
gets pushed home. At a maximum the equilibrium is unstable. Draw a horizontal
line at the total energy $E$; the particle is confined to the regions where
$U(x) \le E$, and it turns around exactly where the line meets the curve.

Near a smooth minimum, every potential looks like a parabola, which is why so
much of physics reduces to a spring.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Never count the same interaction twice. If you write a gravitational
  potential energy $mgh$, do not also include the work done by gravity as a
  separate term. Potential energy is a bookkeeping device that *replaces* that
  work, and adding both makes gravity twice as strong as it is.
</Aside>
