## A force that never speeds anything up

Tie a stone to a string and whirl it around your head at a steady rate. The
stone's speed is not changing. Your hand is still pulling hard on the string,
and you can feel it. Something has to be accelerating, because a force acts
and nothing else is there to cancel it — but the speedometer reading of the
stone, if it had one, would sit perfectly still.

That is the whole puzzle of this lesson, and the resolution is one line long:
acceleration is the rate of change of *velocity*, and velocity is a vector.
Turn the vector without lengthening it and you have still changed it. In the
last lesson you watched a spring trade force for stretch along a single line.
Here the force does something stranger: it bends the path and leaves the speed
alone.

## How big the inward acceleration is

Take an object going round a circle of radius $r$ at constant speed $v$. In a
short time $\Delta t$ it moves along the arc through a small angle
$\Delta\theta$. The velocity vector always points along the direction of
travel, so as the object swings through $\Delta\theta$, the velocity vector
swings through exactly the same angle $\Delta\theta$.

Draw the old velocity and the new velocity starting from the same point. Both
have length $v$, and there is an angle $\Delta\theta$ between them. The change
$\Delta \vec v$ is the little vector joining the tips. For a small angle that
gap is an arc of a circle of radius $v$, so

$$|\Delta \vec v| = v\,\Delta\theta$$

Divide by $\Delta t$. The angle swept per second is the *angular velocity*
$\omega = \Delta\theta/\Delta t$, measured in radians per second, so

$$a = \frac{|\Delta \vec v|}{\Delta t} = v\,\omega$$

And since the object covers arc length $r\,\Delta\theta$ in that same time,
$v = \omega r$. Substituting either way round:

$$a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r = \omega v$$

Which direction does it point? $\Delta \vec v$ is perpendicular to $\vec v$ —
it has to be, since the length of $\vec v$ is not changing — and it tilts
toward the inside of the turn. So the acceleration points at the centre of the
circle. That is what *centripetal* means: centre-seeking. The subscript $c$ is
just a reminder of the direction.

Three more relations you will use constantly. If the period is $T$, one
revolution of arc length $2\pi r$ takes $T$ seconds, so $v = 2\pi r/T$ and
$\omega = 2\pi/T$. And revolutions per minute convert by dividing by 60 to get
revolutions per second, then multiplying by $2\pi$.

  $a_c = v^2/r$ is brutally sensitive to speed. A car that corners comfortably

**Example 1.** A point on a record sits 0.15 m from the centre and the record
turns at 45 revolutions per minute. What is its centripetal acceleration?

Convert the rate first. $45$ rev/min $= 45/60 = 0.75$ rev/s, and each
revolution is $2\pi$ radians, so

$$\omega = 0.75 \times 2\pi = 4.712\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$

Now use the form that already has $\omega$ in it, so you never need the speed:

$$a_c = \omega^2 r = (4.712)^2 \times 0.15 = 22.21 \times 0.15 = 3.33\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$$

## There is no such thing as a centripetal force

This is the single habit that decides whether the rest of the topic is easy or
miserable. $mv^2/r$ is not a force. It is a *requirement*. It is the answer the
real forces have to add up to, in the inward direction, if the object is going
to keep going round.

So the free body diagram never gets an arrow labelled "centripetal force". It
gets the arrows it always got: gravity, the normal force, tension, friction.
Then you pick the inward direction as positive, add up the components of those
real forces along it, and set the total equal to $mv^2/r$:

$$\sum F_{\text{inward}} = \frac{mv^2}{r}$$

Perpendicular to that — along the direction of motion — the forces must sum to
$ma_{\text{tangential}}$, which is zero when the speed is steady.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Do not add $mv^2/r$ to your force diagram as an extra outward arrow to
  "balance" things. Centrifugal force is a bookkeeping device for observers who
  are themselves spinning, and it will cost you a factor of two or a sign the
  moment you mix it with real forces. Draw real forces only, put $mv^2/r$ on
  the right-hand side of the equals sign, and keep it there.
</Aside>

## Cornering: when friction is the inward force

A car on a flat road turns because static friction from the road pushes it
sideways, toward the centre of the curve. The vertical direction is boring —
$N = mg$ — so the most static friction the road can supply is
$f_{\max} = \mu_s N = \mu_s m g$. Setting that equal to the requirement gives
the fastest safe cornering speed:

$$\mu_s m g = \frac{mv^2}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu_s g r}$$

The mass cancels, which is why a loaded truck and an empty one skid at roughly
the same speed.

Friction is one vector, though, with one budget. If the car is also braking,
the friction force has to do two jobs at once: point inward with magnitude
$mv^2/r$ *and* point backward with magnitude $ma_{\text{brake}}$. Those two
demands are perpendicular, so they add like the legs of a right triangle, and
the total cannot exceed $\mu_s m g$.

**Example 2.** A car takes a flat curve of radius 100 m at 20 m/s on a road
with $\mu_s = 0.80$. How hard can it brake at the same time without skidding?

The total friction available, per kilogram, is

$$\frac{f_{\max}}{m} = \mu_s g = 0.80 \times 9.8 = 7.84\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$$

The turning already spends

$$a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{20^2}{100} = \frac{400}{100} = 4.00\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$$

These are at right angles, so what is left is not $7.84 - 4.00$. It is the
other leg of the triangle:

$$a_{\text{brake}} = \sqrt{(7.84)^2 - (4.00)^2} = \sqrt{61.47 - 16.00} = \sqrt{45.47} = 6.74\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$$

Subtracting instead of using Pythagoras gives 3.84, and it is the most common
wrong answer on this kind of problem.

## Vertical circles and the speed you cannot go below

Now swing the stone in a vertical circle instead of a horizontal one. Gravity
still points down, but "inward" now rotates as the stone goes round, so the
component of gravity that counts keeps changing.

At the very top, the centre of the circle is directly below the stone. Both
the tension $T$ and the weight $mg$ point down, which is inward, so

$$T + mg = \frac{mv_{\text{top}}^2}{r}$$

Rearranged, $T = mv_{\text{top}}^2/r - mg$. Slow the stone down and $T$ falls.
A string can pull but it cannot push, so $T$ is not allowed to go negative.
The slowest the stone can go while the string stays taut is the speed at which
$T$ has just reached zero:

$$0 + mg = \frac{mv_{\min}^2}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_{\min} = \sqrt{gr}$$

That is the famous condition, and the reason it matters is what it means
physically: at that speed, gravity alone is exactly the force needed to bend
the path into the circle. The string has nothing left to do. Go slower and
gravity is more than enough, the stone falls inside the circle, and the string
crumples. Notice the mass has dropped out — a bucket of water and a marble
need the same minimum speed at the top of a 1.0 m circle, $\sqrt{9.8 \times 1.0}
= 3.13$ m/s.

At the bottom, the centre is above, so inward is up:

$$T - mg = \frac{mv_{\text{bot}}^2}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T = \frac{mv_{\text{bot}}^2}{r} + mg$$

The plus sign here and the minus sign at the top come entirely from which way
"inward" points. If you only remember one thing, remember to redraw the
picture at the point in the circle the question asks about.

Linking the top speed to the bottom speed takes energy conservation, which you
will meet properly soon but need here. Falling from the top of the circle to
the bottom is a drop of $2r$, so $v_{\text{bot}}^2 = v_{\text{top}}^2 + 4gr$.

**Example 3.** A 0.25 kg ball is swung in a vertical circle at exactly the
minimum speed at the top. What is the tension at the lowest point?

Here is the trick that makes this clean: you are not told $r$, so keep it
symbolic and hope it cancels. At the top, minimum speed means
$v_{\text{top}}^2 = gr$. Then

$$v_{\text{bot}}^2 = gr + 4gr = 5gr$$

Put that into the bottom equation:

$$T = \frac{m(5gr)}{r} + mg = 5mg + mg = 6mg$$

The radius cancelled. So the tension at the bottom of a critical loop is
always six times the weight, whatever the size of the circle:

$$T = 6 \times 0.25 \times 9.8 = 14.7\ \mathrm{N}$$

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Critical vertical loop: zero tension at the top, exactly $6mg$ at the bottom.
  If your answer to a loop problem is not a tidy multiple of $mg$, check
  whether the radius really should have cancelled.
</Aside>

## When the support can push back

The $v_{\min} = \sqrt{gr}$ condition comes from the string's inability to push.
Change the support and the condition changes with it.

A **rigid rod** can push as well as pull. Balanced at the top and nudged, a
ball on a rod can start with essentially zero speed, because the rod will hold
it up on the way round. So for a rod of length $\ell = 0.70$ m, the speed at
the bottom comes straight from the $2\ell$ drop with $v_{\text{top}} \approx 0$:

$$v_{\text{bot}} = \sqrt{4g\ell} = \sqrt{4 \times 9.8 \times 0.70} = \sqrt{27.44} = 5.24\ \mathrm{m/s}$$

A **surface** can push but not pull, which flips the logic the other way. A
block sliding over the outside of a dome of radius $R$ has the centre of the
circle below it, so at an angle $\theta$ from the top the inward equation is

$$mg\cos\theta - N = \frac{mv^2}{R}$$

The block leaves the surface when $N$ falls to zero, so it leaves when
$v^2 = gR\cos\theta$. Meanwhile energy gives $v^2 = 2gR(1 - \cos\theta)$ for a
block starting from rest at the top. Setting those equal:

$$gR\cos\theta = 2gR(1-\cos\theta) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 3\cos\theta = 2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \cos\theta = \tfrac{2}{3}$$

The height above the centre of the dome is $R\cos\theta = \tfrac{2}{3}R$, so a
dome of radius 1.5 m releases the block at $\tfrac{2}{3}(1.5) = 1.0$ m. The
angle is the same for every dome; only the height scales.

## Two more sources of the inward force

**A rotating string carrying several masses.** Cut the string anywhere in your
mind and ask what the piece beyond the cut needs. The inner segment has to
supply the inward force for *everything* outside it. For two 0.30 kg beads at
0.40 m and 0.80 m rotating at 5.0 rad/s, the inner segment carries both:

$$T = m\omega^2 r_1 + m\omega^2 r_2 = 0.30 \times 25 \times (0.40 + 0.80) = 7.5 \times 1.2 = 9.0\ \mathrm{N}$$

For a continuous chain the same logic holds with the mass of the outer piece
placed at its own centre of mass. Tension is largest at the axis and zero at
the free tip.

**Gravity, for an orbit.** A satellite in a circular orbit is held in by
gravity and nothing else, so $GMm/r^2 = mv^2/r$. The satellite's mass cancels.
Writing $v = 2\pi r/T$ and rearranging gives $T^2 \propto r^3$, so
$T \propto r^{3/2}$. Four times the radius means $4^{3/2} = 8$ times the
period.

A **banked track** uses the horizontal component of the normal force. With no
friction, the normal force's vertical part carries the weight and its
horizontal part does the turning, and dividing one by the other gives
$\tan\theta = v^2/(gr)$. For $r = 50$ m, $v = 20$ m/s and $g = 10$ m/s²,
$\tan\theta = 400/500 = 0.8$, so $\theta = 38.7°$. The [next
lesson](/guide/banked-curves) takes that apart properly, along with what
happens when friction joins in.

## Recognising it

Reach for this technique when the problem contains any of these:

- The words circle, loop, curve, corner, orbit, revolution, rpm, or a period
  for something going round.
- A radius together with a speed, or a radius together with an angular rate.
- A question about the *minimum* or *maximum* speed for something to stay on a
  track, in a bucket, or on a string.
- A normal force or tension that you are asked to find at a specific point of a
  curved path.

Then the procedure is always the same four steps. Draw the object at the exact
moment asked about. Mark which way the centre is from there — that is
"inward". Add up the real forces along that direction, with signs, and set the
sum equal to $mv^2/r$. If two different points of the path are involved, link
their speeds with energy conservation before you solve.

The one extra question to ask, every time: can the thing supplying the force
only pull, only push, or both? A string only pulls, a surface only pushes, a
rod does either. That single answer is what sets the critical condition, and
it is where most of the hard problems in this topic hide.
