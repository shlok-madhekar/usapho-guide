## Your boat can't beat the river

Here's a puzzle. Keep it in the back of your head, because we're gonna crack it halfway through.

A river is 150 m wide and flows at 3.0 m/s. You're in a rowboat that does 2.0 m/s in still water. Clearly, the river's faster than you. So you're getting dragged downstream no matter what. Rough. But you do get to pick where the craft points, and you wanna land as close to straight across as you possibly can.

Got it on instinct? Nice. Didn't? Totally fine. By the end of this chapter you'll nail the exact answer in like six lines.

The tool is vectors, and I wanna pitch them to you a bit differently than most books do. Forget "an arrow with a length and a direction." Think of a vector as **a little list of numbers, one per direction, and each number does its own thing.** That's it. The boat problem feels impossible because your brain wants ONE speed. But there are two, across and along, and they straight up ignore each other.

## Splitting a vector

Draw two perpendicular axes, $x$ and $y$. Stick a vector $\overrightarrow{A}$ at the origin, tilted up at an angle $\theta$ from the $+x$ axis. Now drop a straight line from its tip down to the $x$ axis so it hits at a right angle.

Boom, right triangle. $\overrightarrow{A}$ is the long slanty side. One leg runs along $x$, one runs along $y$. Pretty much everything in this chapter grows out of that little triangle.

The hypotenuse has length $A$, the *magnitude* (just a positive number with units). Basic trig hands you the legs:

$$A_x = A\cos\theta, \qquad A_y = A\sin\theta.$$

$A_x$ is the *$x$-component*, aka how far the vector reaches in the $x$ direction. If it comes out negative, the vector's just reaching backwards along that axis. Totally normal, nothing broke.

You can also go backwards. The pieces rebuild the whole vector:

$$A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{A_y}{A_x}.$$

One warning, since this bites everybody once. Don't memorise "cosine goes with $x$." The real rule is about where the angle's measured *from*. If a problem gives you the angle from the vertical, then the vertical leg is the one touching the angle, and it gets the cosine. So sketch the triangle every single time and just read the answer off it. Takes five seconds and saves you from a whole category of dumb mistakes.

<Aside label="Pocket rule">
  A vector equation is secretly a bunch of normal equations stacked together, one per axis. When you write $\overrightarrow{F_{\text{net}}} = m\overrightarrow{a}$ in 2D, you've really written two scalar equations, and you can solve them one at a time.
</Aside>

**Worked example 1.** You kick a soccer ball at 20 m/s, $30^\circ$ above the ground. How far does it go before it lands? (Ignore air.)

Split the launch velocity first:

$$v_x = 20\cos30^\circ = 17.3\ \text{m/s}, v_y = 20\sin30^\circ = 10\ \text{m/s}.$$

Now the two directions get totally separate jobs. Only gravity messes with $y$, so the ball's in the air for $t = 2v_y/g = 2(10)/9.8 = 2.04$ s. Nothing messes with $x$ (unless you consider air resistance), so it just cruises at 17.3 m/s the whole time:

$$\text{range} = v_x t = (17.3)(2.04) \approx 35\ \text{m}.$$

See what happened? You never needed a "diagonal speed." You needed two numbers and the fact that they don't bother each other. That's the whole superpower.

(Same trick works for a kid dragging a sled with a rope. The horizontal part of the tension pulls the sled forward, and the vertical part lifts some of the sled's weight off the ground, which is why pulling at an angle helps with friction. One vector, two jobs.)

## Adding vectors

To add two vectors with a drawing, slide the second one so its tail touches the first one's tip. The sum goes from the first tail to the last tip. With components it's even easier, since the $x$ stuff and the $y$ stuff never even meet:

$$\overrightarrow{A} + \overrightarrow{B} = (A_x + B_x,\; A_y + B_y).$$

A hiker walks 4.0 km east, then 3.0 km north. Her displacement is $(4.0, 3.0)$ km, so she's $\sqrt{4.0^2 + 3.0^2} = 5.0$ km from where she started. Not 7.0. The legs didn't point the same way, so the lengths don't just stack. **Magnitudes don't add. Components do.**

Subtraction is the same thing with a sign flip, and that's where people faceplant. Say a car's going 12 m/s east, then turns a corner and ends up going 12 m/s north. Same speed. Its change in velocity is

$$\Delta\overrightarrow{v} = \overrightarrow{v_f} - \overrightarrow{v_i} = (0, 12) - (12, 0) = (-12, 12)\ \text{m/s},$$

which has magnitude $12\sqrt2 = 17$ m/s. The speedometer didn't budge, but the velocity changed a TON. Remember this one. It's the seed of all of circular motion.

### Back to your boat

Relative velocity is just vector addition with two people watching. Velocities add through the middle man. Your velocity relative to the ground is your velocity relative to the water, plus the water's velocity relative to the ground. With $x$ downstream and $y$ across:

$$\overrightarrow{v_{\text{ground}}} = \overrightarrow{v_{\text{water}}} +  \overrightarrow{v_{\text{current}}}.$$

Now here's the fact that makes every river problem easy:

- The **across** component decides *how long* the crossing takes.
- The **downstream** component decides *how far* you get pushed during that time.

They never talk to each other. Picture a swimmer going 1.6 m/s straight across a 40 m river. She takes $40/1.6 = 25$ s, and a 1.2 m/s current shoves her $(1.2)(25) = 30$ m downstream. The current can't slow her crossing down. It just slides her sideways while she's busy crossing.

**Worked example 2.** Our rower: $v_b = 2.0$ m/s, $v_c = 3.0$ m/s, $L = 150$ m. She aims the bow at an angle $\theta$ upstream of straight across. Find the smallest drift she can get.

Write her ground velocity in pieces:

$$v_y = v_b\cos\theta \quad(\text{across}), \qquad v_x = v_c - v_b\sin\theta \quad(\text{downstream}).$$

The crossing takes $t = L/(v_b\cos\theta)$, and the drift is $d = v_x t$. Put those together and the drift is

$$d = L\,\frac{v_x}{v_y}.$$

So the drift depends only on the *ratio* of the two components. In other words, it depends on how much her ground-velocity arrow leans downstream. To minimise the drift, make that arrow lean as little as possible.

Now the picture. Draw the current as a 3.0 arrow pointing downstream. Her boat velocity is an arrow of fixed length 2.0 that she can aim in any direction, and her ground velocity is the sum of the two. Because she can swing the boat arrow around freely, its tip can land anywhere on a **circle of radius 2.0 centered on the tip of the current arrow**. Her ground velocity is the arrow from the origin to some point on that circle.

Which point on the circle leans downstream the least? Pivot a line around the origin and sweep it toward "straight across." It keeps touching the circle until the moment it just grazes it. That's the *tangent* line, and it's the best she can do. Any steeper and the line misses the circle entirely, which means no heading can produce it.

A tangent to a circle is perpendicular to the radius at the touching point, and here that radius *is* the boat arrow. So the best heading has the boat velocity at a right angle to her ground velocity. That leaves a right triangle:

- The hypotenuse is the current, $v_c = 3.0$.
- One leg is the boat speed, $v_b = 2.0$.
- The other leg is her ground speed, $\sqrt{v_c^2 - v_b^2} = \sqrt{5} = 2.236$ m/s.

Now find the angle. The boat arrow is tilted $\theta$ from the across direction, and the ground arrow is perpendicular to the boat arrow. So the ground arrow is tilted $\theta$ from the downstream direction, which is the angle in the triangle between the current and the ground arrow. The boat arrow is the side opposite it:

$$\sin\theta = \frac{v_b}{v_c} = \frac{2.0}{3.0} = 0.6667.$$

So $\theta = 41.8^\circ$ and $\cos\theta = 0.7454$. Plug in:

- Across speed: $(2.0)(0.7454) = 1.491$ m/s, so $t = 150/1.491 = 100.6$ s.
- Downstream speed: $3.0 - (2.0)(0.6667) = 1.667$ m/s.
- Drift: $(1.667)(100.6) = 168$ m.

Compare that to aiming straight across, which gives 225 m. Tilting upstream saves her about 57 m. And check out how we got there: two components, time from one, distance from the other, then a circle and a tangent line. No fancy machinery.

<Aside label="Calculus shortcut, coming later">
  There's another way to find this: write the drift as a function of $\theta$ and ask where it bottoms out. That takes calculus, which we haven't covered yet. We'll come back to this exact problem in the [derivatives](/guide/derivatives) chapter and get the same $\sin\theta = v_b/v_c$ in a few lines.
</Aside>

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Don't point the *resultant* straight across unless the problem says she lands directly opposite. Heading and course are different vectors. "She aims across" pins down $\overrightarrow{v_{\text{water}}}$. "She lands opposite" pins down $\overrightarrow{v_{\text{ground}}}$. Read which one the problem fixes. (And if $v_c > v_b$, landing directly opposite just isn't possible.)
</Aside>

## Dot product: how much of you points my way?

Sometimes you don't need the whole vector, just the slice of it pointing in one specific direction. The horizontal pull on that sled was exactly this. 

Take two vectors $\overrightarrow{A}$ and $\overrightarrow{B}$ with angle $\theta$ between them. Imagine a flashlight shining straight down onto the line of $\overrightarrow{B}$. The shadow of $\overrightarrow{A}$ on that line has length $A\cos\theta$. Multiply that shadow by the length of $\overrightarrow{B}$ and you've got the *dot product*:

$$\overrightarrow{A}\cdot\overrightarrow{B} = AB\cos\theta.$$

It's just a number, no direction. Positive if the vectors kinda agree, zero at exactly $90^\circ$, negative if they oppose each other. In components it's even simpler, since each axis chips in on its own:

$$\overrightarrow{A}\cdot\overrightarrow{B} = A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z.$$

Two formulas for the same thing is the whole point. The component version turns raw coordinates into a number. The cosine version turns that number into an angle:

$$\cos\theta = \frac{\overrightarrow{A}\cdot\overrightarrow{B}}{AB}.$$

  <Sim id="vectors-dot" />
</Figure>

Three things it's awesome for:

**Work.** $W = \overrightarrow{F}\cdot\overrightarrow{d}$, since only the part of the force along the motion does anything. With $\overrightarrow{F} = (12, -5)$ N and $\overrightarrow{d} = (3, 4)$ m: $W = 36 - 20 = 16$ J.

**Right-angle test.** If $\overrightarrow{A}\cdot\overrightarrow{B} = 0$ and neither one is zero, they're perpendicular. Wanna find the $k$ that makes $(3, k, -2)$ perpendicular to $(4, 5, 1)$? Set $12 + 5k - 2 = 0$, so $k = -2$. Done.

**Projection.** The component of $\overrightarrow{F}$ along some direction is $\overrightarrow{F}\cdot\hat u$, where $\hat u$ is a *unit vector* (length exactly 1) pointing that way. Make one by dividing any vector by its own length: $\hat u = \overrightarrow{d}/|\overrightarrow{d}|$.

**Worked example 3.** A bead's threaded on a straight rod pointing along $\overrightarrow{d} = (1, 2, 2)$. A force $\overrightarrow{F} = (3, 4, 0)$ N acts on it. How big is the part of $\overrightarrow{F}$ perpendicular to the rod?

Grabbing the perpendicular piece directly in 3D is a pain. So be lazy, but smart about it. Find the *parallel* piece with a dot product, then let Pythagoras hand you the leftover. Parallel and perpendicular are the two legs of a right triangle, with $\overrightarrow{F}$ as the hypotenuse.

Unit vector along the rod: $|\overrightarrow{d}| = \sqrt{1+4+4} = 3$, so $\hat d = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac23)$. Then

$$F_\parallel = \overrightarrow{F}\cdot\hat d = (3)\tfrac13 + (4)\tfrac23 + (0)\tfrac23 = 1 + 2.667 = 3.667\ \text{N}.$$

The full size is $|\overrightarrow{F}| = \sqrt{9+16} = 5$ N, so

$$F_\perp = \sqrt{5^2 - 3.667^2} = \sqrt{11.56} = 3.40\ \text{N}.$$

That's how hard the rod has to push sideways to keep the bead on it. "Split into along and across some awkward direction" is one of the most reusable moves in all of physics, so get comfy with it.

## Cross product: how much of you points across me?

The dot product ignores the perpendicular part. But sometimes the perpendicular part is the ONLY part that matters. Push a wrench straight along its handle and nothing happens. Only the sideways push turns the bolt. So we need a second product, built from $\sin\theta$ this time:

$$|\overrightarrow{A}\times\overrightarrow{B}| = AB\sin\theta.$$

Unlike the dot product, this one's a **vector**. Its size is the area of the parallelogram with $\overrightarrow{A}$ and $\overrightarrow{B}$ as sides (base $A$, height $B\sin\theta$). Its direction is perpendicular to both, set by the right-hand rule: fingers along $\overrightarrow{A}$, curl them toward $\overrightarrow{B}$, and your thumb points along $\overrightarrow{A}\times\overrightarrow{B}$.

Order matters: $\overrightarrow{B}\times\overrightarrow{A} = -\overrightarrow{A}\times\overrightarrow{B}$. And crossing a vector with itself, or with anything parallel to it, gives zero, since $\sin 0 = 0$.

  <Sim id="vectors-cross" />
</Figure>

In components, with $\hat\imath, \hat\jmath, \hat k$ along $x, y, z$:

$$\overrightarrow{A}\times\overrightarrow{B} = (A_yB_z - A_zB_y,\; A_zB_x - A_xB_z,\; A_xB_y - A_yB_x).$$

It's cyclic. The $x$ entry uses $y$ and $z$, then the $y$ entry uses $z$ and $x$, then the $z$ entry uses $x$ and $y$. Scribble $x \to y \to z \to x$ in the margin and you'll never botch a sign.

You'll see this same operation wearing a bunch of different name tags. Torque is $\overrightarrow{r}\times\overrightarrow{F}$, angular momentum is $\overrightarrow{r}\times\overrightarrow{p}$, magnetic force is $q\overrightarrow{v}\times\overrightarrow{B}$. A wrench pushed with 80 N at 0.25 m from the bolt and $60^\circ$ from the handle gives $\tau = rF\sin\theta = (0.25)(80)(0.866) = 17.3$ N·m.

Quick bonus: the area of a triangle with sides $(1, 2)$ and $(3, 1)$ is half the cross product's size. Only the $z$ entry survives: $(1)(1) - (2)(3) = -5$. So the area is $5/2 = 2.5$. No base and height needed, which is pretty sweet.

**Worked example 4.** A proton ($q = 1.6\times10^{-19}$ C) zooms along with $\overrightarrow{v} = (2.0, -1.0, 3.0)\times10^6$ m/s through $\overrightarrow{B} = (0.050, 0.10, -0.020)$ T. How big is the magnetic force?

Nothing here's perpendicular to anything, so skip the angle and go straight to components. Drop the $10^6$ for now and put it back at the end:

$$x: (-1.0)(-0.020) - (3.0)(0.10) = 0.020 - 0.300 = -0.280$$
$$y: (3.0)(0.050) - (2.0)(-0.020) = 0.150 + 0.040 = 0.190$$
$$z: (2.0)(0.10) - (-1.0)(0.050) = 0.200 + 0.050 = 0.250$$

(Watch the middle one. Its pattern is $A_zB_x - A_xB_z$, which looks flipped compared to the other two. That's just the cycle doing its thing.)

Magnitude: $\sqrt{0.280^2 + 0.190^2 + 0.250^2} = \sqrt{0.1770} = 0.4207$, so $|\overrightarrow{v}\times\overrightarrow{B}| = 4.207\times10^5$ T·m/s. Then

$$F = q|\overrightarrow{v}\times\overrightarrow{B}| = (1.6\times10^{-19})(4.207\times10^5) = 6.73\times10^{-14}\ \text{N}.$$

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  $\overrightarrow{A}\cdot\overrightarrow{B}$ is a number. $\overrightarrow{A}\times\overrightarrow{B}$ is a vector. Slapping an arrow on a dot product, or expecting a direction out of one, will quietly wreck a problem. And don't mix up sine and cosine: the dot product's biggest when the vectors are parallel, the cross product's biggest at right angles.
</Aside>

## You pick the axes

Nothing says $x$ has to be horizontal. The axes are yours, and where you aim them is the single best decision you can make in a mechanics problem. On a ramp, tilt them so one runs along the slope. Now acceleration sits on one axis, the normal force sits on the other, and only gravity needs splitting: $mg\sin\alpha$ down the slope and $mg\cos\alpha$ into it.

Let's try it. A 40 kg crate sits on a frictionless $25^\circ$ ramp, and a worker pushes horizontally into the hill with 200 N.

- Gravity along the slope: $mg\sin25^\circ = (40)(9.8)(0.4226) = 165.6$ N, down-slope.
- The push makes a $25^\circ$ angle with the slope surface, so its up-slope part is $200\cos25^\circ = 181.3$ N.
- Net along the slope: $181.3 - 165.6 = 15.6$ N, up-slope.

Three lines, and the normal force never even showed up. That's what good axes do for you.

## Field guide: which tool, when?

Problems leave clues. Learn to hear them.

- **An angle stuck to a force, velocity or displacement.** Components. Write $A\cos\theta$ and $A\sin\theta$ before you do anything else.
- **Two motions stacking** (boat and current, plane and wind, you walking down a moving train). Relative velocity, so add components. Then ask: which component sets the *time*, and which sets the *distance*? They're usually different, and spotting that IS the solution.
- **"Component along," "how much of the force does work," "perpendicular to," "at what angle," "for what $k$ are these at right angles."** Dot product. Same goes for any "perpendicular leftover": get the parallel part, then Pythagoras.
- **Torque, angular momentum, magnetic force, area of a parallelogram or triangle, "lever arm," "impact parameter," shortest distance from a point to a line.** Cross product. That last one's a keeper: for a particle on a straight path with position $\overrightarrow{r}$ and velocity $\overrightarrow{v}$, the closest approach is $|\overrightarrow{r}\times\overrightarrow{v}|/|\overrightarrow{v}|$, because the cross product already has perpendicular distance times speed baked in. Two ships on straight courses, an alpha particle skimming past a nucleus: same formula both times.
- **Three cables or supports in 3D.** You've been handed three component equations and three unknown tensions. Write each cable's pull as tension times a unit vector along the cable, and go.

So now you can split a quantity into pieces, glue them back together, and pick axes that make a problem way easier. The obvious next question is how each piece *changes* over time, and that's exactly what [derivatives](/guide/derivatives) are for.
