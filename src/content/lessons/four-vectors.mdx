## Package energy and momentum together

Relativistic collision problems get ugly when you write conservation of energy
and conservation of momentum as separate equations and then chase gamma
factors through both. The fix is to notice that energy and momentum transform
into each other the same way time and space do, and to carry them as one
object:

$$P = (E/c,\; p_x,\; p_y,\; p_z)$$

This is the energy-momentum four-vector. Under a change of frame its
components mix by the Lorentz transformation, exactly as $(ct, x, y, z)$ does.
Conservation of energy and momentum becomes a single statement: the total
four-vector of a system is conserved, $\sum P_{\text{in}} = \sum P_{\text{out}}$.

## The invariant is the whole trick

Four-vectors have a length that every observer agrees on. For $P$ it is

$$P \cdot P = \frac{E^2}{c^2} - p^2 = m^2c^2$$

which rearranges to the relation worth memorizing:

$$E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2$$

The right side contains only the rest mass, so it is the same number in every
frame. A photon has $m = 0$ and therefore $E = pc$.

The method: write the conservation statement as four-vectors, then square both
sides. Squaring produces an invariant, and an invariant can be evaluated in
whichever frame makes it easiest. That freedom is what kills the algebra.

Threshold problems show this best. A proton strikes a stationary proton and
you want the minimum beam energy to produce two protons plus a
proton-antiproton pair. In the lab the final state is four particles with
unknown momenta, which is a mess. Square the total four-vector instead and
evaluate it in the center-of-momentum frame, where at threshold all four
products sit at rest and the invariant is simply $(4m_pc^2)^2/c^2$. Set that
equal to the lab-frame expression and one line of algebra gives the answer.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  The invariant mass of a *system* is not the sum of the masses of its parts.
  Two photons flying apart have zero mass each but a nonzero system invariant
  mass, which is exactly the energy available to create new particles. Ask
  what invariant the problem wants before you pick a frame.
</Aside>

## The relativistic Doppler shift

The same frame-mixing applies to light. A source emitting frequency $f_0$ in
its own rest frame, moving directly away from you at speed $v$, is observed at

$$f = f_0 \sqrt{\frac{1 - \beta}{1 + \beta}}, \qquad \beta = v/c$$

Flip the sign of $\beta$ for approach, which raises the frequency. Two effects
combine here: the classical change in arrival spacing as the source moves, and
time dilation of the source's own emission rate. Only the second is
relativistic, and it is why there is a shift even for **transverse** motion,
where the source is momentarily neither approaching nor receding:

$$f_{\perp} = \frac{f_0}{\gamma}$$

A purely classical treatment predicts no transverse shift at all, so observing
one is direct evidence of time dilation.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Do not mix frames inside a single equation. The commonest error is writing
  momentum with a velocity measured in the lab and an energy measured in the
  center-of-momentum frame. Pick one frame, write every component in it, and
  only then transform. If you find yourself needing a velocity-addition
  formula, that is usually a sign an invariant would have avoided the step.
</Aside>
