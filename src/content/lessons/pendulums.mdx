## Where the period comes from

A pendulum of length $L$ displaced by angle $\theta$ has a restoring torque
from gravity. Only the component of weight perpendicular to the string does
anything:

$$mL^2\ddot{\theta} = -mgL\sin\theta$$

That $\sin\theta$ is the problem. It makes the equation nonlinear and there is
no elementary solution. But for small angles $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, and
the equation collapses to the one shape every oscillation problem reduces to:

$$\ddot{\theta} = -\frac{g}{L}\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}$$

Notice what is absent. No mass, because gravity supplies both the restoring
force and the inertia, and $m$ cancels. And no amplitude, which is the whole
point of the approximation.

## Why amplitude drops out, and when it comes back

Amplitude disappears because the linearized restoring torque is exactly
proportional to displacement. Double the swing and you double the restoring
torque, so the motion covers twice the distance at twice the speed and takes
the same time.

The real $\sin\theta$ is slightly *less* than $\theta$, so a large swing is
restored a little too weakly and runs slow:

$$T \approx 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\left(1 + \frac{\theta_0^2}{16}\right)$$

At 10° the correction is about 0.2 percent. At 60° it is near 7 percent. Small
angle is not a formality, it is a numerical claim you should be willing to
check.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Do not carry $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ into a physical pendulum. A swinging rod or
  hoop is not a point mass on a string, and using its total length as $L$ gives
  the wrong answer. Compute $I$ about the pivot and the distance $d$ to the
  center of mass instead.
</Aside>

## The physical pendulum

Any rigid body pivoted off its center of mass swings the same way. Replace
inertia with the moment of inertia about the pivot and the restoring lever arm
with $d$, the pivot-to-center-of-mass distance:

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{mgd}}$$

A uniform rod of length $L$ pivoted at one end has $I = \tfrac{1}{3}mL^2$ and
$d = L/2$, giving $T = 2\pi\sqrt{2L/3g}$. It swings faster than a simple
pendulum of the same length, because much of its mass sits closer to the pivot
than the tip does.

Setting $I = mL^2$ and $d = L$ recovers the simple pendulum, as it must.
