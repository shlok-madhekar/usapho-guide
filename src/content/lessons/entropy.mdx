## The question the second law leaves open

Put a hot block against a cold one and heat crosses in one direction only. You
have never seen the cold one get colder while the hot one gets hotter, even
though energy would balance perfectly if it did. In the last lesson you met
Carnot's result: no engine running between $T_H$ and $T_C$ can beat
$1 - T_C/T_H$. That was a statement about engines. But the ban on cold-to-hot
heat flow is not about engines at all, and neither is the fact that a gas
released into a vacuum never gathers itself back into one corner.

So there must be some quantity that keeps score. Something that goes up when a
process runs the way processes actually run, and that would have to go down for
the backwards version, which is why the backwards version never happens. That
quantity is entropy, and the useful thing about it is that you can compute it
with numbers and units, not just wave at it.

## A quantity that depends only on where you are

Start from the Carnot engine, because it is the one process you already know
exactly. Over one cycle it takes $Q_H$ from the hot reservoir and dumps $Q_C$
into the cold one, and Carnot's efficiency forces

$$\frac{Q_C}{Q_H} = \frac{T_C}{T_H}$$

Rearrange that into $Q_H/T_H = Q_C/T_C$. Count heat going *into* the working
substance as positive and heat leaving as negative, and the two terms cancel:
the sum of $Q/T$ around the whole cycle is zero. Heat itself does not come back
to zero over a cycle — the engine absorbed more than it rejected, that was the
point. But heat divided by the temperature at which it crossed does.

A quantity whose total change around any closed loop is zero is a *state
function*: its value depends only on which state you are in, not on how you got
there. You have met one before — potential energy has exactly this property,
which is why $\Delta U$ between two points does not care about the path. So
define entropy $S$ by its changes along a reversible path:

$$dS = \frac{dQ_{rev}}{T}, \qquad \Delta S = \int_i^f \frac{dQ_{rev}}{T}$$

Read it in words. $dQ_{rev}$ is a small amount of heat entering the system, in
joules, and $T$ is the absolute temperature in kelvin at which it enters. Their
ratio has units of J/K, and that is what entropy is measured in. Heat in raises
entropy; heat out lowers it. The same joule bought at a low temperature buys
more entropy than at a high one, because you are dividing by a smaller number.

The subscript "rev" is doing real work and is not decoration. The formula only
tells you $\Delta S$ if you evaluate it along a reversible path, one where the
system has a single well-defined temperature at every instant. Real processes
usually do not. That sounds crippling. In a moment it turns out to be the
source of the technique.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Absolute temperature, always. A kelvin in the denominator, never a celsius
  reading. Writing $Q/20$ for a process at $20^\circ$C instead of $Q/293$ is
  the single most common arithmetic wreck in this topic.
</Aside>

## The three integrals you will actually do

Almost every entropy calculation in a problem set is one of three shapes.

**A reservoir, or anything at fixed temperature.** If $T$ never changes, it
comes out of the integral and $\Delta S = Q/T$. A "large bath", a "huge
reservoir", a furnace wall "held at 400 K" — these are all telling you that the
body is big enough that the heat you give it does not shift its temperature.
Melting and boiling are in this family too, because a phase change happens at
one fixed temperature: $\Delta S = mL/T$.

**A body that warms up.** Here $T$ changes as the heat goes in, so you need the
integral. With heat capacity $C = mc$ held constant, $dQ = C\,dT$, and

$$\Delta S = \int_{T_i}^{T_f} \frac{C\,dT}{T} = C\ln\frac{T_f}{T_i}$$

That is the whole of the calculus you need here: the integral of $1/T$ is
$\ln T$. If you have not met logarithms in this role, all you need is that
$\ln(a/b) = \ln a - \ln b$ and that $\ln$ of a number less than 1 is negative —
so cooling gives negative entropy change, as it should.

**An ideal gas going anywhere.** Put the first law inside the definition. For
$n$ moles, $dQ_{rev} = nC_V\,dT + P\,dV$, and $P = nRT/V$, so

$$dS = nC_V\frac{dT}{T} + nR\frac{dV}{V}$$

Integrate each piece and you get two logs:

$$\Delta S = nC_V\ln\frac{T_f}{T_i} + nR\ln\frac{V_f}{V_i}$$

Nothing but the endpoints survives. The path that got the gas there has
vanished from the answer, which is the state-function property showing up
concretely. Set $T_f = T_i$ and you have the isothermal case,
$\Delta S = nR\ln(V_f/V_i)$. Set $V_f = V_i$ and you have constant-volume
heating. For constant pressure it is easier to swap in $C_P$ and the
temperature ratio, $\Delta S = nC_P\ln(T_f/T_i)$, since at fixed pressure the
volume ratio equals the temperature ratio anyway.

## Invent a path

Since $\Delta S$ depends only on the endpoints, you are free to reach them any
way you like. This is a licence, and it is the central trick of the topic.

The real process might be violent: a partition yanked out, two blocks slammed
together, a paddle stirring a fluid. None of those has a reversible path, and
at no instant does the system have one temperature you could put in a
denominator. So do not use the real process. Write down the initial state and
the final state, throw away everything that happened in between, and invent a
slow reversible path connecting the same two states. The number you get is the
entropy change of the real process, because entropy does not know the
difference.

Take the standard case. One mole of ideal gas sits behind a partition in an
insulated box; you remove the partition and it fills twice the volume. No heat
entered, no work was done on anything, so the internal energy and therefore the
temperature are unchanged. The substitute path is a slow isothermal expansion
from $V$ to $2V$, and along it $\Delta S = R\ln 2 = 8.31 \times 0.693 = 5.76$
J/K. That is the answer for the free expansion too.

  temperature difference, stop and build a reversible substitute before you put

## Entropy of the universe, and why it only goes up

The system is never the whole story. Add up the entropy change of everything
involved — the system plus every reservoir it touched — and call it
$\Delta S_{univ}$. The second law, in its most usable form, says this total is
zero for a reversible process and strictly positive for a real one. It never
goes down.

You can see where the positivity comes from with the simplest irreversible
process there is. Let $Q$ leak from a body at $T_H$ to one at $T_C$, both big
enough not to change temperature:

$$\Delta S_{univ} = \frac{Q}{T_C} - \frac{Q}{T_H} = Q\left(\frac{1}{T_C} - \frac{1}{T_H}\right)$$

Positive whenever $T_H > T_C$, which is the only direction it runs. Try it
backwards and the expression flips sign, which is the ban you were looking for
at the start. And notice what happens as $T_H \to T_C$: the entropy generated
goes to zero. That is why reversible heat transfer has to happen across a
vanishing temperature difference, and hence infinitely slowly.

A real engine is the same bookkeeping. The working substance returns to its
initial state each cycle, so its own $\Delta S$ is zero; all the entropy shows
up in the reservoirs, $-Q_H/T_H + Q_C/T_C$. Carnot's engine makes that exactly
zero. Every worse engine rejects more heat than Carnot's and makes it positive.
So Carnot's limit is not a separate law — it is what $\Delta S_{univ} \ge 0$
looks like when you apply it to an engine.

## Worked examples

**One. Heating a pot of water.** 2.0 kg of water, $c = 4186$ J/kg·K, taken
slowly from $20^\circ$C to $80^\circ$C. The body warms, so this is the log
case. First convert: $T_i = 293$ K, $T_f = 353$ K. The heat capacity is
$C = mc = 2.0 \times 4186 = 8372$ J/K. Then

$$\Delta S = 8372 \ln\frac{353}{293} = 8372 \times \ln(1.2048) = 8372 \times 0.1863 = 1560 \text{ J/K}$$

Positive, because heat went in. If you had used $Q/T$ with $Q = 8372 \times 60
= 5.02\times10^5$ J you would have had to pick a $T$, and there is no single
right one to pick — that is the signal that the log is required.

**Two. A hot ball in a cold bath.** A 0.50 kg lead ball, $c = 128$ J/kg·K, at
$100^\circ$C, dropped into a very large water bath held at $20^\circ$C. Find
the entropy change of ball plus bath.

Two bodies, two different treatments, and that is the whole lesson of this
example. The ball's temperature slides from 373 K to 293 K, so it gets the log:

$$\Delta S_{ball} = (0.50)(128)\ln\frac{293}{373} = 64 \ln(0.7855) = 64 \times (-0.2414) = -15.45 \text{ J/K}$$

The bath does not change temperature, so it gets $Q/T$. The heat it receives is
what the ball gave up, $Q = 64 \times 80 = 5120$ J, arriving at 293 K:

$$\Delta S_{bath} = \frac{5120}{293} = +17.47 \text{ J/K}$$

Total: $-15.45 + 17.47 = +2.02$ J/K. Positive, as it must be, because dropping
a hot thing into a cold bath is irreversible. The ball's loss is real; the
bath's gain is bigger because the joules arrived at a lower temperature than
they left.

**Three. Both variables moving.** 2.0 mol of a monatomic ideal gas,
$C_V = \tfrac{3}{2}R$, goes from 300 K at volume $V_0$ to 250 K at $3V_0$ along
"some complicated path". The phrase is a gift: it means you are not supposed to
care. Use the two-log formula.

Temperature term: $nC_V = 2.0 \times 1.5 \times 8.31 = 24.93$ J/K, and
$\ln(250/300) = \ln(0.8333) = -0.1823$, giving $24.93 \times (-0.1823) = -4.55$
J/K. Volume term: $nR = 2.0 \times 8.31 = 16.62$ J/K, and $\ln 3 = 1.0986$,
giving $16.62 \times 1.0986 = +18.26$ J/K. Add them:

$$\Delta S = -4.55 + 18.26 = +13.71 \text{ J/K}$$

Cooling pulled entropy down, expanding pushed it up more.

**Four. Squeezing work out of a cooling block.** A 1.0 kg copper block,
$c = 386$ J/kg·K, starts at 500 K and is used as the hot source of a
*reversible* engine whose cold reservoir is a huge bath at 300 K. The engine
runs until the block has cooled to 300 K. How much work comes out?

You cannot use $1 - T_C/T_H$ directly, because $T_H$ is falling the whole time.
Use entropy instead. Reversible means $\Delta S_{univ} = 0$, and that one
equation is enough.

The block cools from 500 K to 300 K, so its entropy change is the log case:

$$\Delta S_{block} = 386 \ln\frac{300}{500} = 386 \times (-0.5108) = -197.2 \text{ J/K}$$

The bath is at fixed 300 K, so its entropy change is $Q_C/300$. For the total
to be zero, $Q_C/300 = +197.2$, giving $Q_C = 5.916\times10^4$ J. The heat the
block gave up is straightforward energy bookkeeping:
$Q_H = mc\,\Delta T = 386 \times 200 = 7.72\times10^4$ J. Energy in equals
energy out, so

$$W = Q_H - Q_C = 7.72\times10^4 - 5.916\times10^4 = 1.80\times10^4 \text{ J}$$

About 18.0 kJ, out of 77.2 kJ available: an overall efficiency of 23%, below
the 40% a Carnot engine gets at a fixed 500 K, because the source spends most
of its life cooler than that. The same move — set $\Delta S_{univ} = 0$ and
solve — handles two finite bodies driven to a common temperature, where the
condition $C\ln(T_f/T_1) + C\ln(T_f/T_2) = 0$ collapses to
$T_f = \sqrt{T_1T_2}$, the geometric mean rather than the average.

## What entropy is counting

The formula came from engines, but the meaning comes from counting. A
*macrostate* is what you can measure: pressure, temperature, how many molecules
are on the left. A *microstate* is one specific arrangement of every molecule.
Boltzmann's result is that

$$S = k_B \ln \Omega$$

where $\Omega$ is the number of microstates in the macrostate and
$k_B = 1.38\times10^{-23}$ J/K. Take four labelled molecules in a box you
imagine split down the middle. "All four on the left" can happen exactly one
way, $\Omega = 1$. "Two on each side" can happen $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ ways. So
the even split has more entropy by $k_B\ln 6 = 2.47\times10^{-23}$ J/K.

The log is what makes this match the thermodynamic entropy: counts multiply
when you combine systems, logs add, and entropy is additive. It also explains
the free expansion in one line. Double the volume and each of $N$ molecules has
twice as many places to be, so $\Omega$ is multiplied by $2^N$ and
$\Delta S = k_B N \ln 2$. For a mole, $k_B N_A = R$, and you are back to
$R\ln 2$. Two routes, the same number.

## Recognising an entropy problem

Reach for entropy when you see any of these.

The question asks directly for "the entropy change of the universe", or of a
body, in J/K. That is the obvious one, and the work is just deciding, for each
body in the problem, whether it is a reservoir ($Q/T$), a phase change ($mL/T$),
a body that warms ($C\ln(T_f/T_i)$), or a gas (two logs).

A process is described as sudden, free, unresisted, or as happening across a
finite temperature gap. Those words mean irreversible, which means invent a
path, and they also mean the answer for the universe will be positive. If you
get a negative total, you have made a sign error.

The word "reversible" appears and a temperature is changing. Then
$\Delta S_{univ} = 0$ is an equation you can solve, and it is usually the only
one that gives you the unknown — the maximum work, or the final common
temperature. This is the pattern that turns a very hard problem into two lines.

A problem gives you a complicated or unspecified path between two known states.
That is an invitation to ignore the path entirely.
