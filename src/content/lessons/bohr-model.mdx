## A fingerprint made of light

Heat a tube of hydrogen gas until it glows, send the light through a prism, and
you do not get a rainbow. You get four sharp coloured lines on a black
background — red, blue-green, violet, deep violet — and nothing in between.
Heat sodium instead and you get a different set of lines. Mercury, a third set.
Every element has its own pattern, always the same, whether the gas is in a lab
in Zurich or in a star ten thousand light years away.

That is a very strange fact. A hot filament gives off a smooth continuous
spectrum. A hot gas of single atoms gives off a barcode. Something inside the
atom can only give up energy in certain exact amounts, and those amounts are
fixed properties of the element. The previous lesson on the [photoelectric
effect](/guide/photoelectric) left you with a photon of energy $E = hf$ as the
unit in which light is emitted and absorbed. If the atom can only emit photons
of particular energies, the atom itself must only have particular energies.
This lesson works out what those energies are.

## Why the classical atom cannot work

Start with the picture you would draw without knowing any of this. A proton of
charge $+e$ sits at the centre. An electron of mass $m$ and charge $-e$ circles
it at radius $r$ and speed $v$. The only force is Coulomb attraction, and it
points inward, so it is the centripetal force:

$$
\frac{ke^2}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r}
$$

Here $k = 8.99 \times 10^9$ N·m²/C² is the Coulomb constant. Multiply both
sides by $r$ and you get $ke^2/r = mv^2$, so the kinetic energy is

$$
K = \tfrac12 mv^2 = \frac{ke^2}{2r}
$$

The potential energy of two opposite charges a distance $r$ apart is
$U = -ke^2/r$, negative because you would have to do work to pull them apart.
So the total energy is

$$
E = K + U = \frac{ke^2}{2r} - \frac{ke^2}{r} = -\frac{ke^2}{2r}
$$

Notice what fell out: $K = -U/2$, and $E = U/2 = -K$. That relation holds for
any inverse-square orbit — it is the same one you met for satellites in
[gravitation](/guide/gravitation), and it is called the *virial theorem*. It
will save you arithmetic later. If a problem hands you the total energy of a
bound orbit, the potential energy is twice it and the kinetic energy is minus
it.

So far nothing forbids any value of $r$. Shrink the orbit and $E$ drops; every
energy from $0$ down to $-\infty$ is available, so the atom should emit a
continuous spectrum. Worse, an accelerating charge radiates. A classical
electron would spiral into the proton in about $10^{-11}$ seconds. Classical
physics does not predict the wrong spectrum for hydrogen. It predicts that
hydrogen cannot exist.

## Bohr's one extra rule

In 1913 Bohr added a single restriction and changed nothing else. The
electron's orbital angular momentum, $L = mvr$, cannot take any value. It comes
in whole multiples of $\hbar = h/2\pi = 1.055 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s:

$$
L = mvr = n\hbar, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots
$$

The integer $n$ is the *principal quantum number*. Bohr had no justification
for this in 1913; the reason arrives in the [next lesson](/guide/de-broglie).
Take it as a rule and follow the consequences.

You now have two equations, $ke^2/r = mv^2$ and $mvr = n\hbar$, and two
unknowns, $r$ and $v$. Solve the second for $v = n\hbar/(mr)$ and substitute
into the first:

$$
\frac{ke^2}{r} = m \cdot \frac{n^2\hbar^2}{m^2r^2} = \frac{n^2\hbar^2}{mr^2}
$$

Multiply through by $r^2$ and divide by $ke^2$:

$$
r_n = \frac{n^2\hbar^2}{mke^2} = n^2 a_0, \qquad a_0 = 0.0529\ \text{nm}
$$

The constant $a_0$ is the *Bohr radius*, the size of the smallest allowed
orbit. The radii go as $n^2$: 0.0529 nm, 0.212 nm, 0.476 nm, 0.847 nm. The
atom has a definite size, and that size comes out right — hydrogen really is
about a tenth of a nanometre across.

Put $r_n$ back into $v = n\hbar/(mr)$ and the $n^2$ beats the $n$:

$$
v_n = \frac{ke^2}{n\hbar} = \frac{\alpha c}{n}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{1}{137}
$$

The dimensionless number $\alpha = ke^2/(\hbar c)$ is the *fine-structure
constant*. In the ground state the electron moves at $c/137 = 2.19 \times 10^6$
m/s, fast but only 0.7% of light speed, which is why ignoring relativity works
as well as it does.

## The energy ladder

Substitute $r_n = n^2a_0$ into $E = -ke^2/(2r)$:

$$
E_n = -\frac{ke^2}{2a_0}\cdot\frac{1}{n^2} = -\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2}
$$

The 13.6 eV is the *Rydberg energy*. The minus sign means bound: you must
supply energy to get the electron out to $r = \infty$, where $E = 0$. So the
ladder reads $-13.6$ eV, $-3.40$ eV, $-1.51$ eV, $-0.850$ eV, $-0.544$ eV, and
so on, crowding up towards zero. The gaps shrink fast; above $n \approx 10$ the
levels are almost a continuum.

The first prediction is free. To ionise a ground-state hydrogen atom you must
lift it from $-13.6$ eV to $0$, so the ionisation energy is 13.6 eV. That was a
measured number in 1913 and the model reproduced it exactly.

For a *hydrogen-like* ion — one electron, nuclear charge $+Ze$ — replace $e^2$
by $Ze^2$ everywhere in the derivation. The radius picks up a $1/Z$ and the
energy picks up a $Z^2$:

$$
r_n = \frac{n^2 a_0}{Z}, \qquad E_n = -\frac{13.6\,Z^2}{n^2}\ \text{eV}
$$

So He⁺ ($Z = 2$) has a ground state at $-54.4$ eV, and Li²⁺ ($Z = 3$) sits at
$-122.4$ eV in the ground state and $-13.6 \times 9/4 = -30.6$ eV at $n = 2$.
Neutral helium, with two electrons, is not covered: the electrons repel each
other and the derivation collapses.

## Lines are differences

A spectrometer never sees a level. It sees a photon, and a photon appears when
the electron drops from an upper level $n_i$ to a lower level $n_f$, carrying
away exactly the gap:

$$
hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = E_{n_i} - E_{n_f} = 13.6\,Z^2\left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right)\ \text{eV}
$$

Work in electronvolts and nanometres and you never need $h$ or $c$ separately,
because $hc = 1240$ eV·nm. Then $\lambda = 1240/\Delta E$ with $\Delta E$ in eV
and $\lambda$ in nm. Memorise that one number and most of these problems become
two lines of arithmetic.

Group the transitions by where they land. All the drops to $n_f = 1$ form the
*Lyman series*, in the ultraviolet. Drops to $n_f = 2$ form the *Balmer
series*, which is the one in the visible — those four coloured lines. Drops to
$n_f = 3$ are the *Paschen series*, in the infrared. Within a series, the
longest wavelength comes from the smallest gap ($n_i = n_f + 1$) and the
shortest, called the *series limit*, comes from $n_i = \infty$.

Absorption is the same arithmetic backwards. Cold hydrogen sitting in front of
a white-light source swallows a photon only if its energy matches a gap
exactly, so the transmitted light has dark lines in precisely the places where
hot hydrogen glows. That is how you read the composition of a star.

### Worked example 1: the red line

Hydrogen drops from $n = 3$ to $n = 2$. Find the wavelength.

Write both levels first. $E_3 = -13.6/9 = -1.511$ eV. $E_2 = -13.6/4 = -3.400$
eV. The photon carries the difference:

$$
\Delta E = -1.511 - (-3.400) = 1.889\ \text{eV}
$$

Then $\lambda = 1240/1.889 = 656$ nm. That is deep red, and it is the brightest
line in a hydrogen tube. It has a name, H-alpha, and you will meet it again.

### Worked example 2: do Balmer and Paschen overlap?

The Balmer series runs from its limit at 365 nm up to H-alpha at 656 nm. Does
the Paschen series reach down into it?

The shortest Paschen wavelength is the largest Paschen gap, which is $n_i =
\infty$ down to $n_f = 3$:

$$
\Delta E = 13.6\left(\frac{1}{9} - 0\right) = 1.511\ \text{eV}
$$

$$
\lambda = \frac{1240}{1.511} = 820\ \text{nm}
$$

820 nm is longer than 656 nm, so the two series do not overlap — there is a
clean gap between them. This is general: because the series limit of one series
always sits at longer wavelength than the longest line of the series below it,
the hydrogen spectrum sorts itself into separated blocks. That is what makes a
barcode identifiable.

## When the ladder is not quite 13.6

Three corrections turn up often enough that you should recognise them.

**Nuclear charge.** Already covered: multiply the whole ladder by $Z^2$ and
divide the radii by $Z$.

**Reduced mass.** The derivation assumed the nucleus stays put. It does not
quite; both particles orbit their common centre of mass. The fix is to replace
the electron mass $m$ by the *reduced mass*

$$
\mu = \frac{m M}{m + M} = \frac{m}{1 + m/M}
$$

where $M$ is the nuclear mass. Since the energy is proportional to the orbiting
mass, every level scales by $\mu/m$. For hydrogen $m/M = 1/1836$, a 0.05%
correction — small, but it is exactly what separates hydrogen's spectrum from
deuterium's, and that difference is how deuterium was discovered.

**A different orbiting particle.** If a muon, 207 times heavier than an
electron, replaces the electron, the ladder scales by 207: the ground state
drops to $207 \times 13.6 = 2815$ eV, about 2.82 keV, and the orbit shrinks by
the same factor of 207. Positronium, an electron bound to a positron, has
$M = m$, so $\mu = m/2$ and the ground state is $13.6/2 = 6.8$ eV.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  The photon energy is a *difference* between two levels, never a level itself.
  A drop from $n = 3$ to $n = 2$ gives 1.89 eV, not 1.51 eV. Compute both
  energies, subtract, then convert with $\lambda = 1240/\Delta E$. The same
  trap runs backwards: an atom that absorbs a 12.09 eV photon from the ground
  state is not going to the "12.09 eV level", it is going to $-13.6 + 12.09 =
  -1.51$ eV, which is $n = 3$.
</Aside>

### Worked example 3: a helium line disguised as hydrogen

A He⁺ ion emits a photon of exactly the same wavelength as hydrogen's H-alpha
line, and the transition ends on $n_f = 4$. What is the upper level?

From example 1, the photon energy is 1.889 eV. For He⁺, $Z = 2$, so the prefactor
is $13.6 \times 4 = 54.4$ eV:

$$
1.889 = 54.4\left(\frac{1}{16} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right)
$$

Divide: $1.889/54.4 = 0.03472$. Then

$$
\frac{1}{n_i^2} = 0.0625 - 0.03472 = 0.02778
$$

so $n_i^2 = 1/0.02778 = 36$ and $n_i = 6$. The coincidence is not an accident.
Because the He⁺ ladder is exactly four times hydrogen's, the He⁺ transition
$2n_i \to 2n_f$ always lands on hydrogen's $n_i \to n_f$ line. Doubling 3 and 2
gives 6 and 4, which is what you found. Astronomers had to untangle exactly
this confusion in early stellar spectra.

### Worked example 4: how hard must you hit an atom?

A ground-state hydrogen atom flies at a second ground-state hydrogen atom at
rest. What is the *minimum* kinetic energy the incoming atom needs to excite
one of them to $n = 2$?

The naive answer is 10.2 eV, the $1 \to 2$ gap. It is wrong, and the reason is
momentum. The incoming atom carries momentum, and momentum is conserved, so the
pair cannot end up at rest. Whatever kinetic energy they keep is unavailable
for exciting anything.

The cheapest outcome is the one where they keep as little as possible, which is
when both atoms end up moving together with the same velocity — a perfectly
inelastic collision, the same idea you used in
[collisions](/guide/collisions). Let each atom have mass $M$ and let the
incoming one have speed $v$. Momentum gives the common final speed:

$$
Mv = 2Mv_f \quad \Rightarrow \quad v_f = \tfrac12 v
$$

Initial kinetic energy is $\tfrac12 Mv^2$. Final kinetic energy is

$$
\tfrac12 (2M) v_f^2 = M\left(\tfrac{v}{2}\right)^2 = \tfrac14 Mv^2
$$

which is exactly half of what you started with. So only half the incoming
kinetic energy is ever available to be turned into internal energy. Setting
that half equal to the 10.2 eV you need:

$$
\tfrac12 K_{\min} = 10.2\ \text{eV} \quad \Rightarrow \quad K_{\min} = 20.4\ \text{eV}
$$

You have to hit it twice as hard as the gap suggests. The same bookkeeping runs
in reverse when an atom *emits*: the atom recoils, so the photon gets slightly
less than the full gap. The recoil energy is $E_\gamma^2/(2Mc^2)$, and for a
10.2 eV photon off a hydrogen atom whose rest energy is 938 MeV that is
$10.2^2/(2 \times 938 \times 10^6) = 5.5 \times 10^{-8}$ eV. Utterly
negligible here — but the same term is the whole story in nuclear gamma
emission.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  For very large $n$ the gap between neighbours is tiny and you can get it by
  differentiating. Since $E_n = -R/n^2$, a step of one level changes the energy
  by roughly $dE/dn = 2R/n^3$. For $n = 100$ that is $2 \times 13.6/10^6 =
  2.7 \times 10^{-5}$ eV, giving $\lambda \approx 1240/2.7\times10^{-5}
  \approx 4.6 \times 10^7$ nm, about 4.5 cm — radio waves from a single atom.
  These giant Rydberg atoms are real and radio astronomers detect them.
</Aside>

## What the model gets wrong

Keep the ladder, distrust the orbits. The energies $E_n = -13.6\,Z^2/n^2$ are
exactly what full quantum mechanics gives, and they are right. Everything else
in the picture is scaffolding.

The model says nothing about how *bright* a line is, so it cannot tell you
which transitions dominate. It gives no reason the orbits are stable despite
the electron accelerating. It fails completely for any atom with more than one
electron. And the crisp circular path itself is fiction — the electron has no
trajectory, and in the true ground state its angular momentum is zero, not
$\hbar$. Bohr's rule gets the right answer for the wrong reason, which happens
more often in physics than anyone likes to admit.

## Recognising a Bohr problem

Reach for this machinery when you see any of these:

- **Integers attached to an atom.** "$n = 3$ to $n = 2$", "the $n = 4$ level",
  "the upper state". Quantum numbers in the problem statement mean the ladder.
- **A wavelength or a photon energy together with an element.** Emission lines,
  absorption lines, series names (Lyman, Balmer, Paschen), "series limit",
  "ionisation energy". Convert with $\lambda = 1240/\Delta E$ nm and hunt for
  the pair of integers that fits.
- **One electron and a nucleus of charge $Ze$.** He⁺, Li²⁺, Be³⁺, or
  "hydrogen-like". Scale by $Z^2$.
- **A substituted particle.** Muonic hydrogen, positronium, deuterium versus
  hydrogen, "the nucleus is not infinitely heavy". Reduced mass.
- **A collision or a recoil around an excitation.** Franck-Hertz tubes, atoms
  striking atoms, an atom emitting while free. Conserve momentum first, then
  spend what energy is left on the level gap.

The standard opening move is the same every time: write down $E_{n_i}$ and
$E_{n_f}$ as numbers, subtract, and only then decide whether you need a
wavelength, a speed, or a radius. Almost every mistake in this topic comes from
skipping straight to a formula for $\lambda$ without first writing the two
levels.

Bohr's rule was a guess with nothing behind it. Ten years later de Broglie
supplied the reason, and it turned out that $L = n\hbar$ is just the statement
that a wave has to fit around the circle a whole number of times. That is
[next](/guide/de-broglie).
