## A coil that fights its own change

Pull the plug on a running vacuum cleaner in a dark room and you sometimes see a
blue spark jump inside the socket. Nothing was pushing that spark a moment
earlier — the appliance was running quietly on a steady current. The spark
appears at the instant the current is *cut off*, and it can carry a voltage far
larger than the 120 V the wall was supplying.

In [Motional EMF](/guide/motional-emf) you moved a conductor through a field and
got an emf out of it. Nothing had to move here. The coil inside the motor made
its own magnetic field, that field threaded its own turns, and when the current
died the field died with it. A changing flux through a loop induces an emf,
whether the flux came from a magnet across the room or from the very current
flowing in the loop. That second case is what follows, together with the two
circuits — LR and LC — where it dominates everything.

## Flux linkage, and the number called L

Start with a single coil of $N$ turns carrying current $I$. That current makes a
magnetic field everywhere, and everywhere in space the field is proportional to
$I$: double the current and you double $B$ at every point. So the flux $\Phi$
through one turn is proportional to $I$ too, and so is the **flux linkage**
$N\Phi$, the total flux counted once per turn.

Proportional means there's a constant of proportionality. Name it $L$:

$$N\Phi = LI$$

$L$ is the **self-inductance**, measured in henries (1 H = 1 Wb/A). Because both
sides scale with $I$ in the same way, $L$ itself does not depend on $I$ at all.
It depends only on the shape of the coil — how many turns, how big, how long.
Inductance is geometry, exactly as capacitance is geometry.

Now put that into Faraday's law. The emf around the coil is $-d(N\Phi)/dt$, and
since $L$ is a constant,

$$\mathcal{E} = -L\frac{dI}{dt}$$

Read it in words. The coil produces a voltage proportional to the *rate of
change* of its current. A steady current, however large, produces none. The
minus sign is Lenz's law: the emf opposes the change. Push the current up and
the coil pushes back like a headwind. Cut the current and the coil pushes
forward, trying to keep it going — that is the spark in the socket.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  A capacitor cannot change its voltage instantly. An inductor cannot change its
  current instantly. A jump in $I$ would mean infinite $dI/dt$ and infinite emf.
  Almost every transient problem below is solved by writing down what
  the inductor current was just before the switch moved, and insisting it is the
  same just after.
</Aside>

## Getting L from the geometry

The recipe is always the same three steps: assume a current $I$, find the field
it makes, integrate the field to get the flux linkage, then divide by $I$.

Do it for a long solenoid of length $\ell$, cross-sectional area $A$, with $N$
turns, so $n = N/\ell$ turns per metre. Ampère's law gave you $B = \mu_0 n I$
inside and essentially zero outside. Each turn is threaded by $\Phi = BA = \mu_0
n I A$, and there are $N = n\ell$ of them, so

$$N\Phi = n\ell \cdot \mu_0 n I A = \mu_0 n^2 A \ell \, I$$

Divide by $I$ and the current vanishes, as it must:

$$L = \mu_0 n^2 A \ell$$

**Worked example 1.** A solenoid has 500 turns over a length of 0.25 m with
cross-section $4.0\times10^{-4}$ m². Find $L$, and find the emf when the current
in it rises at a steady 40 A/s.

Turn density first: $n = 500/0.25 = 2000$ turns per metre. Then

$$L = (4\pi\times10^{-7})(2000)^2(4.0\times10^{-4})(0.25)$$

Take it piece by piece. $(2000)^2 = 4.0\times10^6$. Times $4\pi\times10^{-7}$
gives $5.027$. Times $4.0\times10^{-4}$ gives $2.011\times10^{-3}$. Times $0.25$
gives

$$L = 5.03\times10^{-4}\ \text{H}$$

The emf is $|\mathcal{E}| = L\,|dI/dt| = (5.03\times10^{-4})(40) = 2.0\times10^{-2}$ V.
Small, because the coil is small. A 0.15 H coil with the same 40 A/s would give
6.0 V.

A toroid works the same way but its field isn't uniform. For a toroid of $N$
turns, rectangular cross-section of height $h$, inner radius $a$ and outer
radius $b$, Ampère's law on a circle of radius $r$ inside gives $B = \mu_0 N
I/(2\pi r)$. The flux through one turn needs an integral because $B$ falls off
across the cross-section:

$$\Phi = \int_a^b \frac{\mu_0 N I}{2\pi r} h\,dr = \frac{\mu_0 N I h}{2\pi}\ln\frac{b}{a}$$

so

$$L = \frac{\mu_0 N^2 h}{2\pi}\ln\frac{b}{a}$$

Inductors in series and in parallel combine like resistors, not like capacitors,
as long as they are far enough apart that neither one's field threads the other.
In series the same $dI/dt$ runs through both and the emfs add, so $L = L_1 +
L_2$. In parallel the voltages match and the currents add, so $1/L = 1/L_1 +
1/L_2$. Two coils of 0.30 H and 0.60 H in parallel give $L = (0.30)(0.60)/0.90 =
0.20$ H.

## Energy in the magnetic field

Building up a current in an inductor costs work, because the whole time you are
pushing against the induced emf. The external source must supply a voltage
$L\,dI/dt$ just to overcome it, so the power it delivers is

$$P = I \cdot L\frac{dI}{dt}$$

Energy is the time integral of power, and $I\,dI/dt\,dt = I\,dI$, so pushing the
current from 0 up to some final $I$ costs

$$U = \int_0^{I} LI'\,dI' = \tfrac12 L I^2$$

That energy is not lost. It sits in the magnetic field and comes back out when
the current falls. A 0.20 H coil carrying 3.0 A holds $\tfrac12(0.20)(9.0) =
0.90$ J.

You can also count the energy field-point by field-point. Put $\tfrac12 LI^2$
for a solenoid next to its volume $A\ell$ and its field $B = \mu_0 n I$:

$$\frac{U}{A\ell} = \frac{\tfrac12 \mu_0 n^2 A\ell I^2}{A\ell} = \tfrac12 \mu_0 n^2 I^2 = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}$$

The geometry dropped out entirely, which is the sign that this is a general
truth about fields, not a fact about solenoids. The **magnetic energy density**
anywhere is

$$u_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}$$

A field of 0.80 T carries $0.64/(2\times4\pi\times10^{-7}) = 2.55\times10^{5}$
J per cubic metre.

**Worked example 2.** A coaxial cable carries 10 A out along an inner conductor
of radius 1.0 mm and back along an outer conductor at radius 5.0 mm. How much
magnetic energy is stored per metre?

There's no coil here, so $\tfrac12 LI^2$ has nothing obvious to plug into. Use
the density instead. Between the conductors, Ampère's law on a circle of radius
$r$ encloses the full 10 A, giving $B = \mu_0 I/(2\pi r)$. Outside the outer
conductor the enclosed current is zero, so there is no field and no energy
there.

Take a thin cylindrical shell of radius $r$, thickness $dr$, one metre long. Its
volume is $2\pi r\,dr$, and

$$dU = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}\,2\pi r\,dr = \frac{\mu_0 I^2}{8\pi^2 r^2}\,2\pi r\,dr = \frac{\mu_0 I^2}{4\pi}\frac{dr}{r}$$

Integrate from $a$ to $b$:

$$U = \frac{\mu_0 I^2}{4\pi}\ln\frac{b}{a} = (10^{-7})(100)\ln 5$$

$\ln 5 = 1.609$, so $U = (10^{-5})(1.609) = 1.61\times10^{-5}$ J per metre. Thin
shells and a logarithm: that combination shows up every time the field goes like
$1/r$.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  An inductor never has a resistance, and it is not a resistor with a delay. Do
  not write $V = IL$, and do not fold an inductor into a series-parallel
  reduction with resistors. Its voltage is set by the slope of its current, not
  its value. The only legal moves are to solve the loop equation, or to use the
  two limiting behaviours: at the first instant after a switch flips the
  inductor holds its old current, and after a long time with everything steady
  it acts as a plain wire.
</Aside>

## Mutual inductance

Now two coils. Run current $I_1$ through the first; some of its flux threads the
second. That flux linkage is again proportional to $I_1$, so define

$$N_2\Phi_2 = M I_1, \qquad \mathcal{E}_2 = -M\frac{dI_1}{dt}$$

$M$ is the **mutual inductance**, also in henries. A remarkable fact, which you
can take on trust here: $M$ comes out the same whichever coil you drive. Two
coils with $M = 0.020$ H, one of them carrying a current changing at 150 A/s,
induce $(0.020)(150) = 3.0$ V in the other.

The practical recipe is to drive whichever coil has the field you can compute.
For a small 100-turn coil of radius 1.0 cm sitting at the centre of a large
20-turn coil of radius 0.20 m, do not try to find the little coil's field at the
big one. Drive the big coil instead: at its centre $B = \mu_0 N_1 I/(2R) =
(4\pi\times10^{-7})(20)I/(0.40) = 6.28\times10^{-5}I$ T, essentially uniform over
the tiny coil. Its area is $\pi(0.010)^2 = 3.14\times10^{-4}$ m², so one turn
catches $1.97\times10^{-8} I$ Wb and 100 turns catch $1.97\times10^{-6}I$. Hence
$M = 1.97\times10^{-6}$ H.

## LR circuits: the switch closes, the switch opens

Put a battery $\mathcal{E}$, a resistor $R$ and an inductor $L$ in a loop and
close the switch at $t = 0$. Going round the loop, the battery lifts the
potential by $\mathcal{E}$, the resistor drops $IR$, and the inductor drops
$L\,dI/dt$:

$$\mathcal{E} = IR + L\frac{dI}{dt}$$

At the first instant $I = 0$ (it was zero before, and current is continuous), so
the whole battery voltage goes into $L\,dI/dt$: the current starts climbing at
$\mathcal{E}/L$. A 15 V battery on a 50 mH coil starts at $15/0.050 = 300$ A/s
whatever the resistance is. As $I$ grows, the resistor eats more of the voltage
and the climb flattens, until at $dI/dt = 0$ the current sits at
$I_\infty = \mathcal{E}/R$. Separating variables in the loop equation gives the
curve between:

$$I(t) = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right), \qquad \tau = \frac{L}{R}$$

Check that $\tau = L/R$ has units of seconds: henries are volt-seconds per amp,
ohms are volts per amp, so the division leaves seconds. And note the direction:
$\tau$ grows with $L$ but *shrinks* with $R$, opposite to the RC circuit. More
resistance drives the current to its final value faster.

Open the switch on a decaying loop instead and the same algebra with no battery
gives $I(t) = I_0 e^{-t/\tau}$. A 0.50 H coil dumping into 20 Ω has $\tau =
0.025$ s, and falling to a tenth of its starting current takes $t = \tau\ln 10 =
(0.025)(2.303) = 0.0576$ s.

**Worked example 3.** A 10 V battery, a 5.0 Ω resistor and a 0.50 H inductor are
connected in series at $t = 0$. How much heat does the resistor dissipate
between $t = 0$ and $t = \tau$?

Heat is not $I^2R\tau$, because $I$ is changing the whole time. You have to
integrate. First the constants: $I_\infty = 10/5.0 = 2.0$ A and $\tau =
0.50/5.0 = 0.10$ s. Then

$$Q = \int_0^{\tau} I^2 R\,dt = I_\infty^2 R\int_0^{\tau}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)^2 dt$$

Expand the square into $1 - 2e^{-t/\tau} + e^{-2t/\tau}$ and integrate each
piece over $0$ to $\tau$. The first gives $\tau$. The second gives
$-2\tau(1 - e^{-1})$. The third gives $\tfrac{\tau}{2}(1 - e^{-2})$. With
$e^{-1} = 0.3679$ and $e^{-2} = 0.1353$, the bracket is

$$\tau\left[1 - 2(0.6321) + \tfrac12(0.8647)\right] = \tau\left[1 - 1.2642 + 0.4323\right] = 0.1681\,\tau$$

So $Q = (2.0)^2(5.0)(0.10)(0.1681) = (2.0)(0.1681) = 0.336$ J. Sanity check: if
the current had been at its full 2.0 A the whole time you'd get $I^2R\tau = 2.0$
J, so 0.336 J is comfortably smaller, as it must be.

**Worked example 4.** A 0.40 H inductor and an 8.0 Ω resistor are switched
across a battery at $t = 0$. When does the inductor hold half of its eventual
energy?

Half the *energy*, not half the current — that's the whole problem. Energy goes
like $I^2$, so half the energy means $I = I_\infty/\sqrt{2} = 0.7071 I_\infty$.
Set that equal to the rise curve:

$$1 - e^{-t/\tau} = 0.7071 \;\Longrightarrow\; e^{-t/\tau} = 0.2929$$

Take logs: $t/\tau = -\ln(0.2929) = 1.228$. With $\tau = 0.40/8.0 = 0.050$ s,

$$t = (1.228)(0.050) = 0.0614\ \text{s}$$

Notice it takes longer than one time constant, because at $t = \tau$ the current
is only 63% of final and the energy only $0.63^2 = 40\%$.

That continuity rule also explains the spark. Suppose a 12 V battery sits in
series with 4.0 Ω, and an ideal inductor is wired in parallel with a 6.0 Ω
resistor. After a long time the inductor is a plain wire, so it shorts out the
6.0 Ω and carries the full $12/4.0 = 3.0$ A. Now open the switch. The inductor's
3.0 A cannot vanish, and the only path left is the 6.0 Ω resistor, so 3.0 A is
forced through it: $V = (3.0)(6.0) = 18$ V, larger than the battery that started
it. Real switches with air gaps and big coils are how you get thousands of
volts, and a spark.

## LC circuits: a circuit that oscillates

Take the resistor out. Charge a capacitor to $Q_0$, connect it across an
inductor, and let go. The loop rule says the capacitor's voltage $Q/C$ equals
the inductor's $L\,dI/dt$, and the current is the rate the capacitor discharges,
$I = -dQ/dt$. Substituting:

$$\frac{Q}{C} + L\frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} = -\frac{1}{LC}Q$$

That is exactly the equation you solved for a mass on a spring, with $Q$ in
place of $x$. So the answer is already known:

$$Q(t) = Q_0\cos\omega t, \qquad \omega = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$$

and the current $I = -dQ/dt = \omega Q_0 \sin\omega t$ has amplitude $I_{\max} =
\omega Q_0$. The analogy is exact and worth keeping: charge plays position,
current plays velocity, $L$ plays mass, and $1/C$ plays the spring constant. The
current peaks a quarter period after the charge does, just as a mass moves
fastest through equilibrium.

For $L = 2.0$ mH and $C = 5.0$ µF, $\omega = 1/\sqrt{(2.0\times10^{-3})(5.0\times10^{-6})}
= 1/\sqrt{10^{-8}} = 10^{4}$ rad/s, so $f = \omega/2\pi = 1592$ Hz.

Energy makes most LC problems faster than solving for the time dependence. The
total is constant:

$$\frac{Q^2}{2C} + \tfrac12 LI^2 = \frac{Q_0^2}{2C} = \tfrac12 LI_{\max}^2$$

**Worked example 5.** A 4.0 µF capacitor charged to 20 V is connected through a
2.0 mH inductor to an identical uncharged 4.0 µF capacitor. Resistance is
negligible. What is the maximum current?

This one needs an idea, because there is no single capacitor with a fixed
charge. Ask when the current is maximum. Current grows as long as there is a net
voltage around the loop to push it, and the push vanishes when the two capacitor
voltages are equal. Charge is conserved, so the 80 µC starts on one and ends
split evenly: 10 V on each.

Energy before: $\tfrac12(4.0\times10^{-6})(20)^2 = 8.0\times10^{-4}$ J. Energy in
the capacitors at that moment: two of them at 10 V, so
$2\times\tfrac12(4.0\times10^{-6})(100) = 4.0\times10^{-4}$ J. The missing
$4.0\times10^{-4}$ J is in the inductor:

$$\tfrac12 L I^2 = 4.0\times10^{-4} \;\Longrightarrow\; I^2 = \frac{8.0\times10^{-4}}{2.0\times10^{-3}} = 0.40$$

$I_{\max} = \sqrt{0.40} = 0.63$ A. Exactly half the stored energy ends up in the
inductor at that instant — a result worth remembering for any two-capacitor
sharing problem.

## Recognising it

Reach for this lesson when you see any of these:

A number given in henries, or a component described as a coil, solenoid, toroid
or choke. Then $L$ is either handed to you or has to be built from geometry with
the three-step recipe.

The words "immediately after the switch is closed" or "a long time later". Those
are the two limiting behaviours, and almost no algebra is needed: current
continuous at the switch, $dI/dt = 0$ at the end.

An exponential asked for at a specific time, or a time asked for at a specific
current. That's $\tau = L/R$ and one logarithm. If the question asks about
*energy* rather than current, remember to take a square root before you take the
log, as in example 4.

Heat, or total charge, over an interval rather than at an instant. That is an
integral of $I^2R$ or $I$, never a product with $\tau$.

A capacitor and an inductor in a loop with no resistance mentioned. That's
oscillation: get $\omega = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ first, and if the question asks for a
current or a charge at some condition, try energy conservation before you try
solving for time.

A surprising voltage across something, larger than any battery in the circuit.
An inductor's current was interrupted and it forced that current through
whatever path was left.
