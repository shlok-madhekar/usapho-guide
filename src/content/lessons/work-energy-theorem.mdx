## Work is force times displacement, but only the useful part

Push a crate sideways and lift it upward, and gravity has done nothing in the
first case and taken energy from you in the second. Only the component of force
along the motion counts, which is exactly what a dot product extracts:

$$W = \vec F \cdot \vec d = Fd\cos\theta$$

Here $\theta$ is the angle between the force and the displacement. At $90°$ the
work is zero, which is why a normal force on a sliding block, a rope tension on
a mass in circular motion, and the magnetic force on a charged particle all do
no work at all. They redirect motion without feeding it.

When the force changes along the way, or the path curves, sum the contributions
piece by piece:

$$W = \int_{A}^{B} \vec F \cdot d\vec r$$

For a spring pulled from $0$ to $x$, the force $-kx$ grows linearly, and the
integral gives $-\tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$. The average force over that stretch is half
the maximum, which is the same result read off the triangle under a force
versus position graph. That graph reading is worth practicing: **area under an
$F$ vs. $x$ curve is work**, with area below the axis counting negative.

## Negative work is a real answer

Friction on a sliding block points opposite the motion, so $\cos\theta = -1$ and
the work is $-f d$. Nothing has gone wrong. Negative work means energy left the
object's kinetic account. A crate lowered slowly by a rope receives negative
work from the tension and positive work from gravity, and the two cancel because
the speed never changes.

## Net work equals the change in kinetic energy

Add up the work from every force acting, and you get one clean statement:

$$W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}mv_i^2$$

This is the work-energy theorem, and it follows from $F = ma$ by integrating
over displacement rather than time. Its value is that it never mentions time.
A block slides $2\text{ m}$ down a ramp of any shape with friction coefficient
$\mu$; ask for the final speed and the theorem answers directly, while $F = ma$
would demand you track the changing direction of motion.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Do not compute $W_{\text{net}}$ from the net force and then add individual
  works on top of it. Pick one route: either find the vector sum of forces and
  dot it with the displacement, or work out each force's contribution
  separately and add the numbers. Doing both double counts.
</Aside>

## What to do with it

Reach for the theorem whenever a problem gives you speeds and distances but no
times, and whenever the force varies in a way that makes acceleration awkward.
Reach for $F = ma$ instead when you need to know *when* something happens, or
when you need the force itself as an answer.
