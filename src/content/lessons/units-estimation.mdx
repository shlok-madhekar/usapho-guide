## Every equation is a sentence about units

An equation says two quantities are the same thing. Two things cannot be the
same if one is a length and the other is a time. So both sides of any correct
physics equation carry identical dimensions, and so does every term in a sum.

Write dimensions in terms of mass $M$, length $L$, and time $T$. Then a force
is $MLT^{-2}$, energy is $ML^2T^{-2}$, pressure is $ML^{-1}T^{-2}$. Arguments
of $\sin$, $\exp$, and $\log$ must be dimensionless, always.

This is a free error check. After deriving a period of oscillation, put the
units in: $\sqrt{L/g}$ has units $\sqrt{\text{m}/(\text{m/s}^2)} = \text{s}$,
which is a time, so the expression survives. If you had written $\sqrt{g/L}$
you would get $1/\text{s}$ and know instantly that the fraction is upside down.

Dimensional analysis also builds answers, not just checks them. A pendulum's
period can only depend on $m$, $L$, and $g$. The only combination of those
with units of time is $\sqrt{L/g}$, so $T = C\sqrt{L/g}$ for some pure number
$C$. Dimensions cannot tell you $C = 2\pi$, but they told you the mass drops
out and the period goes as the square root of length — most of the physics,
for none of the work.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Dimensional analysis is blind to dimensionless factors, and that includes
  ratios built from the problem itself. If a problem has two lengths, $L_1$
  and $L_2$, then $\sqrt{L_1/g}$ and $\sqrt{L_1/g}\cdot f(L_1/L_2)$ have the
  same units for any function $f$. Use the method to constrain and to check;
  do not use it to claim a unique answer when more than one length is in play.
</Aside>

## Order of magnitude

A Fermi estimate is a chain of factors you can each guess within a factor of a
few. The errors are independent and partly cancel, so the product is usually
good to within a factor of ten — enough to decide whether an effect matters.

Estimate the number of air molecules in a room. A room is roughly
$5 \times 5 \times 3 \approx 10^2\ \text{m}^3$. A mole of gas occupies about
$0.022\ \text{m}^3$, so there are about $10^{3.7}$ moles, or roughly
$10^{27}$ molecules. No step needed a calculator.

The habit worth building: before solving, guess the answer's magnitude. Then
when the algebra produces a car accelerating at $10^4\ \text{m/s}^2$ or an
electron moving at $10^9\ \text{m/s}$, you notice.

## Useful anchors

Carry a few numbers in your head so estimates have somewhere to start:
$g \approx 10\ \text{m/s}^2$, air density $\approx 1.2\ \text{kg/m}^3$, water
density $10^3\ \text{kg/m}^3$, atmospheric pressure $10^5\ \text{Pa}$, speed
of sound $340\ \text{m/s}$, Earth's radius $6.4 \times 10^6\ \text{m}$.

On a graded exam, an answer with the wrong units earns nothing. Check them
last, every time; it costs ten seconds.
