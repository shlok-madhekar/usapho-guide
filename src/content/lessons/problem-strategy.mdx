## Decide what you are looking at

Before any equation, draw the situation and put a boundary around the thing
you are analysing. Everything inside is the system; everything outside acts on
it through external forces. That single choice determines which forces appear
and which conservation laws apply.

Choosing well saves enormous work. For two blocks connected by a string,
treating them as one system makes the tension internal and it drops out
entirely — one equation instead of three. If the tension is what you want,
cut the system at the string and analyse one block.

Then choose axes. Axes are free, so pick the ones that make components
trivial. On an incline, tilt the axes to lie along and perpendicular to the
slope: the acceleration is then entirely along one axis, and only gravity
needs decomposing. In circular motion, use radial and tangential directions.
The physics does not change, but the algebra can go from a page to a line.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Free-body diagrams are not a formality. Draw one per body, mark only real
  forces (something must be touching it, or it is gravity), and label them
  before writing a single equation. Most sign errors happen because the
  diagram was skipped.
</Aside>

## Pick the right tool

Newton's laws give you the full time evolution, but they cost the most work.
When the question asks only about a before and an after, a conservation law is
almost always faster.

Energy conservation, when no friction or other dissipative force acts.
Momentum conservation, when no net external force acts during the interval —
true during a collision, even when gravity acts, because the collision is
brief. Angular momentum conservation, when there is no external torque about
your chosen axis. Notice these have different conditions; a collision can
conserve momentum while destroying energy.

Symmetry cuts work before it starts. If the setup is unchanged by rotating it,
the answer cannot depend on the angle. If two objects are interchangeable, the
answer must be symmetric under swapping their labels. Gauss's law is nothing
but this argument used aggressively.

## Check before you commit

An answer is a formula, and a formula makes predictions you can test cheaply.

Check dimensions first. Then push variables to extremes and see whether the
result stays sensible. Take the Atwood machine, $a = (m_1 - m_2)g/(m_1 + m_2)$.
Set $m_1 = m_2$ and the acceleration vanishes, correct. Let $m_2 \to 0$ and
$a \to g$, a free fall, correct. Let $m_2 \to \infty$ and $a \to -g$, also
correct. Three independent checks in ten seconds.

Finally, ask whether the number is physically reasonable. A block sliding at
$300\ \text{m/s}$ after a gentle push, an efficiency above 1, a negative
kinetic energy — each says go back and look.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Applying energy conservation across an inelastic collision is the most
  common error in olympiad mechanics. Momentum is conserved there; kinetic
  energy is not. Before writing any conservation equation, state which
  quantity is conserved and why the condition holds over your interval.
</Aside>
