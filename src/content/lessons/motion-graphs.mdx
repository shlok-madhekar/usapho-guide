## Slopes and areas

A motion graph carries the same information as an equation, but it puts the
calculus where you can see it. Position, velocity, and acceleration form a
chain: each one is the slope of the graph before it, and the area under each
one is the change in the graph before it.

$$v = \frac{dx}{dt}, \qquad a = \frac{dv}{dt}, \qquad \Delta x = \int v\,dt, \qquad \Delta v = \int a\,dt$$

So reading a graph is two questions. How steep is it here? And how much area
has piled up so far?

A car at rest that accelerates uniformly gives a straight $v$-$t$ line rising
from the origin. Its slope is the constant acceleration. The area under it up
to time $t$ is a triangle of area $\tfrac{1}{2}(t)(at) = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$,
which is exactly the distance travelled. The kinematic formula and the triangle
are the same statement.

## Signed area, and what it costs you

Area below the time axis counts as negative. On a $v$-$t$ graph, a chunk below
the axis means motion backward, and it subtracts from displacement.

That is why displacement and distance travelled come apart. Displacement is the
signed area. Distance is the area with every piece counted positive. A ball
thrown up and caught again has zero displacement and a very nonzero distance.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  A common misread is treating height on one graph as slope on the next. On an
  $x$-$t$ graph, a high point does not mean fast, it means far. An object at
  the top of its arc has maximum $x$ and zero $v$. Before answering, decide
  whether the question is about height, slope, or area.
</Aside>

## Sketching in the other direction

Going from $a$ to $v$ to $x$ needs one extra thing the graph cannot give you:
the starting value. Area only tells you the change.

Sketch by segments. Constant $a$ makes $v$ linear and $x$ quadratic. Zero $a$
makes $v$ flat and $x$ linear. Where $a$ jumps, $v$ has a kink but no jump, and
$x$ stays smooth. Each level of integration is one degree smoother than the
last, so a discontinuous acceleration never produces a broken position curve.
