## An insulator that fights back

A dielectric has no free charges, but its molecules polarize: each stretches
or rotates slightly, and the aligned dipoles leave a layer of bound charge on
each face. That bound charge produces a field opposing the applied one, so the
net field inside is weaker by a factor $\kappa$, the dielectric constant:

$$E = \frac{E_0}{\kappa}$$

Nothing conducts, so the cancellation is only partial — $\kappa$ is finite,
typically 2 to 10, and $\kappa \to \infty$ is the conductor limit.

## What it does to a capacitor

Weaker field at the same charge means a smaller voltage across the gap, so

$$C = \kappa C_0 = \frac{\kappa\varepsilon_0 A}{d}$$

Which quantity is held fixed decides everything else, so name it first. With
the battery still connected, $V$ is fixed and $Q$ rises to $\kappa Q_0$. With
the battery removed, $Q$ is fixed and $V$ falls to $V_0/\kappa$. The two cases
have opposite energy answers, which is the classic place to lose a problem.

Dielectrics also let a capacitor survive higher voltages before the material
ionizes and conducts, which is why real capacitors are built with them rather
than air.

## Energy lives in the field

Charging a capacitor means moving charge against a growing potential
difference. Integrating $dW = (q/C)\,dq$ gives

$$U = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \frac{1}{2}CV^2$$

Substitute the parallel-plate values and something more general appears. The
energy divided by the volume $Ad$ is

$$u = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2$$

with $\varepsilon_0 \to \kappa\varepsilon_0$ in a dielectric. This holds for
any electrostatic field, not just the one between plates — the energy is
stored in the field itself, at a density set by the local $E$. That is the
picture that survives into radiation, where the field carries energy away with
no charges anywhere near.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  A slab pulled partway out of a charged capacitor is a favorite problem.
  Compute the force from $F = -dU/dx$ at fixed $Q$, or from $F = +dU/dx$ at
  fixed $V$ — the battery does work in the second case, and the sign flip is
  entirely that. The slab is pulled *in* either way.
</Aside>
