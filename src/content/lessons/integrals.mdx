## Adding up little pieces

Over a stretch of time so short that the velocity cannot change, the
displacement is just $v\,\Delta t$. Chop a whole journey into such slivers,
add them, and shrink the slivers to zero width. That sum is the integral.

$$x(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t v(t')\,dt'$$

Because each term is a height times a width, the integral of $v(t)$ is the
area under the velocity-time graph. Area below the axis counts as negative,
which is what makes the result a displacement rather than a distance.

Integration undoes differentiation, so it runs the chain of kinematics
backwards: from $a$ to $v$ to $x$. Starting from constant $a$,

$$v = v_0 + \int_0^t a\,dt' = v_0 + at, \qquad x = x_0 + \int_0^t (v_0 + at')\,dt' = x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$$

Each integration introduces one unknown constant, fixed by an initial
condition. Two integrations, two conditions: you need both the starting
position and the starting velocity to predict a trajectory. That is why
Newton's second law needs initial data before it predicts anything.

## The rules worth knowing

$$\int t^n dt = \frac{t^{n+1}}{n+1}\ (n \neq -1), \qquad \int \frac{dt}{t} = \ln|t|, \qquad \int e^{kt} dt = \frac{e^{kt}}{k}$$

Two techniques cover most of what mechanics asks for. Substitution is the
chain rule read backwards: set $u = g(t)$ and replace $g'(t)\,dt$ with $du$.
Integration by parts, $\int f g' = fg - \int f' g$, handles products like
$\int t e^{-t}dt$.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  When the integrand is a function of position rather than time, change the
  variable of integration. The identity $a = \dfrac{dv}{dt} = v\dfrac{dv}{dx}$
  turns a time integral into a position integral, and integrating
  $\int v\,dv = \int a\,dx$ gives $\tfrac{1}{2}v^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}v_0^2 = \int a\,dx$
  — the work-energy theorem, derived in one line.
</Aside>

## Work, and why integrals appear everywhere

A constant force over a displacement does work $Fd$. A varying force does the
same thing sliver by sliver:

$$W = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} F(x)\,dx$$

For a spring, $F = -kx$, so stretching from 0 to $x$ costs
$\int_0^x kx'\,dx' = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$. That is where the spring potential
energy formula comes from — not a memorized constant, but an area under a
straight line.

The same pattern recurs constantly: impulse is $\int F\,dt$, charge is
$\int I\,dt$, the mass of a non-uniform rod is $\int \lambda\,dx$. Whenever a
quantity is a product but one factor varies, the answer is an integral.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Do not integrate a formula outside the region where it holds. Splitting the
  domain is usually the fix: for a rope hanging partly off a table, the force
  law changes at the table edge, so integrate the two stretches separately and
  add. Blindly applying one expression across the whole range is the most
  common source of a wrong factor.
</Aside>
