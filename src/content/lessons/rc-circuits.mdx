## Why the response is exponential

Put a resistor and a capacitor in series with a battery. The loop rule gives

$$\mathcal{E} = IR + \frac{Q}{C}$$

Substituting $I = dQ/dt$ turns this into a differential equation whose rate of
change is proportional to how far the system still has to go. That is the
signature of exponential relaxation, and it is why every RC quantity is an
exponential in $t/RC$.

Charging from empty:

$$Q(t) = C\mathcal{E}\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right), \qquad I(t) = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R}e^{-t/RC}$$

Discharging a capacitor through a resistor:

$$Q(t) = Q_0 e^{-t/RC}$$

Notice the current decays the same way in both cases. Only the charge differs,
because charging approaches a nonzero final value and discharging approaches
zero.

## The time constant

The product $\tau = RC$ has units of seconds and sets the only timescale in the
problem. After one $\tau$ the gap to the final value has shrunk to $1/e$, about
37% of what it was; after three, to 5%; after five, to under 1%.

Larger $R$ means a smaller current for the same voltage, so charge accumulates
slowly. Larger $C$ means more charge is needed to reach the same voltage. Both
push $\tau$ up, which is why they multiply rather than divide.

## The two limits do most of the work

Almost every transient question at the olympiad level is answered without
touching an exponential, by sketching two circuits.

**At $t = 0^+$**, a capacitor that held no charge holds no voltage, so it acts
like a plain wire. Replace it with a short and solve the resistive circuit that
remains.

**As $t \to \infty$**, nothing is changing, so no current flows into the
capacitor and it acts like a break. Replace it with a gap and solve again.

Anything in between interpolates exponentially between those two answers with
the time constant set by the resistance the capacitor *sees*.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  In a network with several resistors, $\tau$ is not $RC$ for whichever
  resistor sits next to the capacitor. Kill the sources — short the batteries,
  open the current sources — and find the Thevenin resistance looking back into
  the circuit from the capacitor's two terminals. That is the $R$ in $\tau$. A
  resistor sitting directly across the capacitor changes the time constant even
  though it carries no steady-state charging current.
</Aside>
