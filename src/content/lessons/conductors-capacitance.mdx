Hold a charged rod near a metal doorknob and the knob pulls back, even though
the knob is neutral. Nothing was added to it. The charge that was already
inside it moved, and the moving is the whole story. A conductor is a material
with charges free to wander, and that one property is enough to fix where
every last bit of charge ends up — no force balance, no integration, just one
condition applied over and over.

The last lesson gave you potential: a scalar $V$ you can add up from charges
without tracking directions, with $V = kq/r$ for a point charge and
$\Delta V = -\int \vec E \cdot d\vec s$ between two places. That scalar is
what makes conductors easy. This lesson turns it into a machine for handling
metal, and then into the single number — capacitance — that summarises how
much charge a pair of conductors will hold.

## Why a conductor is one number

Suppose the field inside a lump of metal were not zero somewhere. The free
electrons there would feel a force, they would move, and they would keep
moving until they had piled up somewhere that cancelled the field. So once
things settle — electrostatics means settled — the field inside the metal is
zero. Not small. Zero.

Three things follow, and you should be able to get all three back from that
one sentence.

First, there is no net charge anywhere inside the metal. Draw any closed
surface entirely inside the conductor. The field is zero on it, so the flux is
zero, so by Gauss's law the charge enclosed is zero. Any excess charge has to
live on a surface.

Second, the whole conductor sits at a single potential. Take any two points
$a$ and $b$ in the metal. Then
$V_b - V_a = -\int_a^b \vec E \cdot d\vec s = 0$, because the integrand is
zero the whole way. A conductor is an *equipotential*: one number describes
its surface, its interior, and any face of it you cannot see.

Third, just outside the surface the field is perpendicular to it. If it had a
component along the surface, charges would slide along the surface, and things
would not be settled. To get its size, put a tiny pillbox across a patch of
the surface with area $\Delta A$, one face just outside and one just inside.
The inside face contributes nothing, the sides contribute nothing because $E$
is perpendicular to the surface, and the outside face contributes
$E\,\Delta A$. Gauss's law says that equals $\sigma \Delta A/\varepsilon_0$,
where $\sigma$ is the local surface charge per unit area. So

$$E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}$$

That is twice the field of an isolated infinite sheet, $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$,
and the factor of two catches people. The sheet sends half its flux each way.
A conductor surface sends all of it outward, because there is nothing on the
metal side to receive it.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  $\sigma$ here is the *local* density. It is not uniform unless the geometry
  is. On an isolated conductor the charge crowds where the surface curves
  sharply, which is why the field is strongest at points and corners, and why
  lightning rods are pointed.
</Aside>

## Induced charge is bookkeeping with two rules

Now put a charge near metal, or inside it, and ask where the induced charge
goes. You never need a new idea. You need the same two rules every time:

1. A Gaussian surface drawn *inside the metal* encloses zero net charge.
2. Charge is conserved on each isolated conductor.

Take a hollow conducting shell with a point charge $+q$ floating in the
cavity, the shell itself carrying net charge $Q$. Draw a sphere through the
middle of the metal. It encloses the cavity charge plus whatever sits on the
inner wall, and that total must be zero, so the inner wall carries $-q$. Rule
2 then says the shell's own $Q$ has to be accounted for: if $-q$ is on the
inside, the outer surface must carry $Q + q$.

The consequence is worth stating out loud. Outside the shell, the field is
exactly the field of a charge $Q+q$ sitting at the centre — regardless of
where in the cavity the charge actually is, and regardless of the cavity's
shape. The shell hides the details. That is electrostatic shielding.

Drag the cavity charge and the shell charge and watch the two surfaces rearrange. Notice that the number of field lines outside depends only on the sum.</>}>
  <Sim id="conductors-capacitance-shell" />
</Figure>

Grounding changes rule 2, not rule 1. "Grounded" means connected to a huge
reservoir of charge held at $V=0$; charge flows in or out freely to keep the
conductor at zero potential. For the shell above, grounding the outside drains
the $Q+q$ away, the outer surface ends up bare, and the field outside
vanishes entirely. The inner wall still holds $-q$, because rule 1 never
changed.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Grounded does not mean uncharged. It means $V=0$ with charge free to arrive.
  A grounded plate near a positive charge carries a large *negative* induced
  charge. If you set the charge on a grounded conductor to zero, you have
  thrown away the whole problem.
</Aside>

## Equal potential, unequal charge

Two conductors joined by a wire become one conductor, so they must end up at
the same potential. That is usually all the physics a "connect them and see"
problem contains.

For a sphere of radius $R$ carrying $Q$, far from everything, the potential of
the surface is $V = kQ/R$. Join two such spheres, radii $R_1$ and $R_2$, far
enough apart that neither disturbs the other's field. Setting the potentials
equal gives $kQ_1/R_1 = kQ_2/R_2$, so

$$\frac{Q_1}{Q_2} = \frac{R_1}{R_2}$$

Charge splits in proportion to radius. But look at the surface fields. Using
$E = kQ/R^2$ and $Q \propto R$, you get $E \propto 1/R$: the *small* sphere has
the *stronger* field at its surface, even though it holds less charge. That is
the lightning-rod result again, in a form you can compute.

Try it with numbers. Two spheres, $2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $8.0\,\mathrm{cm}$,
joined by a long wire, total charge $12.0\,\mathrm{nC}$. The split is
$2:8$, so $Q_1 = 12.0 \times 2/10 = 2.4\,\mathrm{nC}$ on the small one. Its
surface field is

$$E = \frac{kQ_1}{R_1^2} = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(2.4\times10^{-9})}{(0.020)^2} = \frac{21.6}{4.0\times10^{-4}} = 5.4\times10^{4}\,\mathrm{V/m}$$

The big sphere holds four times the charge and has a field four times weaker.

## Capacitance

Put $+Q$ on one conductor and $-Q$ on a second nearby one. Double both charges
and every field doubles, so every potential difference doubles. The ratio
$Q/\Delta V$ therefore does not depend on how much charge you put on. It
depends only on the shapes and their spacing. Give the ratio a name:

$$C = \frac{Q}{V}$$

where $V$ means the potential difference between the two conductors and $Q$ is
the magnitude of the charge on either one. The unit is the farad, one coulomb
per volt, which is enormous; real capacitors run from picofarads
($10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F}$) to millifarads.

**Parallel plates.** Area $A$, gap $d$, and $d$ small enough that you can
ignore the fringing at the edges. Each plate has $\sigma = Q/A$. Between them
the two sheets' fields add to $E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0 = Q/\varepsilon_0 A$,
uniform. A uniform field means $V = Ed$, so $V = Qd/\varepsilon_0 A$ and

$$C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}$$

Bigger plates hold more charge at the same voltage. A bigger gap means a
longer climb against the same field, so a bigger voltage for the same charge,
so less capacitance.

**A sphere alone.** One isolated sphere of radius $R$ is a capacitor too, with
its partner infinitely far away at $V=0$. Then $V = kQ/R$, so
$C = Q/V = R/k$. For a $0.15\,\mathrm{m}$ dome,
$C = 0.15/(8.99\times10^{9}) = 1.67\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{F} = 16.7\,\mathrm{pF}$.

**Concentric spheres.** Inner ball radius $a$ with charge $+Q$, outer shell of
inner radius $b$ with $-Q$. Between them Gauss's law gives $E = kQ/r^2$, so

$$V = \int_a^b \frac{kQ}{r^2}\,dr = kQ\left(\frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{b}\right) = kQ\,\frac{b-a}{ab}$$

and therefore $C = ab/[k(b-a)]$.

**Coaxial cable.** Inner radius $a$, outer $b$, length $L$, charge per length
$\lambda$. Gauss's law on a cylinder gives $E = \lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0 r$,
so $V = (\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln(b/a)$ and the capacitance per unit
length is $2\pi\varepsilon_0/\ln(b/a)$. For $a = 0.90\,\mathrm{mm}$ and
$b = 3.00\,\mathrm{mm}$: $\ln(3.33) = 1.204$, so
$C/L = (5.563\times10^{-11})/1.204 = 4.62\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{F/m}$, or
$46.2\,\mathrm{pF/m}$.

Every one of these followed the same three steps: assume $\pm Q$, find $E$
from Gauss's law, integrate $E$ to get $V$, divide. When you meet a shape you
have not seen, run those steps rather than hunting for the formula.

## Series, parallel, and a ladder

**Parallel.** Both capacitors span the same two nodes, so both have the same
$V$. Charges add: $Q = C_1V + C_2V$, so $C_\text{eq} = C_1 + C_2$. A
$4.7\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ and a $2.2\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ in parallel give
$6.9\,\mu\mathrm{F}$.

**Series.** Here is the part worth understanding rather than memorising. The
stretch of conductor between two capacitors in a chain is isolated: it started
neutral and nothing can reach it. So if $+Q$ arrives on the left plate of the
first capacitor, $-Q$ appears on its right plate, and the same $+Q$ must sit
on the next plate along to keep that isolated island neutral. **Every
capacitor in a series chain carries the same charge.** The voltages add:

$$V = \frac{Q}{C_1} + \frac{Q}{C_2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{1}{C_\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2}$$

Those same two capacitors in series give
$1/C = 1/4.7 + 1/2.2 = 0.2128 + 0.4545 = 0.6673$, so
$C_\text{eq} = 1.50\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ — less than either one.

Because the charge is shared, the voltage across a series member is
$V_i = Q/C_i$: the *smallest* capacitor takes the *largest* share. For
$2.0$, $3.0$ and $6.0\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ across $12.0\,\mathrm{V}$:
$1/C = 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/6 = 1$, so $C_\text{eq} = 1.0\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ and
$Q = (1.0\,\mu\mathrm{F})(12.0\,\mathrm{V}) = 12.0\,\mu\mathrm{C}$. The
$2.0\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ capacitor then carries
$V = 12.0/2.0 = 6.0\,\mathrm{V}$, half the battery on its own.

**The ladder trick.** An infinite ladder — a series capacitor $C$ along the
top rail, then a shunt capacitor $C$ down to the bottom rail, repeating
forever — looks the same from node one as from node two. So if the input
capacitance is $X$, then chopping off the first cell leaves a ladder whose
input capacitance is also $X$. Reading from the input: $C$ in series with
(the shunt $C$ in parallel with $X$). That gives

$$X = \frac{C(C+X)}{C + (C + X)} = \frac{C(C+X)}{2C+X}$$

Cross-multiplying, $X(2C+X) = C^2 + CX$, so $X^2 + CX - C^2 = 0$ and
$X = C(\sqrt{5}-1)/2 = 0.618\,C$. For $1.00\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ cells, the input
capacitance is $0.618\,\mu\mathrm{F}$. Self-similarity turns an infinite
circuit into a quadratic.

## Energy, and the pull between the plates

Charging a capacitor means carrying charge from one plate to the other against
the growing voltage. When the charge so far is $q$, the voltage is $q/C$, and
moving the next $dq$ costs $dW = (q/C)\,dq$. Integrate from $0$ to $Q$:

$$U = \int_0^Q \frac{q}{C}\,dq = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \tfrac12 CV^2 = \tfrac12 QV$$

The factor of $\tfrac12$ is there because the early charge moved cheaply. For
a $470\,\mu\mathrm{F}$ flash capacitor at $320\,\mathrm{V}$:
$Q = (4.70\times10^{-4})(320) = 0.150\,\mathrm{C}$ and
$U = \tfrac12 (0.150)(320) = 24.1\,\mathrm{J}$.

That energy is sitting in the field itself. For parallel plates,
$U = \tfrac12 (\varepsilon_0 A/d)(Ed)^2 = \tfrac12 \varepsilon_0 E^2 (Ad)$,
and $Ad$ is the volume between the plates. So the energy per unit volume is

$$u = \tfrac12 \varepsilon_0 E^2$$

This holds for any electrostatic field, not just this one. At the breakdown
field of dry air, $3.0\times10^{6}\,\mathrm{V/m}$,
$u = \tfrac12 (8.85\times10^{-12})(9.0\times10^{12}) = 39.8\,\mathrm{J/m^3}$.
That is the ceiling on how much energy an air-gap capacitor can store per
cubic metre, and it is why capacitors lose to batteries for storing energy.

Now the force. Opposite plates attract, but do not write $F = QE$ with
$E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$ — that counts the plate's own field pushing on
itself. The field one plate makes at the other's location is only
$\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$. So

$$F = Q\,\frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} = \frac{Q^2}{2\varepsilon_0 A}$$

Check it with energy: pulling the isolated plates apart by $dx$ changes
$U = Q^2 d/(2\varepsilon_0 A)$ by $Q^2 dx/(2\varepsilon_0 A)$, and that work
came from you, so $F$ is exactly that over $dx$. It agrees.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Forgetting the $\tfrac12$ in the plate force is the single most common error
  in this topic. A charged surface never feels its own field. Use the field
  produced by *everything else*, which for a plate pair is half the total gap
  field.
</Aside>

**Worked example: force with the battery still connected.** Plates of area
$0.020\,\mathrm{m^2}$, gap $1.00\,\mathrm{mm}$, held at $24.0\,\mathrm{V}$ by
a battery that stays attached. Find the attraction.

The first thing to write on blank paper is *what is held fixed*. The battery
fixes $V$, not $Q$. So compute $Q$ from $V$ and then use the force formula.

$$C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d} = \frac{(8.85\times10^{-12})(0.020)}{1.00\times10^{-3}} = 1.77\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{F}$$

$$Q = CV = (1.77\times10^{-10})(24.0) = 4.248\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C}$$

$$F = \frac{Q^2}{2\varepsilon_0 A} = \frac{(4.248\times10^{-9})^2}{2(8.85\times10^{-12})(0.020)} = \frac{1.805\times10^{-17}}{3.54\times10^{-13}} = 5.10\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{N}$$

That is $51.0\,\mu\mathrm{N}$. Note what you must *not* do: with the battery
attached, $dU/dx$ is not the force, because the battery is pumping energy in
as well. Going through $Q$ avoids the trap entirely.

**Worked example: three shells in series.** Concentric spheres of radii
$2.00$, $3.00$ and $6.00\,\mathrm{cm}$. The inner and outer are wired
together; the middle one is the other terminal. Find the capacitance between
the terminals.

Wiring the inner and outer together makes them one conductor at one potential.
So there are two gaps, and both run from that conductor to the middle sphere —
same pair of potentials across each. Same voltage means *parallel*, despite
the word "series" in the phrasing. Use $C = ab/[k(b-a)]$ on each gap:

$$C_{12} = \frac{(0.0200)(0.0300)}{(8.99\times10^{9})(0.0100)} = \frac{6.00\times10^{-4}}{8.99\times10^{7}} = 6.67\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F}$$

$$C_{23} = \frac{(0.0300)(0.0600)}{(8.99\times10^{9})(0.0300)} = \frac{1.80\times10^{-3}}{2.697\times10^{8}} = 6.67\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F}$$

Adding, $C = 13.3\,\mathrm{pF}$. The lesson is to decide series or parallel
from which quantity the two elements share, never from the picture.

## Images: replacing a conductor with a fake charge

A point charge $+q$ a height $h$ above a large grounded plate is a genuinely
hard problem, because the induced surface charge is spread out in a way you do
not know in advance. Here is the trick that dissolves it.

Forget the plate. Put $-q$ at depth $h$ *below* where the plate was. The plane
halfway between two equal and opposite charges is at $V=0$ everywhere, by
symmetry. So this two-charge arrangement satisfies exactly the same boundary
condition as the real problem: $V=0$ on the plane, one charge $+q$ above it.
The uniqueness theorem for electrostatics says a solution matching the
boundary values and the sources is *the* solution. So in the region above the
plate, the field of the charge plus its **image** is the true field.

The force is then just Coulomb's law between $q$ and $-q$ a distance $2h$
apart:

$$F = \frac{kq^2}{(2h)^2} = \frac{kq^2}{4h^2}$$

directed toward the plate. Energy takes one more step. You cannot write
$U = -kq^2/2h$, because as you move the real charge the image moves too, and
only half the usual work is done. Integrate the force instead:

$$W = \int_h^\infty \frac{kq^2}{4x^2}\,dx = \frac{kq^2}{4h}$$

For $q = 3.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ at $h = 2.0\,\mathrm{cm}$:

$$W = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(3.0\times10^{-9})^2}{4(0.020)} = \frac{8.09\times10^{-8}}{0.080} = 1.01\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{J}$$

The same trick works for a grounded *sphere* of radius $R$ with a charge $q$ a
distance $d$ from its centre, though the image is no longer a mirror copy.
Placing $q' = -qR/d$ at a distance $b = R^2/d$ from the centre, on the line
joining them, makes the whole sphere an equipotential at zero. Take it as
given; the check is a page of algebra.

**Worked example.** $q = 5.0\,\mathrm{nC}$ at $d = 8.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ from the
centre of a grounded sphere of radius $R = 3.0\,\mathrm{cm}$. Find the
attraction.

$$q' = -\frac{qR}{d} = -\frac{(5.0)(3.0)}{8.0} = -1.875\,\mathrm{nC}, \qquad b = \frac{R^2}{d} = \frac{9.0}{8.0} = 1.125\,\mathrm{cm}$$

The separation between the real charge and the image is
$8.000 - 1.125 = 6.875\,\mathrm{cm}$. So

$$F = \frac{k|qq'|}{(0.06875)^2} = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(5.0\times10^{-9})(1.875\times10^{-9})}{4.727\times10^{-3}} = \frac{8.43\times10^{-8}}{4.727\times10^{-3}} = 1.78\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{N}$$

The formula does not care whether the charge is outside the sphere or inside a
grounded cavity of radius $R$; with $d < R$ the image simply lands outside,
at $b = R^2/d > R$, and the force still pulls the charge toward the nearest
wall.

## How to tell which tool a problem wants

The signals are unusually clean here.

*The word "conductor", "metal" or "grounded" with charges near it.* Reach for
the two bookkeeping rules. Draw a Gaussian surface inside the metal, set the
enclosed charge to zero, then conserve charge — unless it is grounded, in
which case drop conservation and impose $V=0$ instead.

*"Connected by a wire", "touched and separated", "joined".* Equal potentials.
For spheres that means charge in proportion to radius.

*Two conductors, $\pm Q$, and a question about $Q$, $V$, or stored energy.*
Capacitance. If the shape is not on your list, run the four steps: assume
$\pm Q$, Gauss for $E$, integrate for $V$, divide.

*"Stays connected to the battery" versus "disconnected".* This single phrase
decides which quantity is fixed. Battery attached, $V$ is constant. Battery
removed, $Q$ is constant. Almost every capacitor trick question turns on it.

*A single point charge near a large grounded conductor of simple shape.*
Images. Plane gives a mirror charge at equal depth; sphere gives $-qR/d$ at
$R^2/d$. Then it is Coulomb's law, and if the question asks for work, you
integrate the force rather than using $qV$.

*An infinite repeating network.* Self-similarity: call the answer $X$, note
that the network beyond the first cell is also $X$, and solve the quadratic.

One thing this lesson kept quiet about: everything assumed vacuum between the
conductors. Real capacitors are packed with plastic, oxide or oil, which
changes the numbers substantially and for a reason worth understanding. That
is [dielectrics and field energy](/guide/dielectrics).
