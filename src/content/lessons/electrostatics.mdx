## Gauss's law is a symmetry weapon

The flux of the electric field through any closed surface counts the enclosed
charge:

$$\oint \vec E \cdot d\vec A = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}$$

It is always true but only *useful* when symmetry lets you pull $E$ out of
the integral. Three symmetries, three Gaussian surfaces: spherical means a
concentric sphere, cylindrical means a coaxial can, planar means a pillbox.

- Point / sphere: $E = \frac{kQ}{r^2}$ outside; inside a uniform sphere, $E \propto r$
- Infinite line: $E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$
- Infinite sheet: $E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$, independent of distance

## Conductors

In electrostatic equilibrium, $\vec E = 0$ inside a conductor, all excess
charge sits on the surface, and the surface is an equipotential. Cavity
problems, where a charge sits inside a hollow conductor, are USAPhO staples:
the induced surface charges shield the outside world from everything except
the total enclosed charge.

<Callout label="Olympiad trap" warn>
  Gauss's law gives the field of *all* charges, using only the *enclosed*
  charge. These are different roles. When symmetry is broken (say, an
  off-center cavity), superposition, not Gauss alone, finishes the job.
</Callout>

## Solve it here

<Problem
  id="np-es-1"
  number={1}
  title="Inside the sphere"
  difficulty="Hard"
  answer={450}
  unit="N/C"
>
  A solid insulating sphere of radius $R = 0.2$ m carries charge distributed
  uniformly through its volume. The field at its surface is 900 N/C. What is
  the field magnitude, in N/C, at $r = 0.1$ m from the center?

  <Hint>
    Inside a uniform sphere, the enclosed charge scales as $r^3$ while the
    Gaussian area scales as $r^2$.
  </Hint>
  <Solution>
    Gauss's law with $Q_{\text{enc}} = Q (r/R)^3$ gives a field growing
    linearly inside:

    $$E(r) = E_{\text{surface}}\,\frac{r}{R} = 900 \times \frac{0.1}{0.2} = 450 \text{ N/C}$$
  </Solution>
</Problem>
