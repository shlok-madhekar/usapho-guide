## Rate of change, made exact

Average velocity over an interval is displacement divided by elapsed time.
Shrink the interval and that ratio settles onto a single number: the velocity
at an instant. That limit is the derivative.

$$v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{x(t + \Delta t) - x(t)}{\Delta t}$$

Acceleration is the same operation applied again, $a = dv/dt = d^2x/dt^2$.
Nothing about kinematics is new here; the derivative is just the honest
version of "slope of the graph at a point."

Geometrically, $v$ is the slope of the $x$ versus $t$ curve. That single fact
carries a lot: where $x(t)$ is flat the object is momentarily at rest, where
$x(t)$ is steep it is moving fast, and where $x(t)$ curves upward the
acceleration is positive.

## The rules you actually use

Four rules cover almost every derivative in mechanics.

$$\frac{d}{dt}t^n = n t^{n-1}, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}(fg) = f'g + fg'$$

$$\frac{d}{dt}\frac{f}{g} = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}f(g(t)) = f'(g)\,g'(t)$$

The last one, the chain rule, is the one that matters most in physics, because
physical quantities are usually functions of functions. The kinetic energy of
a particle is $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ where $v$ depends on time, so its rate of
change is $mv\,\dfrac{dv}{dt} = mva$, which is exactly the power delivered by
the net force.

Also worth memorizing: $\frac{d}{dt}\sin\omega t = \omega\cos\omega t$ and
$\frac{d}{dt}e^{kt} = ke^{kt}$. The second is why exponentials appear in every
damped or decaying system — it is the function whose rate of change is
proportional to itself.

Now differentiate $x(t) = x_0 + v_0 t - \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$ twice. You get
$v = v_0 - gt$ and $a = -g$. The constant-acceleration formulas are not
separate facts; they are one statement viewed at three levels.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Speed is $|v|$, not $v$, and the derivative of speed is not acceleration in
  general. An object in uniform circular motion has constant speed and nonzero
  acceleration. Differentiate the vector, then take the magnitude — never the
  other way around.
</Aside>

## Finding a maximum

At a smooth maximum or minimum the tangent is horizontal, so $df/dx = 0$.
Solve that for the location, then check the second derivative: negative means
a maximum, positive a minimum.

Take projectile range on level ground, $R(\theta) = v_0^2\sin 2\theta / g$.
Setting $dR/d\theta = 2v_0^2\cos 2\theta/g = 0$ gives $2\theta = 90^\circ$, so
$\theta = 45^\circ$. Any olympiad question phrased "for what value of ... is
the ... largest" is asking you to do exactly this.
