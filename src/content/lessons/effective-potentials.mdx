## A graph you can roll a ball down

In the last lesson you tracked energy sloshing between kinetic and potential in
a mass on a spring, and the total stayed fixed. That worked because you already
knew the potential: $U = \tfrac12 kx^2$, a parabola, and a parabola gives simple
harmonic motion.

Most systems are not springs. An atom bound to another atom, a bead threaded on
a bent wire, a marble in a bowl, a satellite in an elliptical orbit — none of
them has an obvious spring in it. And yet all of them oscillate, and all of them
oscillate at a frequency you can write down in about three lines. The reason is
that the potential energy curve, whatever its shape, looks like a parabola if
you get close enough to the bottom of a dip in it.

That is the whole lesson. Draw $U$ against the coordinate, find the dips, and
measure how sharp each dip is. Sharpness gives you the frequency. Everything
else here is bookkeeping about what counts as "the coordinate" and what counts
as "the mass".

## Force is minus the slope

Start with something you already have: for motion along one coordinate $x$ with
no friction, the work done by the force as the particle moves a small distance
$dx$ is $F\,dx$, and that work comes out of the potential energy, so
$F\,dx = -dU$. Divide by $dx$:

$$F(x) = -\frac{dU}{dx}$$

Read this as a picture. Plot $U$ against $x$. Where the curve slopes up to the
right, $dU/dx$ is positive, so $F$ is negative and the particle is pushed left —
downhill. Where the curve slopes down to the right, the particle is pushed
right. Also downhill. A particle on a potential energy graph behaves exactly
like a ball rolling on that graph under gravity. That analogy is not a
coincidence, and it is worth trusting.

Equilibrium means no net force, so it means no slope:

$$\frac{dU}{dx} = 0$$

Flat spots on the graph. Nothing more.

## Stable, unstable, and why the second derivative decides

A flat spot alone does not tell you whether a system will sit there. A marble
balanced on top of a hill has zero net force too. What separates the hilltop
from the valley is the curvature — the second derivative.

Suppose $U'(x_0) = 0$ and you push the particle a small distance $\delta$ to the
right. The slope there is approximately $U'(x_0 + \delta) \approx U''(x_0)\,\delta$,
since the slope starts at zero and changes at rate $U''$. So the force is

$$F \approx -U''(x_0)\,\delta$$

If $U''(x_0) > 0$, the force is opposite to the displacement: push it right and
it pushes back left. It returns. That is **stable equilibrium**, and on the
graph it is a minimum, a valley.

If $U''(x_0) < 0$, the force points the same way as the displacement, the
displacement grows, and the particle runs away. That is **unstable
equilibrium**, a maximum. If $U'' = 0$ as well, the test says nothing and you
have to look at the next non-zero derivative or just sketch the curve.

**Worked example 1.** A particle moves in $U(x) = 2x^3 - 9x^2 + 12x$, with $U$
in joules and $x$ in metres. Find the equilibrium points and say which is
stable.

Differentiate:

$$U'(x) = 6x^2 - 18x + 12 = 6(x^2 - 3x + 2) = 6(x-1)(x-2)$$

So $U' = 0$ at $x = 1$ m and $x = 2$ m. Now the second derivative:

$$U''(x) = 12x - 18$$

At $x = 1$: $U'' = 12 - 18 = -6$ J/m², negative, so that is the hilltop —
unstable. At $x = 2$: $U'' = 24 - 18 = +6$ J/m², positive, so $x = 2$ m is the
stable one. Figure 1 is this exact potential, so you can check the algebra
against the picture.

## The frequency hiding in the curvature

Now the payoff. Near a stable point, write $U$ as a Taylor expansion about
$x_0$ — this is the tool that says any smooth curve equals its value, plus its
slope times the offset, plus half its curvature times the offset squared:

$$U(x) \approx U(x_0) + U'(x_0)(x-x_0) + \tfrac12 U''(x_0)(x-x_0)^2$$

The first term is a constant, and a constant in $U$ changes no force. The second
term is zero, because $x_0$ is an equilibrium. What survives is

$$U(x) \approx U(x_0) + \tfrac12 U''(x_0)\,(x-x_0)^2$$

Compare that to the spring potential $\tfrac12 k x^2$ from the last lesson. It
is the same expression with the displacement measured from $x_0$ and with

$$k_{\text{eff}} = U''(x_0)$$

So the particle does not merely return — it does simple harmonic motion, with
the frequency you already know:

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{U''(x_0)}{m}}$$

and period $T = 2\pi/\omega$. The units check: $U''$ is joules per metre
squared, which is newtons per metre, which is a spring constant. Every stable
equilibrium in physics is a harmonic oscillator if you look closely enough. That
one sentence is why this technique appears everywhere.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Three steps, always the same. Differentiate once and set to zero to find $x_0$.
  Differentiate twice and evaluate at $x_0$ to get $k_{\text{eff}}$. Then
  $\omega = \sqrt{k_{\text{eff}}/m}$. If a problem hands you a potential energy
  function and asks for a frequency, you are done in three lines.
</Aside>

**Worked example 2.** A 0.50 kg particle sits at the stable equilibrium of the
same $U(x) = 2x^3 - 9x^2 + 12x$ and is nudged. Find $\omega$.

From example 1 the stable point is $x_0 = 2$ m and $U''(2) = 6$ N/m. So

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{6}{0.50}} = \sqrt{12} = 3.46\ \text{rad/s}$$

The amplitude never entered. That is the signature of harmonic motion, and it is
only true while the amplitude is small enough that the parabola is a good fit.

## When the coordinate is not a straight line

Often the moving thing is not a particle on the $x$-axis. A bead slides on a
bent wire, a ball rolls in a bowl, a pendulum swings through an angle. The fix
is small: write the kinetic energy in terms of your chosen coordinate $q$, and
whatever multiplies $\tfrac12\dot q^2$ plays the role of the mass. Call it the
effective mass $m_{\text{eff}}$. Then

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{U''(q_0)}{m_{\text{eff}}}}$$

**Worked example 3.** A bead slides without friction on a wire bent into the
parabola $y = cx^2$ in a vertical plane, with $c = 0.80$ m⁻¹. Take
$g = 9.8$ m/s². Find $\omega$ for small oscillations about the bottom.

Potential energy first: $U = mgy = mgcx^2$. Then
$U' = 2mgcx$, zero at $x = 0$, and $U'' = 2mgc$, a constant.

Kinetic energy: the bead moves along the wire, so its speed has both components,
$v^2 = \dot x^2 + \dot y^2$. Since $y = cx^2$, $\dot y = 2cx\dot x$, giving
$v^2 = \dot x^2(1 + 4c^2x^2)$. Near the bottom $x$ is small, so that bracket is
1 and $m_{\text{eff}} = m$. Then

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{2mgc}{m}} = \sqrt{2gc} = \sqrt{2(9.8)(0.80)} = \sqrt{15.68} = 3.96\ \text{rad/s}$$

The mass cancelled, as it must for anything driven purely by gravity.

Now a case where the effective mass does not cancel. A uniform solid ball of
radius $r = 2.0$ cm **rolls without slipping** near the bottom of a spherical
bowl of radius $R = 20.0$ cm. Its centre stays on a circle of radius $R - r$, so
if $\theta$ is the angle of the centre from the bottom,
$U = mg(R-r)(1-\cos\theta)$. For small $\theta$, $1 - \cos\theta \approx
\theta^2/2$, so $U \approx \tfrac12 mg(R-r)\theta^2$ and
$U''(0) = mg(R-r)$.

The kinetic energy has two pieces because the ball spins as it goes. Its centre
moves at $v = (R-r)\dot\theta$, and rolling without slipping means it spins at
$\omega_{\text{spin}} = v/r$. With $I = \tfrac25 mr^2$,

$$K = \tfrac12 mv^2 + \tfrac12\left(\tfrac25 mr^2\right)\frac{v^2}{r^2} = \tfrac{7}{10}mv^2 = \tfrac12\left[\tfrac75 m(R-r)^2\right]\dot\theta^2$$

So $m_{\text{eff}} = \tfrac75 m(R-r)^2$, and

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{mg(R-r)}{\tfrac75 m(R-r)^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{5g}{7(R-r)}} = \sqrt{\frac{5(9.8)}{7(0.180)}} = \sqrt{38.89} = 6.24\ \text{rad/s}$$

A sliding marble in the same bowl would give $\sqrt{g/R}$, faster. The rolling
ball carries extra inertia in its spin, so it is lazier.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Do not mix coordinates. If $U$ is written in terms of the angle $\theta$, then
  $U''$ must be $d^2U/d\theta^2$ and the effective mass must be the thing
  multiplying $\tfrac12\dot\theta^2$ — a moment of inertia, with units of
  kg·m². Differentiating $U(\theta)$ but dividing by a plain mass $m$ gives an
  answer that is wrong by a factor with units of metres, and the error hides
  because nothing in the final number looks obviously broken. Check units at the
  end: $\omega^2$ must come out per second squared.
</Aside>

## Terms that are not really potential energy

The word *effective* in "effective potential" usually means you have folded
something that is not a potential energy into $U$, because it acts like one.

The common case is rotation. A puck on a frictionless table is tethered by a
string through a hole, so it orbits with angular momentum $L$ about the hole,
and $L$ is conserved. Its kinetic energy splits into radial and tangential
parts:

$$K = \tfrac12 m\dot r^2 + \tfrac12 m(r\dot\theta)^2 = \tfrac12 m\dot r^2 + \frac{L^2}{2mr^2}$$

using $L = mr^2\dot\theta$. The second term depends only on $r$, so as far as
the radial motion is concerned it behaves exactly like a potential energy. Move
it across:

$$E = \tfrac12 m\dot r^2 + U_{\text{eff}}(r), \qquad U_{\text{eff}}(r) = U(r) + \frac{L^2}{2mr^2}$$

Now the radial motion is a one-dimensional problem with the machinery you
already have. That extra term is the **centrifugal barrier**. It blows up as
$r \to 0$, which is the statement that a body with angular momentum cannot reach
the centre. A circular orbit is just an equilibrium of $U_{\text{eff}}$, and its
stability and radial frequency come from $U_{\text{eff}}''$.

Rotating frames work the same way. A bead on a wire spun about a vertical axis
at rate $\Omega$ feels a centrifugal term $-\tfrac12 m\Omega^2 x^2$ that you add
to the gravitational $U$ before differentiating. For the parabolic wire
$y = cx^2$ spun at $\Omega$,

$$U_{\text{eff}} = mgcx^2 - \tfrac12 m\Omega^2 x^2, \qquad \omega = \sqrt{2gc - \Omega^2}$$

Spin it fast enough and $2gc - \Omega^2$ goes negative: the bottom stops being a
minimum, and the bead runs outward instead of oscillating.

**Worked example 4.** A spacecraft moves in Earth's field with energy per unit
mass $E = -1.42\times10^{7}$ J/kg and angular momentum per unit mass
$\ell = 6.47\times10^{10}$ m²/s. At a turning point all the radial speed is
gone, so $E = U_{\text{eff}}$:

$$E = \frac{\ell^2}{2r^2} - \frac{GM}{r}$$

Find the closest approach, with $GM = 3.986\times10^{14}$ m³/s².

Multiply through by $r^2$ and collect:

$$Er^2 + GM\,r - \frac{\ell^2}{2} = 0$$

Put the numbers in. $\ell^2 = (6.47\times10^{10})^2 = 4.186\times10^{21}$, so
$\ell^2/2 = 2.093\times10^{21}$. Multiplying by $-1$ to make the leading
coefficient positive:

$$1.42\times10^{7}\,r^2 - 3.986\times10^{14}\,r + 2.093\times10^{21} = 0$$

Quadratic formula. The discriminant is

$$(3.986\times10^{14})^2 - 4(1.42\times10^{7})(2.093\times10^{21}) = 1.589\times10^{29} - 1.189\times10^{29} = 4.00\times10^{28}$$

whose square root is $2.00\times10^{14}$. So

$$r = \frac{3.986\times10^{14} \pm 2.00\times10^{14}}{2(1.42\times10^{7})}$$

The two roots are $r = 2.10\times10^{7}$ m and $r = 6.99\times10^{6}$ m. Two
turning points, because the orbit is bound: the far one is apogee, the near one
is perigee. Closest approach is $6.99\times10^{6}$ m.

There is a useful check built into that answer. Two real roots means bound. One
root, where the discriminant vanishes, means the energy sits exactly at the
minimum of $U_{\text{eff}}$ — a circular orbit. No real roots would mean that
energy is not available at that angular momentum at all.

## How far the parabola can be trusted

The frequency $\sqrt{U''/m}$ is an approximation, good while the displacement is
small enough that the cubic and quartic parts of the Taylor series stay
negligible. Push the amplitude up and the period starts to depend on amplitude.

For $U(x) = \tfrac12 kx^2 + \beta x^4$ the standard first correction is

$$\omega \approx \omega_0\left(1 + \frac{3\beta A^2}{2k}\right)$$

with $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ and $A$ the amplitude. Take $m = 0.20$ kg,
$k = 50$ N/m, $\beta = 4.0\times10^{3}$ J/m⁴ and $A = 3.0$ cm. Then
$\omega_0 = \sqrt{50/0.20} = \sqrt{250} = 15.81$ rad/s, and the correction
factor is

$$1 + \frac{3(4.0\times10^{3})(0.030)^2}{2(50)} = 1 + \frac{10.8}{100} = 1.108$$

so $\omega = 15.81 \times 1.108 = 17.5$ rad/s. A positive $\beta$ makes the well
steeper than a parabola, so the oscillation is faster. That sign is worth
remembering as a sanity check.

One more thing the higher derivatives are good for. The force is $-U'$, so the
force is largest in magnitude where $U'' = 0$ — the inflection point. For the
Lennard-Jones potential

$$U(r) = 4\varepsilon\left[\left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{12} - \left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{6}\right]$$

the minimum sits where $U' = 0$, which gives $r_0 = 2^{1/6}\sigma$, about
$1.122\sigma$; with $\sigma = 0.340$ nm that is 0.382 nm. The inflection, where
the attraction is strongest and beyond which the bond gives way, is at
$r = (26/7)^{1/6}\sigma \approx 1.244\sigma = 0.423$ nm. Same function, three
different questions, three different derivatives.

## Recognising the technique

Reach for this whenever a problem hands you an energy rather than a force. The
tells:

- The problem states $U(x)$, $U(r)$ or $U(\theta)$ explicitly and asks where
  something sits, or how fast it wiggles. Set $U' = 0$, then use $U''$.
- The words **small oscillations**, **slightly displaced**, **nudged**, or
  **near the bottom** appear. That is a request for $\sqrt{U''/m_{\text{eff}}}$
  and nothing else.
- Something rotates at fixed angular momentum, or the frame spins at fixed
  $\Omega$. Build $U_{\text{eff}}$ first, with the $L^2/2mr^2$ or
  $-\tfrac12 m\Omega^2 x^2$ term inside it, then treat $r$ or $x$ as an
  ordinary one-dimensional coordinate.
- You are asked for a **turning point**, a closest approach, or the range of
  motion at a given energy. Set $E = U_{\text{eff}}$ and solve; the number of
  real roots tells you whether the motion is bound.
- The system is not a particle on a line — a rolling ball, a bead on a wire, a
  rigid body on a pivot. Write $K$ in your coordinate, read off $m_{\text{eff}}$,
  and carry on.

The one habit to build is drawing the curve. Sketch $U$, mark the flat spots,
decide which hold water, and only then start differentiating. Half the errors in
this topic are solving correctly for the wrong root.
