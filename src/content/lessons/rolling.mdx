## The constraint

A wheel rolling without slipping lays its rim onto the ground like tape: one
turn advances it exactly one circumference. Differentiate $x = r\theta$ and you
get the constraint that ties the two motions together.

$$v_{cm} = \omega r, \qquad a_{cm} = \alpha r$$

This is a geometric statement about the contact, not a law of nature. It holds
only while the surface grips. Kick a ball hard and initially $v > \omega r$ —
it skids, friction acts until the two agree, and only then does true rolling
begin.

A cleaner way to see it: the contact point is instantaneously at rest. The
whole body is momentarily rotating about that point, so the top of the wheel
moves at $2v$ and the center at $v$. Watch a car's hubcap in a photograph —
the bottom spokes are sharp and the top ones are blurred.

## Two places for the energy

Because the constraint links them, the kinetic energy of a rolling object
splits into a translation and a rotation:

$$K = \tfrac12 mv^2 + \tfrac12 I\omega^2 = \tfrac12 mv^2\left(1 + k\right),
\qquad k = \frac{I}{mr^2}$$

The shape factor $k$ is all that survives: $0.4$ for a solid sphere, $0.5$ for
a disk, $1$ for a hoop. Release from height $h$ and

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1+k}}, \qquad a = \frac{g\sin\theta}{1+k}$$

Neither expression contains $m$ or $r$. A marble and a bowling ball tie; a
sphere always beats a disk, which always beats a hoop, because the hoop must
divert half its energy into spin. A frictionless slider ($k = 0$) beats every
rolling object.

## Friction that costs nothing

Static friction is what supplies the torque that spins the object up, so
rolling is impossible without it. Yet the contact point does not move, and work
is force times displacement *at the point of application* — so static friction
does zero work. Mechanical energy is conserved.

That stops being true the moment the object slips. Then the contact slides,
kinetic friction acts, energy is lost, and $v = \omega r$ no longer applies. On
a steep enough ramp, or with too little $\mu_s$, this is exactly what happens.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Do not assume the friction on a rolling object equals $\mu N$. Static friction
  takes whatever value the constraint demands, up to that maximum. Solve for it
  from $\tau = I\alpha$ together with $a = \alpha r$, then check it is below
  $\mu_s N$; if it is not, the object slips and the whole approach changes.
</Aside>
