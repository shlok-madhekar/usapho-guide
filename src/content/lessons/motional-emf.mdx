## The rod on rails

Faraday's law already tells you that a changing flux drives an emf. Motional
emf is the case where the field is static and the *circuit* is what changes.
Take a conducting rod of length $L$ sliding at speed $v$ along two parallel
rails, in a uniform field $B$ perpendicular to the plane of the circuit. The
enclosed area grows at rate $Lv$, so

$$\mathcal{E} = BLv$$

There is a second route to the same number, and it is worth carrying because it
generalises. Each free charge in the rod moves with the rod, so it feels
$q\vec{v}\times\vec{B}$ pushing it along the rod's length. That force per unit
charge, integrated along the rod, is the emf:

$$\mathcal{E} = \int (\vec{v}\times\vec{B})\cdot d\vec{\ell}$$

For a rod rotating about one end with angular velocity $\omega$, the local
speed is $\omega r$ and this integral gives $\tfrac12 B\omega L^2$ — a result
the flux picture also produces, but the integral gets there without any
argument about what area is being swept.

## The force balance

This is what makes the topic more than a restatement of Faraday. The emf drives
a current $I = BLv/R$ around the loop. That current sits in the same field, so
the rod feels a force $F = BIL$. Substituting:

$$F = \frac{B^2L^2v}{R}$$

Lenz's law fixes the sign: the force opposes the motion. Note the structure —
the retarding force is proportional to $v$, exactly like linear drag. A rod
released with initial speed $v_0$ and no applied force obeys $m\dot{v} =
-B^2L^2v/R$ and decays exponentially with time constant $mR/B^2L^2$. A rod
sliding down inclined rails under gravity reaches a terminal velocity when
$mg\sin\theta$ balances the magnetic force.

## Why generators need work

Pull the rod at constant speed and you must supply force $F = B^2L^2v/R$
against that opposition. The mechanical power you deliver is

$$P_{mech} = Fv = \frac{B^2L^2v^2}{R} = \frac{(BLv)^2}{R} = \frac{\mathcal{E}^2}{R}$$

which is exactly the electrical power dissipated in the resistor. Not
approximately — identically. Every joule of electrical energy came from the
agent pulling the rod.

This is the whole principle of a generator. Turn on a large load and the
turbine gets harder to turn. Nothing converts motion into current for free; the
retarding force is the price, and it is set by how much current you draw.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Do not add the magnetic force on the *charges* to your energy accounting. The
  magnetic force does no net work — it redirects the work done by whoever moves
  the rod. The correct ledger has two entries: mechanical work in, resistive
  dissipation out. Adding a third term for the magnetic field double-counts and
  will produce a factor-of-two error.
</Aside>
