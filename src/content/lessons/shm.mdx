## The equation that defines it

Any restoring force proportional to displacement produces the same motion:

$$\ddot x = -\omega^2 x \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$$

For a spring $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$; for a simple pendulum
$\omega = \sqrt{g/\ell}$. The amplitude $A$ appears nowhere in $\omega$, which
is the whole reason pendulum clocks work: the period does not care how far the
bob swings.

## Finding the frequency of anything

Most olympiad oscillation problems are not about springs. They give you a
physical system, disturb it slightly, and ask for the frequency. The recipe is
always the same: find the equilibrium, expand the net force to first order in
the displacement from it, and read off $\omega^2$. Equivalently, from the
potential:

$$\omega^2 = \frac{1}{m}\left.\frac{d^2U}{dx^2}\right|_{x_0}$$

This is why the small-angle approximation matters so much. The pendulum is not
a harmonic oscillator; it is a system whose restoring torque happens to be
proportional to displacement once $\sin\theta \approx \theta$.

<Aside label="Combining springs">
  Springs in parallel share the displacement, so their constants add. In
  series they share the force, so the reciprocals add, and the pair is always
  softer than either spring alone.
</Aside>
