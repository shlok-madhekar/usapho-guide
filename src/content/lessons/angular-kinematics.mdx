## Describing a spin

A point on a spinning wheel travels a different distance depending on how far
it sits from the axle, but every point sweeps the same angle in the same time.
That is why rotation is described by angle rather than position.

Angular position $\theta$ is measured in radians, and radians are chosen so
that arc length is simply $s = r\theta$. Angular velocity is how fast the angle
changes, $\omega = d\theta/dt$, and angular acceleration is how fast that
changes, $\alpha = d\omega/dt$.

Signs matter and are a convention you pick: counterclockwise positive is the
usual choice. A wheel slowing down while spinning counterclockwise has positive
$\omega$ and negative $\alpha$, exactly as a car braking has positive $v$ and
negative $a$.

## The equations you already know

Because $\theta$, $\omega$, $\alpha$ relate to each other by the same
derivatives that link $x$, $v$, $a$, constant angular acceleration gives the
same five equations with the letters swapped.

$$\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t, \qquad \theta = \omega_0 t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2$$

$$\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\theta$$

A record player spun up from rest at $\alpha = 2\ \text{rad/s}^2$ reaches
$\omega = 6$ rad/s in 3 s, having turned $\tfrac12(2)(9) = 9$ radians, or about
1.4 revolutions. No new physics — only new symbols.

## Back to the rim

To connect a rotating body to a point on it, multiply by the radius:

$$v = \omega r, \qquad a_t = \alpha r$$

Here $v$ is the speed along the circular path and $a_t$ is the tangential
acceleration, the part that changes speed. There is a second piece: the
centripetal acceleration $a_c = \omega^2 r$, which changes direction instead.
The total acceleration of a rim point is the vector sum of the two, and they
are perpendicular.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Every one of these relations assumes $\theta$ is in radians. Feeding degrees
  into $v = \omega r$ gives an answer off by a factor of 57. Convert at the very
  start, and treat revolutions and rpm as raw data to be converted, never as
  units you can compute with.
</Aside>
