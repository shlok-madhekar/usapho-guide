Push a heavy crate across a floor. Nothing happens. Push harder — still nothing.
Push harder again and suddenly it breaks loose and slides more easily than it
took to start it. Somewhere in there the floor was pushing back on the crate
with exactly the force you were applying, and then it stopped being able to.

That "exactly the force you were applying" is the whole lesson. In the previous
lesson you drew free body diagrams and every force on them had a known size:
weight was $mg$, the normal force was whatever the surface pressed with.
Friction is the first force you will meet whose size is not handed to you. Most
of the time you have to solve for it.

## Two laws, two grammars

Friction acts along the contact surface and opposes relative sliding. It comes
in two kinds, and the difference between them is the difference between an
equation and an inequality.

Once two surfaces are already sliding across each other, the friction force has
a fixed magnitude set by how hard they are pressed together:

$$f_k = \mu_k N$$

Here $N$ is the normal force, the perpendicular push of the surface on the
object, and $\mu_k$ is the coefficient of kinetic friction — a dimensionless
number, usually between 0.05 and 1, that depends on what the two materials are.
Notice what is *not* in that formula: the speed, the area of contact, the
weight. Only $N$ and the materials. A brick lying flat and the same brick
standing on end feel the same kinetic friction.

Static friction, when the surfaces are not sliding, is a different animal. It
supplies whatever force is needed to prevent sliding, up to a limit:

$$f_s \le \mu_s N$$

Read that as a budget. Push the crate with 20 N and it stays put — static
friction is 20 N, not $\mu_s N$. Push with 50 N and it stays put — static
friction is 50 N. The number $\mu_s N$ is the largest cheque the surface can
write. Only at the instant the crate is about to slip does the inequality
become an equality, and that is the only moment you are allowed to write
$f_s = \mu_s N$.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Writing $f_s = \mu_s N$ for an object sitting in ordinary equilibrium. That
  equation describes the threshold of slipping, not every static contact. If the
  problem does not say "maximum", "minimum", "just barely", or "on the verge",
  treat $f_s$ as an unknown and let Newton's second law tell you what it is.
  A 5.0 kg block resting on a 30° slope with $\mu_s = 0.70$ feels a friction
  force of $mg\sin 30^\circ = 24.5$ N, not $\mu_s mg\cos 30^\circ = 29.7$ N.
</Aside>

## Worked example 1: a car that locks its brakes

A car travelling at $20\ \mathrm{m/s}$ locks its wheels on a level road with
$\mu_k = 0.70$. How far does it skid?

The wheels are locked, so the tyres slide: kinetic friction, an equation, not a
budget. On level ground the only vertical forces are weight and normal force,
so $N = mg$. The friction force is $f_k = \mu_k mg$ and it is the only
horizontal force, so Newton's second law along the road gives

$$ma = -\mu_k mg \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a = -\mu_k g = -(0.70)(9.8) = -6.86\ \mathrm{m/s^2}.$$

The mass cancelled. That is worth pausing on: a loaded truck and an empty one,
same tyres, skid the same distance. Now use the kinematic relation with no time
in it, $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a d$, with final speed zero:

$$d = \frac{v_0^2}{2\mu_k g} = \frac{20^2}{2(0.70)(9.8)} = \frac{400}{13.72} = 29.2\ \mathrm{m}.$$

The same three lines run backwards give you $\mu_k$ from a measured skid, or
from a measured deceleration: if a block slows at $2.5\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ on a
level table, then $\mu_k = a/g = 2.5/9.8 = 0.255$.

## Slopes and the angle of repose

Tilt a plank with a block on it and raise the angle $\theta$ slowly. Set up
axes along the slope and perpendicular to it, rather than horizontal and
vertical — that way the acceleration, if there is any, lies along one axis.
Gravity $mg$ points straight down, which splits into $mg\sin\theta$ down the
slope and $mg\cos\theta$ into it. Perpendicular to the surface nothing
accelerates, so

$$N = mg\cos\theta.$$

Along the slope, gravity demands $mg\sin\theta$ of friction to hold the block.
The budget is $\mu_s N = \mu_s mg\cos\theta$. The block sits still as long as
demand does not exceed budget:

$$mg\sin\theta \le \mu_s\, mg\cos\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tan\theta \le \mu_s.$$

The mass cancelled again, and so did $g$. The critical angle, called the
**angle of repose**, is $\theta_{\max} = \arctan\mu_s$. Tilt a ramp until a
block just starts to slide at 32°, and you have measured
$\mu_s = \tan 32^\circ = 0.625$. Go the other way: a carton with $\mu_s = 0.60$
holds up to $\arctan 0.60 = 31.0°$.

Past that angle the block slides, friction switches to $\mu_k N$ pointing up
the slope, and

$$a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta).$$

For a 20° slope with $\mu_k = 0.25$: $a = 9.8(0.342 - 0.25 \times 0.940)
= 9.8(0.342 - 0.235) = 1.05\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.

One sign trap hides here. If the block is launched *up* the slope, it is still
moving, so friction still has magnitude $\mu_k mg\cos\theta$ — but it now
points *down* the slope, because it opposes the motion, not gravity. The two
effects add:

$$a = g(\sin\theta + \mu_k\cos\theta).$$

A 6.0 kg block sent up a 25° incline with $\mu_k = 0.30$ decelerates at
$9.8(0.423 + 0.30 \times 0.906) = 9.8(0.423 + 0.272) = 6.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$,
much harder than it would accelerate coming back down.

## The friction cone

There is a picture that makes all of this one idea instead of several. Add the
normal force and the friction force as vectors and call the result the contact
force. Since $f \le \mu_s N$, the contact force can lean away from the
perpendicular by at most $\arctan \mu_s$. Sweep that limit around and you sweep
out a cone.

The rule is then: **if the other forces on the object point into the cone, the
surface can hold it; if they point outside, nothing can.** The angle of repose
is the moment gravity leaves the cone. It also explains why shoving a crate
downward at a steep angle never helps you start it moving — pushing at an angle
outside the cone adds to the driving component and to $N$ in exactly the ratio
that keeps you outside it.

## Worked example 2: the best angle to pull

A 25 kg sled is dragged at constant speed over ground with $\mu_k = 0.50$. You
may choose the rope angle. What is the smallest tension that works?

Blank paper first: draw the sled, the weight $mg$ down, the normal force $N$ up,
friction $f_k$ backwards, and the rope tension $T$ at angle $\theta$ above the
horizontal. Constant speed means zero acceleration in both directions.

Vertically, the rope now carries part of the load, so the ground carries less:

$$N + T\sin\theta = mg \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N = mg - T\sin\theta.$$

This is the step people miss. Tilting the rope lightens the sled, which shrinks
$N$, which shrinks friction. Horizontally,

$$T\cos\theta = \mu_k N = \mu_k(mg - T\sin\theta).$$

Collect the $T$ terms: $T\cos\theta + \mu_k T\sin\theta = \mu_k mg$, so

$$T(\theta) = \frac{\mu_k mg}{\cos\theta + \mu_k\sin\theta}.$$

Check it on an easier case first. A 20 kg trunk, $\mu_k = 0.30$, rope at 30°:
$T = (0.30)(20)(9.8) / (0.866 + 0.30 \times 0.500) = 58.8/1.016 = 57.9$ N.

To minimise $T$ you maximise the denominator. Write
$\cos\theta + \mu\sin\theta = \sqrt{1+\mu^2}\,\cos(\theta - \arctan\mu)$ — that
is the standard trick for turning $a\cos\theta + b\sin\theta$ into a single
cosine — and the largest it can be is $\sqrt{1+\mu^2}$, reached when
$\theta = \arctan\mu$. So the best rope angle is the friction cone angle, and

$$T_{\min} = \frac{\mu_k mg}{\sqrt{1+\mu_k^2}} = \frac{(0.50)(25)(9.8)}{\sqrt{1.25}} = \frac{122.5}{1.118} = 110\ \mathrm{N}.$$

Pulling flat would have cost 122.5 N. The curve is flat near its minimum, which
is why nobody has to be precise about it in real life.

The same bookkeeping runs in reverse when a force presses a block *into* a
slope. Push horizontally with force $F$ on a 10 kg block on a 40° incline with
$\mu_s = 0.30$, and now $F$ has a component into the surface (raising $N$) and a
component up the slope. Perpendicular: $N = mg\cos\theta + F\sin\theta$. Along
the slope, on the verge of sliding down, friction acts up at full budget:
$F\cos\theta + \mu_s N = mg\sin\theta$. Solving for the minimum $F$ gives
42.2 N. The structure is identical — resolve, note that $N$ now depends on the
unknown, then solve.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  $N$ is almost never $mg$. It is $mg$ only on level ground with nothing else
  pushing or pulling vertically. On a slope it is $mg\cos\theta$; with an angled
  rope it is $mg \mp T\sin\theta$; in a lift it is $m(g+a)$. Since friction is
  proportional to $N$, getting $N$ wrong wrecks everything downstream. Solve the
  perpendicular direction first, every time.
</Aside>

## Two bodies, two budgets

Stack a block on a block, or tie two together, and each contact gets its own
$N$, its own $\mu$, and its own budget. The system moves as one rigid object
only while *every* contact stays inside its budget.

The procedure is always the same three steps. Assume nothing slips and find the
common acceleration by treating the whole stack as one object. Then isolate one
body and ask how much friction that acceleration requires at each contact.
Finally compare each required value with that contact's own $\mu_s N$.

**Worked example 3.** A 2.0 kg block sits on a 5.0 kg block on a frictionless
floor, with $\mu_s = \mu_k = 0.40$ between them. A horizontal 40 N force is
applied to the *lower* block. Find the lower block's acceleration.

Assume they move together. Total mass 7.0 kg, one external horizontal force:

$$a = \frac{40}{7.0} = 5.71\ \mathrm{m/s^2}.$$

Now isolate the top block. The only horizontal force on it is friction from the
block underneath, so that friction alone must produce $5.71\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$:

$$f_{\text{needed}} = (2.0)(5.71) = 11.4\ \mathrm{N}.$$

The budget at that contact is $\mu_s N = (0.40)(2.0)(9.8) = 7.84$ N. Demand
beats budget, so the assumption fails: the top block slides backwards relative
to the bottom one. Redo it with kinetic friction fixed at 7.84 N and separate
accelerations. The top block gets $a_1 = 7.84/2.0 = 3.92\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. The
bottom block feels the 40 N push forward and the top block's friction dragging
backward on it (Newton's third law):

$$a_2 = \frac{40 - 7.84}{5.0} = \frac{32.16}{5.0} = 6.43\ \mathrm{m/s^2}.$$

Run the same machinery to find the largest force that keeps a stack together.
For 3.0 kg on 7.0 kg with $\mu_s = 0.40$ between them and a frictionless floor,
the top block can be accelerated at most at $\mu_s g = 3.92\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, so
the whole 10 kg can be pushed with at most $F = (10)(3.92) = 39.2$ N.

When the floor is rough too, there are two contacts to check and the loser is
not always the obvious one. Put a 2.0 kg block on a 4.0 kg block on the ground,
$\mu_s = 0.30$ between them and $0.20$ underneath, and push the *top* block.
The top block slips once $F > (0.30)(2.0)(9.8) = 5.88$ N. The ground contact
carries all 6.0 kg, so it does not give until $F > (0.20)(6.0)(9.8) = 11.8$ N.
The upper surface fails first at 5.88 N, even though it is the one with the
larger coefficient.

That last check is a real problem type. A 2.0 kg block with $\mu_k = 0.10$ is
tied ahead of a 3.0 kg block with $\mu_k = 0.40$ on a 30° slope. Treat the pair
as one body: the effective coefficient is the weighted average
$(0.10 \times 2.0 + 0.40 \times 3.0)/5.0 = 0.28$, so
$a = 9.8(0.500 - 0.28 \times 0.866) = 2.52\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. Then isolate the
front block, where the string pulls backward:
$T = m_1 g\sin\theta - \mu_1 m_1 g\cos\theta - m_1 a
= 9.80 - 1.70 - 5.05 = 3.06$ N.

## When the whole thing is accelerating

Friction problems get one level harder when the surface itself accelerates. A
block rides on the 20° face of a wedge, and the wedge is driven horizontally at
$A = 3.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ into the hill. What is the smallest $\mu_s$ that keeps
the block from sliding on the face?

Stay in the ground frame and use axes along and across the slope. The block's
acceleration is horizontal with magnitude $A$, so its component along the slope
is $A\cos\theta$ and across the slope is $A\sin\theta$. Perpendicular to the
face, with the block pressed in,

$$N = mg\cos\theta + mA\sin\theta.$$

Along the face, gravity pulls down-slope at $mg\sin\theta$, the required
acceleration accounts for $mA\cos\theta$ up-slope, and friction $f$ makes up the
difference:

$$mg\sin\theta - f = -mA\cos\theta \ \Longrightarrow\ f = m(g\sin\theta - A\cos\theta).$$

Divide by $N$, and the mass drops out once more:

$$

\mu_{\min} = \frac{g\sin\theta - A\cos\theta}{g\cos\theta + A\sin\theta}
= \frac{3.352 - 2.819}{9.209 + 1.026} = \frac{0.533}{10.24} = 0.052.

$$

Driving the wedge into the hill did most of the holding, so barely any friction
is needed. Set $A=0$ and you recover $\tan\theta$, the angle-of-repose answer,
which is a good check that the algebra is right.

Multi-stage motion is the other common escalation: a block slides 3.0 m down a
30° ramp with $\mu_k = 0.20$, then onto a floor with $\mu_k = 0.35$. Do it in
two pieces, carrying the speed across the join. On the ramp,
$a = 9.8(0.500 - 0.20 \times 0.866) = 3.20\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, so
$v^2 = 2(3.20)(3.0) = 19.2\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}$. On the floor,
$a = -\mu_k g = -3.43\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, so $d = 19.2/(2 \times 3.43) = 2.80$ m.
You never need $v$ itself, only $v^2$.

## How to recognise a friction problem

The word "friction" is usually there, but the useful signal is finer than that.

If the problem says **"minimum force", "maximum force", "just barely", "on the
verge", or "about to slip"**, you are at the threshold and you may write
$f_s = \mu_s N$. That phrase is the permission slip for the equals sign.

If the object is **already sliding**, you have $f_k = \mu_k N$ immediately and
the only work left is getting the direction right: opposite the velocity, not
opposite the applied force. Blocks moving up a rough slope are where this
bites.

If the object is **at rest and the problem asks for the friction force**, do not
use either coefficient until you have checked. Solve the equilibrium equations
for $f$, then compare with $\mu_s N$ to confirm it is affordable.

If there is **more than one contact surface**, expect to assume no slipping,
solve for a common acceleration, and check every contact against its own budget.
Whichever fails first is where the motion begins.

And if only an **angle** appears with no masses at all, the answer is almost
certainly $\tan\theta = \mu$ in some disguise — the angle of repose, the best
rope angle, or the ladder condition $\tan\theta \ge 1/(2\mu_s)$, which is the
same friction budget argument run through a torque balance instead of a force
balance. For $\mu_s = 0.40$ that gives $\arctan(1.25) = 51.3°$.
