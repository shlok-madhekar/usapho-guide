Pull a block sideways on a spring and let go. It comes back, overshoots, goes
out the other side by the same distance, and comes back again. Do it on a
frictionless bench and it never stops. Pull it twice as far and it still takes
exactly the same time to complete a round trip — it just moves faster the whole
way. That last fact is strange enough to be worth the rest of this page.

In [rolling without slipping](/guide/rolling) you found the motion by writing a
force law and a torque law and grinding out an acceleration. You can do the same
here, but something new happens. The acceleration turns out to depend on where
the block is, which means it changes from instant to instant, which means the
constant-acceleration kinematics you have been leaning on is useless. You need a
different tool. The good news is that one tool covers a whole family of problems:
springs, pendulums, floating blocks, rocking cylinders, atoms in a crystal. The
shape of the motion is always the same.

## Where the equation comes from

A spring stretched or compressed by an amount $x$ from its natural length pushes
back with force

$$F = -kx$$

This is Hooke's law. $k$ is the **spring constant** or **stiffness**, measured in
newtons per metre: it says how many newtons of pull you get per metre of stretch.
The minus sign is the whole story. It says the force always points back toward
$x = 0$. Stretch the spring right and it pulls left; compress it left and it
pushes right. A force like that is called a **restoring force**.

Now attach a block of mass $m$ and apply Newton's second law. The only horizontal
force is the spring, so $ma = -kx$, or

$$a = -\frac{k}{m}\,x$$

Read that as a sentence: the acceleration is proportional to the displacement and
points the opposite way. At the far end of the swing the block is momentarily
still but accelerating hardest. At the middle it is moving fastest but not
accelerating at all. Nothing in constant-acceleration kinematics behaves like
that.

The quantity $k/m$ has units of $1/\text{s}^2$, so its square root is a rate.
Give it a name:

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \qquad a = -\omega^2 x$$

Any motion obeying $a = -\omega^2 x$ is called **simple harmonic motion**, SHM for
short. You can check by substitution that

$$x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$$

solves it: differentiate twice and each derivative pulls down a factor of
$-\omega$, giving $a = -\omega^2 A\cos(\omega t + \phi) = -\omega^2 x$. Here $A$ is
the **amplitude**, the largest distance from the middle, and $\phi$ is the **phase
constant**, which only sets where in the cycle the clock starts. Choose $\phi = 0$
if you release the block from rest at full stretch; choose $\phi = -\pi/2$ if it
passes through the middle at $t = 0$.

The cosine repeats when $\omega t$ advances by $2\pi$, so the **period** is

$$T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}, \qquad f = \frac{1}{T}$$

with $T$ in seconds and the frequency $f$ in hertz. Look at what is missing: $A$
does not appear. Doubling the amplitude doubles the distance travelled and
doubles the average speed, and those cancel exactly. That is the fact you noticed
at the top, and it is special to forces that are strictly proportional to
displacement.

Differentiating $x(t)$ once gives $v = -A\omega\sin(\omega t + \phi)$ and again
gives $a = -A\omega^2\cos(\omega t + \phi)$. Since sine and cosine reach 1,

$$v_{\max} = A\omega, \qquad a_{\max} = A\omega^2$$

Those two lines solve a surprising number of problems on their own.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Three numbers describe an oscillation completely: $\omega$ from the physics of
  the system, $A$ and $\phi$ from how you started it. The system sets the
  rhythm; you only choose how hard and when.
</Aside>

**Example 1.** A 0.25 kg glider on a frictionless track is attached to a spring
of stiffness 100 N/m, pulled 4.0 cm aside and released. Find the period, the
maximum speed and the maximum acceleration.

Start with $\omega$, always:

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{100}{0.25}} = \sqrt{400} = 20.0\ \text{rad/s}$$

Then $T = 2\pi/20.0 = 0.314$ s, so $f = 1/0.314 = 3.18$ Hz. The amplitude is
$A = 0.040$ m because the glider was released from rest there, so

$$v_{\max} = (0.040)(20.0) = 0.80\ \text{m/s}$$
$$a_{\max} = (0.040)(20.0)^2 = (0.040)(400) = 16\ \text{m/s}^2$$

That acceleration is larger than $g$, which should make you slightly suspicious of
anything resting loosely on top of an oscillator. Hold that thought.

## The energy shortcut

Timing is often not what a problem wants. It wants a speed at a particular place,
and it does not care when the block gets there. For that, use energy.

The work you do stretching a spring from 0 to $x$ is the area under the force
graph, a triangle of base $x$ and height $kx$, so the stored **elastic potential
energy** is $U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$. On a frictionless surface the total mechanical
energy never changes:

$$E = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$$

At the turning point $v = 0$ and $x = \pm A$, so $E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2$. Setting the
two expressions equal and solving for $v$:

$$\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; v = \omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$$

using $k/m = \omega^2$ in the last step. At $x = 0$ this gives $v = A\omega$, which
matches what you got from calculus. At $x = A$ it gives zero. In between it gives
everything.

  <Sim id="shm-energy" />
</Figure>

**Example 2.** A 2.0 kg block on a spring of stiffness 800 N/m oscillates with
amplitude 5.0 cm. How fast is it moving 3.0 cm from equilibrium?

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{800}{2.0}} = \sqrt{400} = 20.0\ \text{rad/s}$$
$$v = 20.0\sqrt{(0.050)^2 - (0.030)^2} = 20.0\sqrt{0.0025 - 0.0009} = 20.0\sqrt{0.0016}$$
$$v = 20.0 \times 0.040 = 0.80\ \text{m/s}$$

The 3-4-5 triangle hiding in there is not an accident; problem setters like it.
And notice the total energy, if you want it, is
$\frac{1}{2}(800)(0.050)^2 = 1.0$ J.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Energy is quadratic, so it is never safe to scale it linearly. Doubling the
  amplitude does not double the energy, it quadruples it. And $v$ at half the
  amplitude is not half of $v_{\max}$ — it is
  $\omega\sqrt{A^2 - (A/2)^2} = 0.866\,A\omega$. Always go back through
  $v = \omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$ rather than guessing a fraction.
</Aside>

## Hanging springs: gravity shifts the middle, not the rhythm

Hang the same block from the same spring vertically and a new force appears. Let
$y$ measure downward from the spring's natural length. The net downward force is

$$F = mg - ky$$

This is zero at $y_0 = mg/k$. That is the **static sag**: where the block hangs at
rest. Now measure displacement from *there* instead, writing $u = y - y_0$:

$$F = mg - k(u + y_0) = mg - ku - mg = -ku$$

The $mg$ cancelled exactly. The block obeys the identical SHM equation about the
hanging position, with the identical $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$. Gravity moved the
centre of the oscillation and changed nothing else.

This gives you a free measurement. Since $y_0 = mg/k$, you have $k/m = g/y_0$, so

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{y_0}}$$

Measure how far a spring sags under a load and you know the period without ever
knowing $m$ or $k$ separately. A spring that sags 4.5 cm oscillates at
$\omega = \sqrt{9.8/0.045} = \sqrt{217.8} = 14.76$ rad/s, giving $T = 0.426$ s.

**Example 3.** A 2.0 kg package is dropped from rest 20 cm above the massless pan
of a spring scale of stiffness 500 N/m, and stays on the pan. Find the amplitude
of the oscillation that follows. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².

Blank paper, first line: the motion after contact is SHM about the *loaded*
equilibrium, not about the pan's starting position. So find that point first.

$$y_0 = \frac{mg}{k} = \frac{(2.0)(9.8)}{500} = 0.0392\ \text{m}$$

At the moment of contact the package is $0.0392$ m *above* the new equilibrium, so
its displacement is $u = -0.0392$ m. Its speed there comes from the free fall:

$$v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2(9.8)(0.20)} = \sqrt{3.92} = 1.980\ \text{m/s}$$

Now use $v = \omega\sqrt{A^2 - u^2}$, rearranged for the amplitude:

$$A = \sqrt{u^2 + \frac{v^2}{\omega^2}}, \qquad \omega^2 = \frac{500}{2.0} = 250\ \text{s}^{-2}$$
$$A = \sqrt{(0.0392)^2 + \frac{3.92}{250}} = \sqrt{0.001537 + 0.015680} = \sqrt{0.017217}$$
$$A = 0.1312\ \text{m}$$

The two contributions matter comparably, so you cannot drop either. Skipping the
sag term is the standard way to get this one wrong.

## Building the right $k$ and the right $m$

Most problems are not a bare block on a bare spring. They are something that
*behaves* like one, and your job is to find the effective stiffness $k_{\text{eff}}$
and the effective mass $m_{\text{eff}}$ so that
$\omega = \sqrt{k_{\text{eff}}/m_{\text{eff}}}$.

**Springs side by side (parallel).** Both stretch by the same $x$, and their forces
add: $F = k_1x + k_2x$. So $k_{\text{eff}} = k_1 + k_2$. Two springs pulling
together are stiffer than either alone.

**Springs end to end (series).** They carry the same force $F$, and their stretches
add: $x = F/k_1 + F/k_2$. Since $k_{\text{eff}} = F/x$,

$$\frac{1}{k_{\text{eff}}} = \frac{1}{k_1} + \frac{1}{k_2}$$

A series pair is always softer than either spring alone. Two identical 400 N/m
springs joined end to end give 200 N/m.

**Two free masses joined by a spring.** Neither end is nailed down, so both move.
The separation obeys SHM with the **reduced mass**
$\mu = m_1m_2/(m_1 + m_2)$, giving $\omega = \sqrt{k/\mu}$. The quick way to see
it: each mass accelerates as $a_1 = F/m_1$ and $a_2 = -F/m_2$, so the stretch
accelerates as $a_1 - a_2 = F(1/m_1 + 1/m_2) = F/\mu$.

**A spring with its own mass.** Not every coil moves at the block's speed — the
fixed end does not move at all, and the speed grows linearly along the spring.
Adding up $\frac{1}{2}(dm)v^2$ along the length gives an extra kinetic energy of
$\frac{1}{2}(m_s/3)v^2$, so use $m_{\text{eff}} = m + m_s/3$.

**Something rolling or rotating.** Write the total energy and read off the
coefficients. For a solid cylinder rolling without slipping, $v = \omega_{\text{roll}}R$
and $I = \frac{1}{2}mR^2$, so the kinetic energy is
$\frac{1}{2}mv^2 + \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}mR^2\right)(v/R)^2 = \frac{3}{4}mv^2$,
which is $\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{3}{2}m\right)v^2$. Effective mass $\frac{3}{2}m$,
and the period lengthens by $\sqrt{3/2}$.

That last move generalises. If you can write

$$E = \tfrac{1}{2}m_{\text{eff}}\dot q^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}k_{\text{eff}}q^2$$

for some coordinate $q$ measured from equilibrium, the system is a harmonic
oscillator with $\omega^2 = k_{\text{eff}}/m_{\text{eff}}$, whatever $q$ physically is.

**Example 4.** Two carts of mass 0.50 kg and 1.50 kg sit on a frictionless track
joined by a spring of stiffness 200 N/m. Pull them apart and release. Find the
frequency.

Neither cart is fixed, so use the reduced mass:

$$\mu = \frac{(0.50)(1.50)}{0.50 + 1.50} = \frac{0.75}{2.00} = 0.375\ \text{kg}$$
$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{200}{0.375}} = \sqrt{533.3} = 23.09\ \text{rad/s}$$
$$f = \frac{23.09}{2\pi} = 3.67\ \text{Hz}$$

Check it for sanity: $\mu$ is smaller than either mass, so the pair oscillates
faster than 1.50 kg alone on that spring would. It should, because both ends are
free to respond.

<Aside label="Levers and pulleys" warn>
  When a spring is attached through a lever or a pulley, its stretch is not the
  same as the mass's displacement. If the spring stretches $n$ times as far as
  the mass moves, the energy $\frac{1}{2}k(nq)^2$ tells you
  $k_{\text{eff}} = n^2k$ — the factor is squared, not linear. Getting $n$ upside
  down turns a factor of 4 into a factor of $1/4$, so draw the geometry and check
  which end moves further before you trust the number.
</Aside>

## When the motion is only partly harmonic

Two more patterns show up often enough to name.

**Half-cycles glued together.** A puck sliding between two springs it touches but
is not attached to spends half a period on each. Each half is a genuine
half-oscillation of that spring's system, taking $\pi\sqrt{m/k}$. So the total
period is

$$T = \pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k_1}} + \pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k_2}}$$

Do not average the stiffnesses. Add the times.

**Losing contact.** Anything merely resting on an oscillator is held down only by
gravity, and the surface can only push, never pull. The book stays on the platform
as long as the required downward acceleration at the top of the motion does not
exceed $g$. Since $a_{\max} = A\omega^2$, contact survives while

$$A\omega^2 \le g \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A_{\max} = \frac{g}{\omega^2} = \frac{mg}{k}$$

which is the static sag again. Push the oscillation past the sag and the book
leaves the platform at the top.

## Recognising it

Reach for SHM when a problem contains any of these:

- The words *period*, *frequency*, *oscillates*, *vibrates*, *bounces*, or
  *small oscillations*. "Small" is the tell that a curved potential is about to be
  approximated by a parabola.
- A restoring force or torque proportional to displacement, or one you can show is
  proportional after expanding for small displacements.
- A system displaced from equilibrium and released, with no friction mentioned.

Then the routine is fixed. Find the equilibrium. Displace by a small $q$ from it.
Get either the net force, in the form $F = -k_{\text{eff}}q$, or the total energy,
in the form $\frac{1}{2}m_{\text{eff}}\dot q^2 + \frac{1}{2}k_{\text{eff}}q^2$. Read off
$\omega = \sqrt{k_{\text{eff}}/m_{\text{eff}}}$. Everything else — period, maximum speed,
maximum acceleration, speed at a given place — follows from $\omega$, $A$ and the
two formulas $v_{\max} = A\omega$ and $v = \omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$.

If instead the question gives you a place and asks for a speed, go straight to
energy and skip $t$ entirely. If it gives you a time, use the cosine.

The next system worth this treatment has no spring in it at all: a mass on a
string, restored by gravity, harmonic only because
$\sin\theta \approx \theta$ when $\theta$ is small. That is
[pendulums](/guide/pendulums).
