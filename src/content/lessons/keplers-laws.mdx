## Three empirical statements

Kepler extracted these from Tycho Brahe's naked-eye observations decades before
Newton explained them.

1. **Orbits are ellipses**, with the Sun at one focus — not at the centre.
2. **The line from the Sun to the planet sweeps equal areas in equal times.**
3. **$T^2 \propto a^3$**, where $a$ is the semi-major axis.

An ellipse is described by its semi-major axis $a$ and eccentricity $e$. The
closest and farthest points, perihelion and aphelion, sit at $a(1-e)$ and
$a(1+e)$ from the focus. A circle is the special case $e = 0$, with the two
foci merged at the centre.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Treating Earth's seasons as a consequence of the first law. Earth's orbit has
  $e \approx 0.017$, so the Sun's distance varies by under 4% — and perihelion
  falls in early January, during northern winter. Seasons come from axial tilt.
  Kepler's laws describe the geometry of the orbit, not the climate.
</Aside>

## The second law is angular momentum

In a short time $dt$, the planet moves $\vec v\,dt$ and the swept region is
nearly a triangle with the Sun. Its area is half the magnitude of the cross
product of the two sides, so

$$\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}\,|\vec r \times \vec v| = \frac{L}{2m}$$

Gravity always points from the planet straight back at the Sun, parallel to
$\vec r$. A force along $\vec r$ exerts no torque about that point, so $L$ is
constant — and therefore so is $dA/dt$. Equal areas in equal times is angular
momentum conservation, wearing a geometric disguise.

This is why it holds for *any* central force, not just inverse square. The
first and third laws are specific to $1/r^2$; the second is much more general.

It also gives you a fast tool. At perihelion and aphelion the velocity is
perpendicular to $\vec r$, so $L = mvr$ at both, and

$$v_p\, r_p = v_a\, r_a$$

Combine that with energy conservation and you can solve most two-point orbit
problems without touching the shape of the ellipse.

## Where the third law comes from

For a circular orbit the derivation is two lines. Gravity supplies the
centripetal force:

$$\frac{GMm}{r^2} = m\omega^2 r, \qquad \omega = \frac{2\pi}{T}$$

Rearranging,

$$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}\,r^3$$

The full result replaces $r$ with the semi-major axis $a$ and is exact for
ellipses too. The proportionality constant $4\pi^2/GM$ depends only on the
central mass, which is what makes the law useful: measure a moon's period and
orbital radius, and you have weighed the planet. The orbiting body's own mass
never appears, so long as it is small compared to $M$.

The exponents are worth remembering as a pattern. Doubling the semi-major axis
multiplies the period by $2^{3/2} \approx 2.83$ and divides the speed by
$\sqrt{2}$.
