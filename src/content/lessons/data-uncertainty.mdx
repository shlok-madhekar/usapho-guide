## An answer without an uncertainty is not a measurement

Every measured number carries a range. The job of data analysis is to carry
that range through the algebra so the final result has one too, and to arrange
the algebra so a straight line does the work.

Two rules cover almost every propagation you will need. For a sum or
difference, absolute uncertainties add in quadrature:

$$Q = a \pm b \quad \Rightarrow \quad \delta Q = \sqrt{(\delta a)^2 + (\delta b)^2}$$

For a product, quotient, or power, *relative* uncertainties add in quadrature,
with the exponent as a multiplier:

$$Q = a^m b^n \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{\delta Q}{Q} = \sqrt{\left(m\,\frac{\delta a}{a}\right)^2 + \left(n\,\frac{\delta b}{b}\right)^2}$$

The quadrature is there because independent errors do not conspire; they
partly cancel. Add them linearly only if the two inputs share a common
systematic source.

The exponent matters more than people expect. Measuring $g$ from a pendulum
uses $g = 4\pi^2 L / T^2$, so a 1% error in $T$ becomes a 2% error in $g$,
while a 1% error in $L$ stays 1%. Time many periods at once and divide.

## Linearize before you fit

A graph is only useful if you expect a straight line, because the eye and the
least-squares fit are both good at lines and bad at curves. So transform until
the model is linear.

For a power law $y = Ax^n$, take logs: $\ln y = \ln A + n \ln x$. Plot
$\ln y$ against $\ln x$ and the slope is the exponent $n$, the intercept is
$\ln A$.

For an exponential decay $N = N_0 e^{-t/\tau}$, take logs of $N$ only:
$\ln N = \ln N_0 - t/\tau$. Plot $\ln N$ against $t$ and the slope is
$-1/\tau$.

Sometimes no logs are needed, just a smarter choice of axes. For a pendulum,
plotting $T^2$ against $L$ gives a line of slope $4\pi^2/g$ through the
origin, which also lets you check the origin as a diagnostic: a nonzero
intercept is evidence of a systematic offset.

## Reading a slope and its uncertainty

Draw the best-fit line by eye through the error bars, then draw the steepest
and shallowest lines that still pass through essentially all of them. Half the
difference of those two slopes is a defensible uncertainty:

$$\delta m \approx \frac{m_{\max} - m_{\min}}{2}$$

Then propagate. If the slope is $4\pi^2/g$, then $g = 4\pi^2/m$ and
$\delta g / g = \delta m / m$. Quote the result with one or at most two
significant figures in the uncertainty, and round the value to match:
$g = 9.75 \pm 0.14~\text{m/s}^2$, never $9.7482 \pm 0.1413$.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Propagating relative uncertainty through a *difference* of two close numbers
  is the classic disaster. If $a = 10.0 \pm 0.1$ and $b = 9.8 \pm 0.1$, then
  $a - b = 0.2 \pm 0.14$, a 70% uncertainty out of two 1% measurements. When a
  problem hands you a subtraction of near-equal quantities, that is usually the
  point of the problem: find an experimental design that measures the small
  difference directly.
</Aside>
