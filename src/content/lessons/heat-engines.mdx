## The first law is bookkeeping

Energy added to a gas either raises its internal energy or is spent doing work:

$$\Delta U = Q - W, \qquad W = \int P\,dV$$

Sign conventions cause more lost points than the physics does. Here $W$ is
work done *by* the gas, so expansion is positive. Internal energy depends only
on temperature for an ideal gas, which makes $\Delta U = 0$ the defining
feature of an isothermal process.

The four standard processes follow directly:

- Isothermal: $\Delta U = 0$, so $Q = W = nRT\ln(V_f/V_i)$.
- Adiabatic: $Q = 0$, so $W = -\Delta U$, along $PV^\gamma$ constant.
- Isobaric: $W = P\Delta V$.
- Isochoric: $W = 0$, so all heat goes to internal energy.

## Cycles

On a PV diagram, the work done in a cycle is the enclosed area, positive for a
clockwise loop. Since the gas returns to its starting state, $\Delta U = 0$
around any full cycle and therefore $Q_{net} = W_{net}$.

Efficiency compares what you want to what you paid for:

$$\eta = \frac{W_{net}}{Q_{in}} \le 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H}$$

The bound on the right is Carnot's, and it depends only on the reservoir
temperatures. Any claimed efficiency above it is a sign of an arithmetic
error, not a discovery.

## Entropy

For a reversible process, $dS = dQ/T$. The second law says the entropy of an
isolated system never decreases, which is what makes heat flow spontaneously
from hot to cold and forbids the perfect engine.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Entropy is a state function, so its change between two states does not depend
  on the path. To get the entropy change of an irreversible process, invent
  any convenient reversible path between the same endpoints and compute along
  that instead.
</Aside>
