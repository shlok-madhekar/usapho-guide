## The symmetry

Light was known to be a wave, then shown to carry momentum in discrete lumps.
De Broglie proposed the converse: things known to be particles should carry a
wavelength. Take the photon relation $p = h/\lambda$ and solve it the other way
round.

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$$

For a non-relativistic particle $p = mv$, and if it was accelerated from rest
through a potential difference $V$, then $p = \sqrt{2mqV}$, giving

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mqV}}$$

An electron across 54 V comes out at about 1.7 Å. That is the spacing between
atoms in a crystal, which is not a coincidence in the history — Davisson and
Germer fired electrons at nickel and got a diffraction pattern with peaks
exactly where Bragg's law put them for that wavelength.

## Why you never see it

Plug in a thrown baseball: $m = 0.15$ kg, $v = 40$ m/s, so $p = 6$ kg·m/s and

$$\lambda = \frac{6.6 \times 10^{-34}}{6} \approx 10^{-34}\ \text{m}$$

Twenty orders of magnitude smaller than a proton. Diffraction requires an
aperture comparable to $\lambda$, and no such aperture exists or could exist,
so the baseball travels in a straight line. The wave nature is not switched
off for large objects; it is present and unmeasurable, which is the same thing
in practice.

The rule of thumb: use the wave picture when $\lambda$ is comparable to the
size of whatever the particle is interacting with. Electrons and crystal
planes, yes. Neutrons and nuclei, yes. Anything you can hold, no.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Use momentum, not energy, and not speed alone. Two particles with the same
  kinetic energy have different wavelengths if their masses differ, because
  $p = \sqrt{2mK}$ — a proton at 1 eV has a wavelength about 43 times shorter
  than an electron at 1 eV.
</Aside>

## What it buys you

The de Broglie wavelength turns Bohr's angular momentum rule from a decree into
a picture. Requiring a whole number of wavelengths around a circular orbit,
$n\lambda = 2\pi r$, and substituting $\lambda = h/mv$, reproduces
$mvr = n\hbar$ exactly. Standing waves fit; anything else interferes with
itself and dies out.

It also sets the resolution limit of an electron microscope. Optical
microscopes stall at a few hundred nanometres because visible light will not
resolve finer than its own wavelength. Accelerate electrons to 100 keV and
$\lambda$ drops below a picometre, which is why atoms are visible at all.
