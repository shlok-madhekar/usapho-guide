## Why a hammer is hard to spin one way and easy the other

Hold a hammer by the end of its handle and swing it around in a circle. Now hold
it by the head and swing it the same way. The second is noticeably easier. You
did not change the hammer, and you did not change its mass. All you changed was
where the heavy part sits relative to the axis you are turning about.

In the [last lesson](/guide/torque-equilibrium) you worked out what makes
something start to turn: torque, $\tau = rF\sin\theta$. You balanced torques so
that nothing moved. Now you want to know what happens when they do not balance,
and for that you need the rotational answer to a different question. In straight
lines, mass tells you how hard it is to speed something up. In rotation, mass is
not enough. You need a number that knows about mass *and* about where the mass
is. That number is the moment of inertia, and this whole lesson is about
computing it.

## Where the formula comes from

Take a rigid body spinning about a fixed axis at angular velocity $\omega$
(radians per second). Chop it into tiny pieces. Call the mass of one piece $m_i$
and its perpendicular distance from the axis $r_i$. Because the body is rigid,
every piece goes around at the same $\omega$, so piece $i$ moves at speed
$v_i = \omega r_i$ — the pieces far out move fast, the pieces near the axis
crawl.

Add up the kinetic energy:

$$

K = \sum \tfrac12 m_i v_i^2 = \sum \tfrac12 m_i (\omega r_i)^2
   = \tfrac12 \Big(\sum m_i r_i^2\Big)\,\omega^2.

$$

Compare that with $K = \tfrac12 m v^2$. The $\omega$ sits exactly where $v$ used
to, and the bracket sits exactly where $m$ used to. So define

$$I = \sum_i m_i r_i^2, \qquad K_{\text{rot}} = \tfrac12 I \omega^2.$$

$I$ is the moment of inertia. Read the definition in words: every gram of the
body contributes its own mass times the *square* of its distance from the axis.
The square is the entire story. Mass twice as far out counts four times as much.
That is why a flywheel is built with its metal in the rim, why a tightrope
walker carries a long pole, and why the hammer swung by its handle fights you.

Two warnings hide in the definition. First, $r_i$ is the distance from the
**axis**, measured perpendicular to it — not the distance from the centre of the
object. Second, $I$ is not a property of a body on its own. It is a property of
a body *and an axis*. Change the axis and the number changes. Always write down
which axis you mean before you compute anything.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  A quick check: since $I = \sum m_i r_i^2$ and no piece of a body of radius $R$
  is farther than $R$ from a central axis, any $I$ about a symmetry axis must
  come out as $kMR^2$ with $0 < k \le 1$. If you ever get $k = 3$, you have made
  an arithmetic slip.
</Aside>

For a solid body the sum becomes an integral, $I = \int r^2 \, dm$. You will
only need a few of these, and the standard results all come out of one line of
calculus. A thin rod of mass $M$ and length $L$, axis through the centre,
perpendicular to the rod: the mass per unit length is $\lambda = M/L$, so
$dm = \lambda\,dx$, and $r = |x|$ with $x$ running from $-L/2$ to $L/2$.

$$

I = \int_{-L/2}^{L/2} x^2 \frac{M}{L}\,dx
    = \frac{M}{L}\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-L/2}^{L/2}
    = \frac{M}{L}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{L^3}{8} = \tfrac{1}{12}ML^2.

$$

A uniform disk of mass $M$ and radius $R$ about its central axis: slice it into
thin rings. A ring at radius $r$ of thickness $dr$ has area $2\pi r\,dr$, so
with surface density $\sigma = M/\pi R^2$ its mass is $dm = \sigma 2\pi r\,dr$,
and every bit of it sits at the same distance $r$:

$$

I = \int_0^R r^2\,\sigma 2\pi r\,dr = 2\pi\sigma \frac{R^4}{4}
    = \frac{M}{\pi R^2}\cdot\frac{\pi R^4}{2} = \tfrac12 MR^2.

$$

These are the two you should be able to rebuild from scratch. The rest are worth
knowing by heart.

## The short table you actually need

All of these are about an axis through the centre of mass, and all have the form
$I = kMR^2$ or $kML^2$:

- Point mass at distance $r$: $I = mr^2$. Everything else is built from this.
- Thin hoop or hollow cylinder, axis through the centre: $I = MR^2$ $(k = 1)$.
- Uniform disk or solid cylinder, central axis: $I = \tfrac12 MR^2$.
- Annulus (a washer) with inner radius $R_1$, outer $R_2$:
  $I = \tfrac12 M(R_1^2 + R_2^2)$.
- Solid sphere about a diameter: $I = \tfrac25 MR^2$.
- Thin spherical shell about a diameter: $I = \tfrac23 MR^2$.
- Solid cone about its symmetry axis: $I = \tfrac{3}{10}MR^2$.
- Thin rod about its centre, perpendicular: $I = \tfrac{1}{12}ML^2$.
- Thin rod about one end, perpendicular: $I = \tfrac13 ML^2$.

Look at the coefficients as a story about where mass sits. Hoop $1$: all the
mass is at the rim. Disk $0.5$: mass spread evenly over the area, so most of it
is well inside $R$. Shell $2/3$, solid sphere $2/5$: the same contrast for
spheres, and the solid sphere is lowest of all because a sphere's mass crowds
towards the middle, near the axis. If you forget a number, you can usually
bracket it by asking how much of the body lives far from the axis.

One more quantity lives here. If you want a single length that summarises how
far out the mass effectively sits, define the **radius of gyration** $k$ by
$I = Mk^2$, so $k = \sqrt{I/M}$. It is the radius at which you would have to put
all the mass, as a single hoop, to get the same $I$. For a body of mass
$8.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ with $I = 0.50\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$,
$k = \sqrt{0.50/8.0} = \sqrt{0.0625} = 0.25\ \mathrm{m}$.

## Moments of inertia add, and they subtract

Because $I$ is a sum over mass, two bodies rotating about the *same* axis have a
combined moment of inertia equal to the sum of the separate ones. A disk with a
lump of clay on its rim, about the central axis:

$$I = \tfrac12 M_d R^2 + m_{\text{clay}} R^2.$$

With $M_d = 4.0\ \mathrm{kg}$, $R = 0.30\ \mathrm{m}$ and
$m = 1.0\ \mathrm{kg}$: $I = \tfrac12(4.0)(0.09) + (1.0)(0.09) = 0.18 + 0.09
= 0.27\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$.

The same logic run backwards handles holes. A disk with a piece drilled out is
(full disk) minus (the piece that used to be there), so

$$I_{\text{with hole}} = I_{\text{full}} - I_{\text{removed piece}},$$

both taken about the same axis. This trick is worth more than any table entry,
because it turns an impossible-looking shape into two easy ones.

### Worked example 1: a disk with a central hole

A uniform disk of radius $0.40\ \mathrm{m}$ and surface density
$\sigma = 20\ \mathrm{kg/m^2}$ has a hole of radius $0.20\ \mathrm{m}$ cut out,
centred on the axis. Find $I$ about that axis.

You are given density, not mass, which is the hint that you should compute both
masses yourself. Full disk:

$$

M_{\text{full}} = \sigma \pi R^2 = 20 \times \pi \times (0.40)^2
= 20 \times \pi \times 0.16 = 10.053\ \mathrm{kg},

$$

$$I_{\text{full}} = \tfrac12 (10.053)(0.16) = 0.8042\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}.$$

Removed disk, radius $0.20\ \mathrm{m}$:

$$

M_{\text{hole}} = 20 \times \pi \times 0.04 = 2.513\ \mathrm{kg}, \qquad
I_{\text{hole}} = \tfrac12 (2.513)(0.04) = 0.0503\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}.

$$

Subtract: $I = 0.8042 - 0.0503 = 0.754\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$.

Sanity check it against the annulus formula,
$I = \tfrac12 M(R_1^2 + R_2^2)$ with $M = 10.053 - 2.513 = 7.540\ \mathrm{kg}$:
$\tfrac12 (7.540)(0.04 + 0.16) = \tfrac12(7.540)(0.20) = 0.754$. Same number.
The annulus formula is not something extra to memorise; it is this subtraction,
done once in symbols.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  When you subtract a hole, subtract the hole's *own* mass, not the mass of the
  body you were given. In the example above the removed disk is
  $2.513\ \mathrm{kg}$ because it is made of the same material at the same
  density. Students routinely subtract $\tfrac12 M R_{\text{hole}}^2$ using the
  full mass $M$, which quietly removes far too much.
</Aside>

## Moving the axis: the parallel axis theorem

Everything so far was about an axis through the centre of mass. Real problems
pivot things at their ends, their rims, a third of the way along. You do not
need a new integral for each one.

Put the origin at the centre of mass and let the new axis be parallel to the old
one, a distance $d$ away. For piece $i$, its position vector from the centre of
mass is $\vec{r}_i$, and its distance from the new axis is
$|\vec{r}_i - \vec{d}\,|$. Then

$$

I = \sum m_i |\vec{r}_i - \vec{d}\,|^2
   = \sum m_i r_i^2 - 2\vec{d}\cdot\sum m_i \vec{r}_i + d^2 \sum m_i .

$$

The first term is $I_{\text{cm}}$. The last is $Md^2$. The middle term contains
$\sum m_i \vec{r}_i$, which is $M$ times the position of the centre of mass —
measured from the centre of mass, so it is zero. That leaves

$$I = I_{\text{cm}} + Md^2.$$

In words: shifting the axis away from the centre of mass always *increases* $I$,
by exactly the amount a point mass $M$ sitting at the centre of mass would
contribute. Check it on the rod: about the end, $d = L/2$, so
$I = \tfrac{1}{12}ML^2 + M(L/2)^2 = \tfrac{1}{12}ML^2 + \tfrac14 ML^2
= \tfrac13 ML^2$, which is the table entry you were told to memorise.

Two consequences fall straight out. The centre of mass axis gives the *smallest*
possible $I$ among all parallel axes, since $Md^2 \ge 0$. And the growth is
quadratic in $d$, so moving the pivot out is punishing fast.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  The theorem starts from the centre of mass and only from there. To go from a
  rod's end to a point a quarter of the way along, you cannot add $Md^2$ with
  $d = L/4$. Come back to the centre of mass first, then step out to the new
  axis in one move.
</Aside>

### Worked example 2: a rod with masses on its ends

A uniform rod of mass $2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ and length $1.2\ \mathrm{m}$ carries
$0.50\ \mathrm{kg}$ point masses at each end, and rotates about its centre.
Find $I$.

Write down the pieces before touching a calculator. Three bodies, one axis, so
three terms added.

Rod about its centre: $\tfrac{1}{12}(2.0)(1.2)^2 = \tfrac{1}{12}(2.0)(1.44)
= 0.24\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$.

Each end mass is a point at $r = 0.60\ \mathrm{m}$:
$mr^2 = (0.50)(0.36) = 0.18\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, and there are two of them, so
$0.36\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$.

Total: $I = 0.24 + 0.36 = 0.60\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$. Notice that the two little
masses, together only half the rod's mass, contribute more than the rod does.
The $r^2$ again.

### Worked example 3: an off-centre hole

A uniform disk of mass $9.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ and radius $0.30\ \mathrm{m}$ has a
hole of radius $0.10\ \mathrm{m}$ cut out, centred $0.15\ \mathrm{m}$ from the
axis. Find $I$ about the original central axis.

This one needs both tools at once, which is why it looks hard and is not.

Full disk: $I_{\text{full}} = \tfrac12 (9.0)(0.30)^2 = \tfrac12(9.0)(0.09)
= 0.405\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$.

The removed piece: same material, so its mass is the full mass scaled by area
ratio,

$$

m_{\text{hole}} = 9.0 \times \frac{\pi (0.10)^2}{\pi (0.30)^2}
= 9.0 \times \frac{0.01}{0.09} = 1.0\ \mathrm{kg}.

$$

Its moment of inertia about *its own* centre is
$\tfrac12 (1.0)(0.01) = 0.005\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, but you need it about the
disk's central axis, $0.15\ \mathrm{m}$ away. Parallel axis:

$$

I_{\text{hole}} = 0.005 + (1.0)(0.15)^2 = 0.005 + 0.0225
= 0.0275\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}.

$$

Subtract: $I = 0.405 - 0.0275 = 0.3775\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$.

### Worked example 4: a rod whose density is not uniform

A rod runs from $x = -1.0\ \mathrm{m}$ to $x = +1.0\ \mathrm{m}$, with mass per
unit length $\lambda = \lambda_0(1 + x^2/L^2)$, where
$\lambda_0 = 3.0\ \mathrm{kg/m}$ and $L = 1.0\ \mathrm{m}$. Find $I$ about the
centre.

No table entry helps here, so go back to the definition. Take a slice of width
$dx$ at position $x$. Its mass is $dm = \lambda\,dx$ and its distance from the
axis is $|x|$, so $r^2\,dm = x^2\lambda\,dx$:

$$

I = \int_{-1}^{1} x^2 \lambda_0\left(1 + \frac{x^2}{L^2}\right)dx
    = 3.0\int_{-1}^{1} (x^2 + x^4)\,dx .

$$

Both integrands are even, so integrate from $0$ to $1$ and double:

$$

\int_{-1}^{1} x^2 dx = \frac{2}{3}, \qquad
  \int_{-1}^{1} x^4 dx = \frac{2}{5}.

$$

So $I = 3.0\left(\tfrac23 + \tfrac25\right) = 3.0 \times \tfrac{16}{15}
= 3.2\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$.

Worth a sanity check. The total mass is
$\int \lambda\,dx = 3.0(2 + \tfrac23) = 8.0\ \mathrm{kg}$, so the radius of
gyration is $\sqrt{3.2/8.0} = 0.63\ \mathrm{m}$. A uniform rod of the same
length would have $k = L_{\text{tot}}/\sqrt{12} = 2.0/3.46 = 0.58\ \mathrm{m}$.
Slightly larger, as it should be: this rod is heavier at its ends.

### Worked example 5: where to pivot for the fastest swing

A uniform rod of length $L = 1.0\ \mathrm{m}$ hangs as a pendulum from a pivot a
distance $d$ from its centre. The period of small swings is
$T = 2\pi\sqrt{I/(Mgd)}$, where $d$ is the pivot-to-centre-of-mass distance.
Which $d$ makes $T$ smallest?

First put in the parallel axis theorem, $I = \tfrac{1}{12}ML^2 + Md^2$:

$$

T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{M\left(\tfrac{1}{12}L^2 + d^2\right)}{Mgd}}
    = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{1}{g}\left(\frac{L^2}{12d} + d\right)}.

$$

The mass cancelled, which already tells you something. $T$ is smallest when the
bracket $f(d) = L^2/(12d) + d$ is smallest. Differentiate and set to zero:

$$

f'(d) = -\frac{L^2}{12d^2} + 1 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; d^2 = \frac{L^2}{12}
\;\Longrightarrow\; d = \frac{L}{\sqrt{12}} = \frac{1.0}{3.464}
= 0.289\ \mathrm{m}.

$$

That is the tug-of-war Figure 2 shows. Pivot too close to the centre and gravity
has almost no lever arm, so the swing is slow. Pivot at the end and $I$ has
grown too large. The best point is neither, and it lands where the two terms in
$f(d)$ are equal.

For the record, the plain end-pivot case: $d = 0.50\ \mathrm{m}$,
$I = \tfrac13(M)(1.0)^2$, so
$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac13/(9.8 \times 0.50)} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.0680}
= 2\pi(0.2608) = 1.64\ \mathrm{s}$.

## How to recognise these problems

You are looking at a moment-of-inertia problem when the question involves
something rigid turning about an axis and you need a number to plug into
$K = \tfrac12 I\omega^2$, $\tau = I\alpha$, $L = I\omega$, or a pendulum period.
Once you know that, the shape of the body tells you which tool:

- **One named standard shape, axis through the centre.** Read off the table.
  Nothing to do but square the radius.
- **Several bodies on one axis.** Add. Clay on a rim, masses on a rod, a disk on
  a shaft: compute each $I$ about the shared axis and sum them.
- **A hole, a notch, a missing quarter.** Subtract. Compute the whole body, then
  the removed piece, and take the difference. If the removed piece is off
  centre, you will need the parallel axis theorem for it.
- **The axis is not through the centre of mass.** Parallel axis theorem, always
  starting from $I_{\text{cm}}$. Phrases like "about its end", "about its rim",
  "a third of the way along" are the giveaway.
- **A density that depends on position**, or a shape with no table entry. Go to
  $I = \int r^2\,dm$. Write $dm$ in terms of $dx$ or $dr$ first; that step is
  the whole problem.
- **"Smallest", "fastest", "optimum".** Write $I$ as a function of the free
  variable using the parallel axis theorem, then differentiate.

Two habits will save you most of the marks. Write the axis down before anything
else, because $I$ is meaningless without it. And check the coefficient at the
end: a sensible answer for a body of size $R$ looks like $kMR^2$ with $k$ under
one, unless the axis has been shifted away from the centre of mass.

With $I$ in hand, the rotational version of Newton's second law is waiting.
Torque plays the part of force, $I$ plays the part of mass, and angular
acceleration plays the part of $a$. That is the next lesson:
[Rotational Dynamics](/guide/rotational-dynamics).
