## Sort the collision before solving it

Every collision conserves total momentum. What varies is how much kinetic
energy survives, and that is the first thing to establish, because it decides
how many equations you get.

- **Elastic**: kinetic energy is conserved too. Two equations per dimension of
  motion, so a head-on collision of two known masses is fully determined.
- **Perfectly inelastic**: the bodies leave with a common velocity. Momentum
  alone determines the answer, and the energy loss is whatever is left over.
- **Partially inelastic**: everything in between. You need a third piece of
  information, usually the coefficient of restitution
  $e = |v_{\text{sep}}| / |v_{\text{app}}|$, which is $1$ for elastic and $0$
  for perfectly inelastic.

A useful phrasing of the loss: the kinetic energy that can disappear is only
the part associated with relative motion. The energy carried by the motion of
the center of mass can never be lost, because that is fixed by momentum
conservation.

## One dimension: use the relative velocity

Solving two simultaneous equations, one of them quadratic, is slow and
error-prone. Replace the energy equation with its linear consequence: in an
elastic collision the relative velocity reverses.

$$v_{1i} - v_{2i} = -(v_{1f} - v_{2f})$$

Pair that with momentum conservation and you have two linear equations. For a
mass $m_1$ striking a stationary $m_2$:

$$v_{1f} = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}v_{1i}, \qquad v_{2f} = \frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}v_{1i}$$

Check the limits, since they are worth memorizing as behavior rather than
formula. Equal masses: the incoming body stops dead and the target leaves with
the full speed. A light body hitting a very heavy one: it bounces back at
nearly its original speed, like a ball off a wall. A heavy body hitting a light
one: it barely slows, and the light one leaves at nearly twice the incoming
speed.

## Two dimensions: momentum is a vector

In a plane, conserve $p_x$ and $p_y$ separately. That is two equations, plus
one more if the collision is elastic — still one short of the four unknown
final components, which is why 2D problems must hand you an extra fact such as
one outgoing angle.

Set your axes along the initial velocity so that the incoming momentum has no
$y$ component. Then the $y$ equation reads simply
$m_1 v_{1f}\sin\theta_1 = m_2 v_{2f}\sin\theta_2$, with the two bodies going to
opposite sides.

One result is worth carrying: **equal masses colliding elastically, one
initially at rest, always separate at 90°.** Momentum gives
$\vec v_i = \vec v_1 + \vec v_2$, and energy gives $v_i^2 = v_1^2 + v_2^2$;
squaring the first and comparing forces $\vec v_1 \cdot \vec v_2 = 0$. Pool
players rely on it. If the angle is not 90°, the collision was not elastic.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Do not write a conservation equation for speeds. Momentum conservation is a
  vector statement, so $m_1v_{1i} = m_1v_{1f} + m_2v_{2f}$ is wrong in two
  dimensions. Resolve into components first, every time.
</Aside>
