You have a table of numbers. Somewhere in it is a law of physics, and your job
is to get the law out. A pendulum was timed at five lengths. A cart rolled down
a ramp past five photogates. A detector counted clicks every two minutes for
ten minutes. None of those tables announces what it means. Stare at raw columns
long enough and you can convince yourself of almost anything.

The last lesson was about attacking a problem on paper: name what you know,
name what you want, find the bridge. Data is the same job with one extra
difficulty. The numbers are not exact. Every one of them is a little wrong, and
you need an answer anyway, plus an honest statement of how wrong the answer
might be. This lesson is the toolkit: turn the table into a straight line, read
the physics off the slope and the intercept, and attach an uncertainty that
means something.

## Slope and area are the two things a graph gives you

Start with the graph you already half-know. Plot velocity against time. Between
two instants the velocity climbs by $\Delta v$ while the clock advances by
$\Delta t$, and acceleration is defined as exactly that ratio, so the slope of
a $v$–$t$ graph *is* the acceleration. Steeper line, bigger acceleration. A
flat line means no acceleration at all.

Now cut the same graph into thin vertical strips. Over a strip so narrow that
$v$ barely changes, the distance covered is $v\,\Delta t$ — which is the height
of the strip times its width, its area. Add up every strip and you get the
total displacement. So the area under a $v$–$t$ graph is the displacement.
Slope and area: those two readings cover most of what any graph in a lab is
for.

Read that area as a trapezium: parallel sides of $8.0$ s and $4.0$ s, height
$4.0$ m/s, so area $=\tfrac12(8.0+4.0)(4.0) = 24$ m. Chopping it into a
triangle, a rectangle and another triangle gives the same 24 m. Use whichever
shape is easier.

The same two readings work on any axes. On a force-extension graph the slope is
the spring constant, since $F = kx$ means $k = \Delta F/\Delta x$. On a
voltage-current graph the slope is the resistance. On a position-time graph the
slope is the speed. Before you read anything off a graph, say out loud what its
slope means physically.

## Make the graph straight before you read it

A straight line is the only shape your eye judges reliably. You can spot one
point that has strayed off a line. You cannot spot one point that has strayed
off a curve, because you do not know what the curve should have been. So the
first move with any dataset is to plot it in whatever variables make the
expected relation a straight line.

Take the pendulum. Theory says $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$, where $T$ is the period in
seconds, $L$ the length in metres and $g$ the acceleration of free fall. Plot
$T$ against $L$ and you get a gentle curve that a dozen different laws would
fit about equally well. Square both sides instead:

$$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{g}\,L$$

That is $y = mx$ with $y = T^2$, $x = L$ and $m = 4\pi^2/g$. Plot $T^2$ against
$L$ and the prediction is a straight line through the origin. Its slope
measures $g$, and the fact that it comes out straight is itself evidence that
the square-root law is right.

The general move: rearrange the expected relation into $y = mx + b$ where $x$
and $y$ are things you can compute from your columns. Four patterns cover
almost everything you will meet.

- **Squared variable.** $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2ax$ is already linear in $v^2$ against
  $x$: slope $2a$, intercept $v_0^2$.
- **Reciprocals.** The thin lens equation $1/u + 1/v = 1/f$ becomes
  $1/v = -\,1/u + 1/f$. Plot $1/v$ against $1/u$: slope $-1$ and intercept
  $1/f$. The physics you want sits in the *intercept* here, not the slope.
- **Power law with unknown exponent.** If $y = Ax^{n}$, take natural logs of
  both sides: $\ln y = \ln A + n\ln x$. On a log-log plot the slope is the
  exponent $n$ and the intercept is $\ln A$.
- **Exponential.** If $y = A e^{-t/\tau}$, then $\ln y = \ln A - t/\tau$. On a
  semi-log plot ($\ln y$ against $t$, with $t$ *not* logged) the slope is
  $-1/\tau$.

Those last two are different plots and telling them apart is a result in
itself. A power law straightens on log-log. An exponential straightens on
semi-log. If you are unsure which law you have, try both and see which one
comes out straight.

## Worked example 1: a curve straightened, twice

A cart is released from rest on a ramp and photogates measure its speed at four
positions: $(0.25\ \text{m},\ 1.24\ \text{m/s})$, $(0.50\ \text{m},\ 1.75\
\text{m/s})$, $(0.75\ \text{m},\ 2.15\ \text{m/s})$, $(1.00\ \text{m},\ 2.48\
\text{m/s})$. Find the acceleration.

Speed against position curves, so do not plot that. From rest, $v^2 = 2ax$, so
square the speeds and plot $v^2$ against $x$:

$1.24^2 = 1.538$, $1.75^2 = 3.063$, $2.15^2 = 4.622$, $2.48^2 = 6.150$, all in
m²/s².

Those four numbers against $0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00$ do lie on a line through
the origin. Take the slope from the two end points, which are the furthest
apart and so the least affected by the wobble in any single reading:

$$m = \frac{6.150 - 1.538}{1.00 - 0.25} = \frac{4.612}{0.75} = 6.149\ \text{m/s}^2$$

The slope is $2a$, so $a = 6.149/2 = 3.07$ m/s². Two things to notice. The
squaring was not a trick, it came from the kinematics you already know. And the
line passing through the origin confirms the cart really did start from rest;
a positive intercept would have meant it was given a push.

## Worked example 2: the answer lives in the intercept

A converging lens forms real images. Measured object and image distances are
$(0.200\ \text{m},\ 0.300\ \text{m})$ and $(0.300\ \text{m},\ 0.200\
\text{m})$. Find the focal length.

Write the lens equation in straight-line form: $1/v = -1/u + 1/f$. Compute the
reciprocals.

For the first pair: $1/u = 1/0.200 = 5.000\ \text{m}^{-1}$ and
$1/v = 1/0.300 = 3.333\ \text{m}^{-1}$.
For the second: $1/u = 3.333\ \text{m}^{-1}$, $1/v = 5.000\ \text{m}^{-1}$.

Slope first, as a check on the model:

$$m = \frac{5.000 - 3.333}{3.333 - 5.000} = \frac{1.667}{-1.667} = -1.00$$

Theory says the slope must be exactly $-1$, and it is, so the data is
behaving. Now the intercept. Using the first point,

$$b = \frac{1}{v} - m\cdot\frac{1}{u} = 3.333 - (-1.00)(5.000) = 8.333\ \text{m}^{-1}$$

and $b = 1/f$, so $f = 1/8.333 = 0.120$ m, which is 12.0 cm. If you had only
hunted for a slope you would have found $-1$, a pure number with no physics in
it, and missed the lens entirely.

## Worked example 3: a background that ruins the straight line

A detector watches a radioactive source and records total count rates:
$(0\ \text{min},\ 425\ \text{s}^{-1})$, $(3\ \text{min},\ 225\ \text{s}^{-1})$,
$(6\ \text{min},\ 125\ \text{s}^{-1})$. With the source taken away it still
reads $25\ \text{s}^{-1}$ from cosmic rays and the walls. Find the half-life.

Try the semi-log plot on the raw numbers first, to see the failure. $\ln 425 =
6.052$, $\ln 225 = 5.416$, $\ln 125 = 4.828$. The drops are $0.636$ then
$0.588$ — not equal, so the points are not on a line, and a plain exponential
does not fit. That is the signature of an added constant.

Subtract the background, because only the source part decays: $425-25 = 400$,
$225-25 = 200$, $125-25 = 100$. Now $\ln 400 = 5.991$, $\ln 200 = 5.298$,
$\ln 100 = 4.605$. The drops are $0.693$ and $0.693$, identical. Straight line.

The slope is

$$-\lambda = \frac{4.605 - 5.991}{6.0 - 0} = \frac{-1.386}{6.0} = -0.2310\ \text{min}^{-1}$$

and the half-life is the time to fall by a factor of two, so
$\lambda t_{1/2} = \ln 2$ and

$$t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{0.2310} = 3.00\ \text{min}$$

You could have seen it without logs at all: the corrected rates halve every
three minutes. The logarithm is there so the eye can check that they keep
halving at the same rate all the way along.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  An exponential sitting on a constant offset still *looks* like a decay, and
  its semi-log plot still slopes downwards. It is only slightly bent, and if
  you fit a line through it anyway you get a half-life that is too long and no
  warning that anything happened. Whenever a decay is measured by a detector,
  ask what the reading is with the source gone, and subtract it first.
</Aside>

## Uncertainty: the number after the plus-or-minus

A measurement with no uncertainty attached is not a measurement, it is a rumour.
Two different things can go wrong.

**Random error** scatters points on both sides of the truth. Timing by hand,
you are sometimes early and sometimes late. Averaging beats it down.
**Systematic error** shifts every point the same way: a ruler with a worn end,
a stopwatch that starts late, a length measured to the top of a pendulum bob
instead of its centre. Averaging does nothing to it. A thousand repeats with a
bad ruler gives a beautifully precise wrong answer.

For a single measurement quoted as $L = 12.4 \pm 0.3$ cm, the $0.3$ cm is the
**absolute uncertainty** and the ratio $\delta L / L = 0.3/12.4 = 0.024$, or
$2.4\%$, is the **relative uncertainty**. The same relation read backwards
turns a percentage specification into a number: a balance good to $1.6\%$
reading $4.75$ kg has $\delta m = 0.016 \times 4.75 = 0.076$ kg.

Repeat a measurement $N$ times and you get a spread. The **standard deviation**
$s$ measures how far a typical single reading falls from the mean:

$$s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{N-1}}$$

But you do not report a single reading, you report the mean, and the mean is
better than any one reading. Its uncertainty is the **standard error**

$$\sigma_{\bar{x}} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{N}}$$

Try five timings: $2.31$, $2.28$, $2.35$, $2.30$, $2.26$ s. The mean is
$11.50/5 = 2.300$ s. The deviations are $+0.01, -0.02, +0.05, 0.00, -0.04$, and
their squares sum to $0.0001+0.0004+0.0025+0+0.0016 = 0.0046$. Divide by
$N-1 = 4$ to get $0.00115$, take the root: $s = 0.034$ s. Then
$\sigma_{\bar x} = 0.034/\sqrt{5} = 0.034/2.236 = 0.015$ s. Report
$2.300 \pm 0.015$ s.

That $\sqrt{N}$ is also the answer to "how many trials do I need". Rearranged,
$N = (s/\sigma_{\bar x})^2$. To pull a spread of $0.24$ s down to a standard
error of $0.05$ s you need $N = (0.24/0.05)^2 = 4.8^2 = 23.04$. A count has to
be a whole number, and $23$ would leave the standard error just above target,
so round *up*: 24 trials. Notice how brutal the square root is: cutting the uncertainty in half
costs four times the work.

## Carrying uncertainty through a calculation

Uncertainties combine, and they do not simply add. Two independent errors are
unlikely to both be at their worst in the same direction, so you add them **in
quadrature**, which means squaring, adding, and taking the root.

For a sum or difference $z = x \pm y$, the absolute uncertainties combine:

$$\delta z = \sqrt{(\delta x)^2 + (\delta y)^2}$$

For a product or quotient $z = xy$ or $z = x/y$, the *relative* uncertainties
combine:

$$\frac{\delta z}{z} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\delta x}{x}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\delta y}{y}\right)^2}$$

And a power multiplies the relative uncertainty by the exponent: if $z = x^n$
then $\delta z/z = |n|\,\delta x/x$. That last rule tells you where to spend
your effort. In $g = 4\pi^2 L/T^2$ an error in $T$ counts double, so time
twenty swings and divide by twenty before you worry about the ruler.

**Worked example 4.** Five pendulum timings are fitted with $T^2$ against $L$
and the least-squares fit reports a slope $m = 4.037 \pm 0.025$ s²/m. What is
$g$, and how well do you know it?

From $T^2 = (4\pi^2/g)L$, the slope is $4\pi^2/g$, so

$$g = \frac{4\pi^2}{m} = \frac{39.478}{4.037} = 9.779\ \text{m/s}^2$$

Since $g$ is a constant divided by $m$, it is a quotient, and the relative
uncertainty passes straight through:

$$\frac{\delta g}{g} = \frac{\delta m}{m} = \frac{0.025}{4.037} = 0.00619$$

$$\delta g = 0.00619 \times 9.779 = 0.061\ \text{m/s}^2$$

Report $g = 9.78 \pm 0.06$ m/s². Round the uncertainty to one significant
figure, then round the value to the same decimal place. Writing
$9.7792 \pm 0.06$ claims four digits you have not earned.

One more number you will be asked for: the **percent difference** from an
accepted value, which is just $|{\text{measured}-\text{accepted}}|/\text{accepted}$
as a percentage. Here $|9.779-9.80|/9.80 = 0.0021$, or $0.2\%$. Compare that
with your own $\pm 0.06$: the disagreement is smaller than your uncertainty, so
the experiment agrees with the accepted value.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Forcing a fit through the origin because theory says the intercept is zero
  throws away the best warning you have. Fit both the slope and the intercept,
  then ask whether the intercept is consistent with zero. If a $T^2$ against
  $L$ line hits the vertical axis well above zero, every length was measured
  short by the same amount — and the size of that missing length is
  $b/m$, which you can now report as a finding instead of a mistake.
</Aside>

## Recognising that this is the technique

You are being asked a data-analysis question, and should reach for this
toolkit, whenever one of these shows up.

**A table of pairs and the word "plot", "graph", "slope" or "intercept".** Even
if the word is absent, four or five $(x, y)$ pairs in the problem statement
means fit, not solve. You are not meant to substitute one pair into a formula.

**A relation that is not linear, plus an unknown constant.** Then the question
is which combination of the variables to put on the axes. Rearrange to
$y = mx + b$ first and everything else follows.

**An unknown exponent.** "Assuming $v = Am^{p}$, find $p$" always means logs of
both columns, then the slope. If the independent variable appears in an
exponent instead — a decay, a discharge, a cooling curve — log only the
dependent variable.

**A plus-or-minus anywhere in the given data.** Then the answer wants an
uncertainty too, and you should track relative uncertainties through the
algebra rather than the absolute ones, except across sums and differences.

**Words like "background", "offset", "zero error", or a description of how the
measurement was made that sounds slightly wrong.** Those are systematic-error
problems in disguise, and the fix is either to subtract a constant before
fitting or to read the constant off the intercept.

The equipment in these questions changes every time — lenses, springs, filters,
counters — but the moves do not. Get the relation into a straight line, decide
in advance what the slope and the intercept mean, take the slope from
well-separated points, and never quote a number without saying how well you
know it.
