## Acceleration without a change in speed

An object moving in a circle at constant speed is still accelerating, because
its velocity keeps changing direction. That acceleration points at the center
and has magnitude

$$a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r$$

The important habit: $a_c$ is not a force. Nothing called "centripetal force"
appears in a free body diagram. You draw the real forces (gravity, normal,
tension, friction) and then set their net inward component equal to $mv^2/r$.

## Where the inward force comes from

The whole topic is really a catalogue of what supplies that net inward force.

- Ball on a string: tension.
- Car on a flat road: static friction, so the maximum speed is $v = \sqrt{\mu g r}$.
- Car on a banked track: the horizontal component of the normal force, giving $\tan\theta = v^2/gr$ when no friction is needed.
- Satellite: gravity.
- Bucket swung overhead: gravity plus the normal force from the bucket, which is why there is a minimum speed at the top.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  At the top of a vertical loop, both gravity and the normal force point down,
  so $N + mg = mv^2/r$. The critical case is $N = 0$, giving $v^2 = gr$. Adding
  the two forces with the wrong sign here is the standard mistake.
</Aside>

## Gravitation

Newton's law of gravitation and the circular-orbit condition together give
almost every orbital result you need:

$$F = \frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}, \qquad \frac{GMm}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r}$$

Eliminating $v$ gives Kepler's third law, $T^2 \propto r^3$. The orbiting
mass cancels, which is why a satellite's mass never appears in its period.

Two results worth carrying: orbital speed falls as $1/\sqrt{r}$, so a higher
orbit is slower, and the total energy of a circular orbit is $-GMm/2r$,
exactly half the potential energy and negative because the orbit is bound.
