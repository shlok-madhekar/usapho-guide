## What "massless" buys you

A real rope has mass, and pulling one end accelerates the rope itself, so the
tension at the far end is smaller. Setting the mass to zero removes that
difference: with $m = 0$, any net force would give infinite acceleration, so
the net force on every segment must vanish and the tension is the same
everywhere along the rope.

An ideal pulley extends this around a corner. It has no mass and no bearing
friction, so it needs no net torque, and the tension is unchanged from one side
to the other. All it does is redirect the rope.

Drop either idealization and tension stops being uniform. A pulley with moment
of inertia $I$ needs a torque to spin up, which requires $T_1 \ne T_2$. A heavy
hanging rope has more tension at the top than the bottom, since the upper part
supports more weight.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Tension is uniform along a single massless rope, but two different ropes are
  two different unknowns. Label them $T_1$ and $T_2$ from the start; a rope that
  passes through a hand, a knot, or a body is not one rope.
</Aside>

## The Atwood machine

Masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ hang from a rope over an ideal pulley. Take down as
positive for $m_1$ and up as positive for $m_2$, which builds the constraint
into the signs: whatever distance one falls, the other rises.

$$m_1 g - T = m_1 a, \qquad T - m_2 g = m_2 a$$

Add the two equations and $T$ disappears:

$$a = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,g, \qquad T = \frac{2m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}\,g$$

Check the limits. Equal masses give $a = 0$. If $m_2 \to 0$, then $a \to g$ and
$T \to 0$: free fall. And $T$ always lies strictly between $m_2 g$ and $m_1 g$
— the tension can never be the full weight of either side unless nothing
accelerates.

## Writing the constraint

The constraint is a statement about lengths, and the reliable way to get it is
to write the total rope length in terms of positions and note that it is
constant. Differentiate twice and you have a relation among the accelerations.

For a movable pulley with two rope segments supporting it, the length
bookkeeping gives $x_{\text{load}} = \tfrac{1}{2}x_{\text{end}}$: pull the free
end a metre and the load rises half a metre, at half the speed and half the
acceleration. In exchange, the load feels twice the tension. This is the whole
mechanical advantage of a block and tackle, and it falls out of length
counting, not force intuition.
