## One point that stands in for the whole system

A wrench spun across a table tumbles in a complicated way, but one particular
point inside it slides in a perfectly straight line. That point is the center of
mass, the mass-weighted average position of everything in the system:

$$\vec R_{\text{cm}} = \frac{\sum_i m_i \vec r_i}{\sum_i m_i}$$

For a continuous body the sum becomes $\vec R_{\text{cm}} = \frac{1}{M}\int
\vec r\, dm$. Two practical shortcuts do most of the work in problems. First,
exploit symmetry: a uniform sphere, rod, or disc has its center of mass at its
geometric center, so no integral is needed. Second, treat composite objects as
point masses located at their own centers — a hammer is a rod plus a head, and
a plate with a hole punched in it is the full plate plus a **negative** mass
where the hole is.

The center of mass need not lie inside the object. A doughnut's is in the hole,
and a high jumper arching over the bar keeps hers below it.

## Its velocity obeys Newton's laws

Differentiate the definition and the total momentum falls out:

$$M\vec V_{\text{cm}} = \sum_i m_i \vec v_i = \vec P_{\text{total}}$$

Differentiate once more:

$$\vec F_{\text{ext}} = M\vec A_{\text{cm}}$$

Internal forces cancel in pairs by Newton's third law, so they cannot move the
center of mass. Only external forces can. This is the sharpest statement of why
you cannot lift yourself by your own bootstraps, and why an astronaut adrift
must throw something to get anywhere.

The corollary is the one problems use: **with no net external force, the center
of mass moves at constant velocity.** If it started at rest, it stays put no
matter how violently the parts rearrange. A person walking from one end of a
floating raft to the other slides the raft backward by exactly the amount that
keeps $\vec R_{\text{cm}}$ fixed.

## The exploding projectile

Fire a shell on a parabolic trajectory and let it burst at the top. The
explosive forces are internal, so they change nothing about the center of mass.
Gravity is still the only external force, and it acts on the total mass. The
center of mass therefore continues along the original parabola as though
nothing happened, until the first fragment hits the ground and the ground
starts supplying an external force.

That gives you a free equation. If a shell splits into two equal pieces and one
lands at a known spot, the other must land where the average of the two
positions sits on the undisturbed parabola's landing point.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  The center of mass follows the parabola only while gravity is the sole
  external force. Once a fragment lands, or air drag becomes significant, the
  normal force or drag is external and the argument stops. Always state the
  moment your reasoning expires.
</Aside>

## Working in the center-of-mass frame

Shifting to the frame moving with $\vec V_{\text{cm}}$ often collapses the
algebra, because in that frame the total momentum is zero and momenta come in
equal and opposite pairs. Solve there, then add $\vec V_{\text{cm}}$ back to
every velocity to return to the lab.
