## Two slits

Two narrow slits a distance $d$ apart, lit coherently, act as two sources. For
a screen far away the rays to a given point are effectively parallel, and the
path difference is the leg of a small right triangle, $d\sin\theta$. Bright
fringes appear where that is a whole number of wavelengths:

$$d\sin\theta = m\lambda$$

For small angles $\sin\theta \approx y/L$, so fringes are evenly spaced by
$\lambda L / d$. Narrower slit spacing spreads the pattern out. Notice that
this measures $\lambda$ with a ruler — the fringe spacing is a macroscopic
stand-in for a wavelength you cannot see.

## Many slits

A grating with thousands of slits obeys the same condition $d\sin\theta =
m\lambda$, because a maximum still requires adjacent slits to agree. What
changes is sharpness. With two slits, going slightly off a maximum costs only a
small phase error. With $N$ slits, that same small error accumulates across the
array, and the contributions curl around and cancel completely once the total
spread reaches $2\pi$. The maxima narrow as $1/N$ while staying in place.

Sharp maxima are what makes a grating a measuring instrument: resolving power
grows with $N$, so a grating separates two nearby wavelengths that a double
slit smears together.

## A formula that means the opposite

A single slit of width $a$ gives

$$a\sin\theta = m\lambda, \qquad m = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$$

Identical in form, but these are the dark fringes. The reasoning differs. Treat
the slit as a continuum of sources and pair each point in the top half with the
point a distance $a/2$ below it. When $a\sin\theta = \lambda$, every such pair
is exactly out of phase, so the whole slit cancels. That argument produces
zeros, never maxima, and $m = 0$ is excluded because it would pair every point
with itself.

The real double-slit pattern is the interference fringes multiplied by this
single-slit envelope. When an envelope zero lands on an interference maximum,
that fringe is missing.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Writing $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ for a single slit and calling the result a
  bright fringe is the most common error in this topic. Check which length you
  are using: $d$ is a separation between slits and gives maxima; $a$ is the
  width of one slit and gives minima.
</Aside>
