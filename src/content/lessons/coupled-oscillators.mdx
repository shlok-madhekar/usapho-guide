## Two carts that will not leave each other alone

Put one cart on an air track, tie it to a wall with a spring, pull it aside and
let go. You get a sine wave at one frequency, forever. Nothing surprising.

Now put a second cart next to it, tie that one to the opposite wall, and join
the two carts with a third, much weaker spring. Pull only the first cart aside
and release. For a few seconds it swings and the second cart barely stirs. Then
the second one starts to build. A minute later the first cart is hanging dead
still and the second is doing all the swinging. Then it comes back. Nobody
pushed anything; the energy walked from one cart to the other through a spring
that is barely there.

The [Lagrangian](/guide/lagrangian) gave you a machine for writing equations of
motion for systems with several coordinates. This lesson is about what to do
with those equations once you have them, when the coordinates refuse to
separate. The answer is always the same: find the special motions in which the
whole system rings at a single frequency. Those are the **normal modes**, and
once you have them, every other motion in the system is just a sum of them.

## Why the equations refuse to separate

Take two equal masses $m$ on a line. Each is tied to its nearby wall by a
spring of stiffness $k$, and a coupling spring of stiffness $k_c$ joins them.
Let $x_1$ and $x_2$ be displacements from equilibrium, both measured to the
right.

The wall spring on cart 1 is stretched by $x_1$ and pulls back with $-kx_1$.
The middle spring's length has changed by $x_2 - x_1$: if cart 2 has moved
right more than cart 1, the middle spring is stretched and pulls cart 1 to the
right. So it contributes $+k_c(x_2-x_1)$ to cart 1 and the opposite to cart 2.
That gives

$$
m\ddot x_1 = -kx_1 + k_c(x_2-x_1)
$$

$$
m\ddot x_2 = -kx_2 - k_c(x_2-x_1)
$$

Neither line can be solved by itself: each one contains the other cart's
coordinate. That is what "coupled" means, and it is the entire difficulty.

There is a cheap trick here that shows you the way out. Add the two equations.
The $k_c$ terms cancel exactly, and with $s = x_1+x_2$ you get
$m\ddot s = -ks$. Subtract them instead. With $d = x_1-x_2$ the coupling terms
add rather than cancel, and you get $m\ddot d = -(k+2k_c)d$.

Two ordinary, uncoupled oscillator equations. Each combination has its own
single frequency:

$$
\omega_+=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}},\qquad \omega_-=\sqrt{\frac{k+2k_c}{m}}
$$

$s$ and $d$ are called **normal coordinates**. The physical motions they
describe are the normal modes. In the $s$ mode ($d=0$, so $x_1=x_2$) the carts
move together, the middle spring never changes length, and it might as well not
exist — hence $\omega_+$ has no $k_c$ in it at all. In the $d$ mode
($s=0$, so $x_2=-x_1$) the carts move oppositely, the middle spring is
stretched by $2x_1$, and each cart feels an extra restoring force $2k_cx_1$.

The antiphase mode is always the faster one, because it is the one that
deforms the coupling spring. Turn the coupling up and the two frequencies
spread apart, with the in-phase one pinned in place.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
Count the modes before you hunt for them. $N$ masses moving along one line have
$N$ degrees of freedom and therefore exactly $N$ normal modes. If your symmetry
guesses have produced fewer, you have missed one — often a zero-frequency mode
in which the whole system drifts without stretching any spring.
</Aside>

## Worked example 1: the numbers for a real pair

Two carts of mass $m=0.50$ kg, wall springs $k=200$ N/m, coupling spring
$k_c = 50$ N/m.

In-phase:

$$
\omega_+=\sqrt{\frac{200}{0.50}}=\sqrt{400}=20.0\ \text{rad/s}
$$

Antiphase:

$$
\omega_-=\sqrt{\frac{200+2(50)}{0.50}}=\sqrt{\frac{300}{0.50}}=\sqrt{600}=24.49\ \text{rad/s}
$$

That is the whole calculation for the easy version of this problem. Write down
the two shapes, ask what each does to the coupling spring, read off the
effective stiffness.

## Any motion at all is a sum of the modes

The equations are linear, so if two motions solve them, so does their sum. The
general solution is

$$
x_1(t)=A\cos(\omega_+t+\phi_+)+B\cos(\omega_-t+\phi_-)
$$

$$
x_2(t)=A\cos(\omega_+t+\phi_+)-B\cos(\omega_-t+\phi_-)
$$

Four constants $A,B,\phi_+,\phi_-$ for four initial conditions (two positions,
two velocities). The sign flip on $B$ is the only difference between the lines,
and it is just the statement that the second mode is antiphase.

Now the opening mystery is not mysterious. Hold cart 2 at rest, pull cart 1
aside by $a$, release both. You need $x_1(0)=a$, $x_2(0)=0$, which forces
$A=B=a/2$, and zero initial velocities force $\phi_\pm=0$. Then

$$
x_1(t)=\frac{a}{2}\left(\cos\omega_+t+\cos\omega_-t\right)
= a\cos\!\left(\frac{\omega_--\omega_+}{2}t\right)\cos\!\left(\frac{\omega_-+\omega_+}{2}t\right)
$$

using the identity $\cos P+\cos Q = 2\cos\frac{P-Q}{2}\cos\frac{P+Q}{2}$. The
second factor is a fast wobble at the average frequency. The first is a slowly
changing envelope. Cart 2 gets the same expression with a sine in place of the
first cosine, so when one envelope is at its largest the other is at zero.
That is a **beat**, and it is the energy sloshing back and forth.

Two timescales matter, and people mix them up constantly. The envelope of
$x_1$ vanishes when $\frac{\omega_--\omega_+}{2}t=\pi/2$, so the energy is
fully handed over after

$$
t_{\text{transfer}}=\frac{\pi}{\omega_--\omega_+}
$$

and the whole pattern repeats — cart 1 loud again — after twice that,

$$
T_{\text{beat}}=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_--\omega_+}
$$

If the modes are quoted in hertz rather than rad/s, those become
$t_{\text{transfer}} = 1/(2\Delta f)$ and $T_{\text{beat}}=1/\Delta f$.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
The beat period is set by the difference of the two mode frequencies, not by
either frequency and not by their average. And the energy has moved across in
*half* a beat period, because energy does not care about the sign of the
amplitude. Two modes at 3.00 Hz and 3.20 Hz give $\Delta f = 0.20$ Hz, a beat
period of 5.0 s, and complete transfer after 2.5 s.
</Aside>

## Worked example 2: unequal masses, no symmetry to lean on

Symmetry guessing dies the moment the two sides stop matching. A cart of
$m_1=1.0$ kg and a cart of $m_2=2.0$ kg each have a wall spring of
$k=400$ N/m, joined by $k_c=200$ N/m. Find the mode frequencies and the shape
of the slower one.

The equations of motion are built exactly as before, only with different
masses:

$$
m_1\ddot x_1 = -(k+k_c)x_1 + k_cx_2,\qquad
m_2\ddot x_2 = -(k+k_c)x_2 + k_cx_1
$$

A normal mode means both carts moving at one frequency, so try
$x_1 = A_1\cos\omega t$ and $x_2=A_2\cos\omega t$. Each $\ddot x$ brings down
$-\omega^2$, the cosines divide out, and you are left with two algebraic
equations:

$$
(600-1.0\,\omega^2)A_1 = 200A_2,\qquad (600-2.0\,\omega^2)A_2=200A_1
$$

with $k+k_c=600$ N/m. Multiply them together and cancel $A_1A_2$:

$$
(600-\omega^2)(600-2\omega^2)=200^2=40000
$$

Expand: $360000-1200\omega^2-600\omega^2+2\omega^4=40000$, so
$2\omega^4-1800\omega^2+320000=0$, and dividing by 2,

$$
\omega^4-900\omega^2+160000=0
$$

This is a quadratic in $\omega^2$. The discriminant is
$900^2-4(160000)=810000-640000=170000$, whose square root is $412.3$. So

$$
\omega^2=\frac{900\pm412.3}{2}=656.2\ \text{or}\ 243.8\ \text{s}^{-2}
$$

giving $\omega_- = \sqrt{656.2}=25.6$ rad/s and
$\omega_+=\sqrt{243.8}=15.6$ rad/s. Two masses, two modes, as promised.

For the shape, put $\omega^2=243.8$ back into the *first* equation, which you
never used on its own:

$$
\frac{A_2}{A_1}=\frac{600-243.8}{200}=\frac{356.2}{200}=1.78
$$

The heavy cart swings $1.78$ times as far as the light one in the slow mode.
That should feel right: this is the mode where they move in the same
direction, and the mode adjusts until the momenta balance out sensibly.

The recipe generalises with no changes. Assume every coordinate goes as
$\cos\omega t$, get a set of linear equations in the amplitudes, and demand
that the determinant of the coefficient matrix vanish (otherwise the only
solution is all amplitudes zero). For three masses that determinant is a cubic
in $\omega^2$; for the chain of $1.0$, $2.0$ and $3.0$ kg between four springs
of $500$ N/m it gives modes at $11.8$, $24.5$ and $34.6$ rad/s.

## Chains, rings, and free systems

Three shortcuts cover most of what appears in competition.

**A uniform chain between fixed walls.** $N$ equal masses $m$ joined by $N+1$
identical springs $k$ to each other and to two walls. The mode shapes are
sampled sine waves that vanish at the walls, and the frequencies are

$$
\omega_n = 2\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\,\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi}{2(N+1)}\right),
\qquad n=1,2,\dots,N
$$

You can check it against the case you already did: $N=1$ gives
$2\sqrt{k/m}\sin(\pi/4)=\sqrt{2k/m}$, which is right for one mass between two
springs. Use this whenever the chain is uniform; setting up a five-by-five
determinant by hand when the formula exists is a way to lose twenty minutes.

**A free system.** If nothing is tied to a wall, one mode has $\omega=0$: the
whole thing translates. Two free masses $m_1,m_2$ joined by a spring $k$ have
that zero mode plus one real one at $\omega=\sqrt{k/\mu}$, where
$\mu = m_1m_2/(m_1+m_2)$ is the reduced mass. For $1.0$ and $2.0$ kg with
$k=300$ N/m, $\mu = 2/3$ kg and $\omega=\sqrt{450}=21.2$ rad/s.

**A ring.** Three equal masses joined head to tail in a loop by three identical
springs has the zero translation mode plus a doubly degenerate mode at
$\omega=\sqrt{3k/m}$. For $m=0.50$ kg and $k=200$ N/m that is $\sqrt{1200}=34.6$
rad/s. The counting works: three masses, three modes, one of them zero.

## Worked example 3: two pendulums of different lengths

This one combines everything: a coupling that is not symmetric because the two
oscillators are not identical.

Two bobs of mass $m=0.40$ kg hang on strings of length $L_1=1.00$ m and
$L_2=1.20$ m, joined by a spring $k=3.0$ N/m that is unstretched when both hang
straight down. Take $g = 9.80$ m/s². Use the horizontal bob displacements
$x_1,x_2$ as coordinates.

A pendulum of length $L$ displaced by $x$ feels a restoring force $-mgx/L$, so
on its own it has $\omega^2 = g/L$. The spring adds a coupling exactly like the
carts:

$$
m\ddot x_1 = -\frac{mg}{L_1}x_1 + k(x_2-x_1),\qquad
m\ddot x_2 = -\frac{mg}{L_2}x_2 - k(x_2-x_1)
$$

Divide by $m$ and write $\omega_1^2 = g/L_1 + k/m$ and
$\omega_2^2 = g/L_2 + k/m$ for the diagonal entries, with $k/m$ appearing on
the off-diagonal too. Numbers: $k/m = 3.0/0.40 = 7.5$ s⁻²,
$g/L_1 = 9.80$ s⁻², $g/L_2 = 9.80/1.20 = 8.167$ s⁻². So
$\omega_1^2 = 17.30$ and $\omega_2^2=15.667$ s⁻².

The determinant condition for a two-by-two system of this shape has a tidy
closed form worth memorising:

$$
\omega^2 = \frac{\omega_1^2+\omega_2^2}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{\omega_1^2-\omega_2^2}{2}\right)^2+\left(\frac{k}{m}\right)^2}
$$

The mean of the two uncoupled values, plus or minus a term that mixes the
detuning with the coupling. Here the mean is $(17.30+15.667)/2 = 16.483$, the
half-difference is $(17.30-15.667)/2=0.8167$, and

$$
\sqrt{0.8167^2+7.5^2}=\sqrt{0.667+56.25}=\sqrt{56.92}=7.544
$$

So $\omega^2 = 16.483 - 7.544 = 8.939$, giving the lower mode
$\omega=\sqrt{8.939}=2.99$ rad/s, and $\omega^2 = 24.03$ for the upper,
$\omega = 4.90$ rad/s.

That formula also tells you what detuning does. If the two oscillators are
identical the square root is just $k/m$ and the splitting is symmetric about
the common frequency. Make them different and the splitting *grows*, but the
modes stop being fifty-fifty mixtures: each mode leans towards the oscillator
it started closest to. Which is why detuned oscillators never fully hand their
energy over. Writing $\kappa$ for the coupling rate and $\delta$ for half the
frequency difference, the largest fraction of energy that ever reaches the
second oscillator is

$$
f_{\max}=\frac{\kappa^2}{\kappa^2+\delta^2}
$$

With $\kappa = 0.40$ rad/s and $\delta = 0.60$ rad/s, that is
$0.16/(0.16+0.36)=0.16/0.52 = 0.308$. Not even a third of the energy gets
across, no matter how long you wait. Detuning kills transfer; that is the whole
reason a tuning fork rings a matched fork across the room and ignores a
mismatched one.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
Two nearly degenerate oscillators joined by any coupling at all split into two
modes and trade energy back and forth. It does not matter that one mode is a
vertical bounce and the other a twist, as in a Wilberforce pendulum. Measure
the two mode frequencies, take the difference, and every timing question
follows: modes at $1.480$ and $1.560$ Hz differ by $0.080$ Hz, so pure bounce
becomes pure twist after $1/(2\times0.080)=6.25$ s.
</Aside>

## Driving a coupled system

Push cart 1 with a force $F\cos\omega t$ and let the damping be tiny. In steady
state both carts oscillate at the drive frequency $\omega$, so put
$x_1 = X_1\cos\omega t$, $x_2 = X_2\cos\omega t$ into the equations. Cart 2 has
no drive of its own, so its equation is

$$
-m\omega^2X_2 = -(k+k_c)X_2 + k_cX_1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{X_2}{X_1}=\frac{k_c}{k+k_c-m\omega^2}
$$

That single expression answers a family of problems. For $m=0.50$ kg,
$k=200$ N/m, $k_c=50$ N/m, drive at the in-phase mode $\omega=20.0$ rad/s:
$m\omega^2 = 0.50\times400=200$, the denominator is $250-200=50$, and the ratio
is $50/50 = 1.0$. The carts move together, which is what driving at a mode
frequency should do — the drive builds up that mode and nothing else. Drive at
$22.0$ rad/s instead, between the modes: $m\omega^2=0.50\times484=242$, the
denominator is $250-242 = 8$, and the ratio is $50/8 = 6.25$. The undriven cart
swings six times as far as the driven one.

Push that further and you get the **vibration absorber**. The denominator of
$X_1$ (not $X_2$) carries a factor $k_a - m_a\omega^2$ when you bolt a small
mass $m_a$ on a spring $k_a$ to a shaking machine. Tune it so
$\omega^2 = k_a/m_a$ at the drive frequency and the machine stops dead: the
absorber moves exactly enough to cancel the drive. To quiet a machine driven at
$14.14$ rad/s with an absorber of $m_a = 0.40$ kg you need

$$
k_a = m_a\omega^2 = 0.40\times(14.14)^2=0.40\times200=80\ \text{N/m}
$$

## Recognising the technique

Reach for normal modes the moment a problem has **more than one thing that can
oscillate and a spring, string, or torque linking them**. The specific
giveaways:

- Two or more masses, and the equation for each contains the other's
  coordinate. That is the definition of coupled, and guessing single-frequency
  motion is the only general way out.
- The words *in phase*, *antiphase*, *symmetric*, *antisymmetric*, or a
  question asking for "the higher mode" or "the lowest mode". Count degrees of
  freedom first, then hunt for exactly that many frequencies.
- **Beats, energy transfer, or sloshing.** Any question about how long until
  the other oscillator has the energy is really asking for two mode frequencies
  and their difference. Find $\omega_-$ and $\omega_+$, subtract, then use
  $\pi/\Delta\omega$ for transfer and $2\pi/\Delta\omega$ for the full beat.
- A uniform line of $N$ identical masses and identical springs. Use the sine
  formula, not a determinant.
- Nothing attached to a wall. Expect a zero-frequency mode and use the reduced
  mass for the remaining one.
- A driven system where the question asks for an amplitude ratio or how to make
  something stop shaking. Write the undriven body's equation alone.

The general strategy never changes: choose coordinates measured from
equilibrium, write one equation of motion per coordinate, assume everything
goes as $\cos\omega t$, and demand that the resulting homogeneous system have a
nonzero solution. Symmetry, when the system has it, just lets you skip to the
answer.

The same idea — find the special shapes in which a whole extended system
oscillates coherently — is what makes fields tractable too. Next you use it on
space rather than on a handful of masses, in
[boundary-value problems in E&M](/guide/boundary-value-em).
