## The universal oscillator

Any system with a restoring force proportional to displacement oscillates
sinusoidally. The defining equation and its solution:

$$\ddot x = -\omega^2 x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$$

For a spring, $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$; for a simple pendulum,
$\omega = \sqrt{g/\ell}$. Notice the amplitude never appears: period is
amplitude-independent, the defining magic of SHM.

## The olympiad move: effective ω

Most USAPhO oscillation problems are "find the frequency of small
oscillations" in disguise. The recipe: find the equilibrium, expand the force
(or potential) to first order in the displacement, and read off $\omega^2$
from $\ddot x = -\omega^2 x$. Equivalently:

$$\omega^2 = \frac{1}{m}\left.\frac{d^2 U}{dx^2}\right|_{x_0}$$

<Problem
  id="np-shm-1"
  number={1}
  title="Quadrupling the mass"
  difficulty="Normal"
  answer={4}
  unit="s"
>
  A mass on a spring oscillates with a period of 2.0 s. The mass is
  quadrupled. What is the new period, in seconds?

  <Hint>
    $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$, so period scales as $\sqrt{m}$.
  </Hint>
  <Solution>
    $$T' = T\sqrt{\frac{4m}{m}} = 2T = 4 \text{ s}$$

    Amplitude is irrelevant, which is the whole point of SHM.
  </Solution>
</Problem>
