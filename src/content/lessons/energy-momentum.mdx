## Two laws that answer different questions

Momentum $\vec p = m\vec v$ is conserved whenever no external force acts, or
when the interaction is brief enough that external forces have no time to
matter. Mechanical energy is conserved only when nothing dissipative does
work. Collisions are exactly the case where those two conditions diverge,
which is why the topic is worth its own module.

$$W = \int \vec F \cdot d\vec x, \qquad K = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2, \qquad \vec p_i = \vec p_f$$

## Collisions

Elastic collisions conserve both momentum and kinetic energy. Perfectly
inelastic ones conserve momentum only, and lose the most energy possible while
still respecting it, which is not all of the energy unless the total momentum
is zero.

Two facts save a lot of algebra. In one dimension, an elastic collision
reverses the relative velocity:

$$v_{1i} - v_{2i} = -(v_{1f} - v_{2f})$$

And in two dimensions, equal masses colliding elastically always separate at
90°, which is why pool players can predict a cue ball.

## Center of mass

The center of mass of an isolated system moves at constant velocity no matter
what happens internally. An exploding projectile is the standard illustration:
the fragments scatter, but their center of mass continues along the original
parabola until a fragment lands.
