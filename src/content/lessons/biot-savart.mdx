## Field from a piece of current

There is no such thing as an isolated current element, but treating a current
as a sum of little pieces and adding their contributions works. Each element
$I\,d\vec{\ell}$ contributes a field at displacement $\vec{r}$ from it:

$$d\vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\vec{\ell}\times\hat{r}}{r^2}$$

with $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}$ T·m/A. The structure mirrors Coulomb's law —
an inverse square, a constant out front — with two differences that matter. The
source is a vector, and the cross product puts the field perpendicular to both
the current element and the line joining it to the field point. That is why
magnetic field lines circle a wire instead of radiating from it.

Two immediate consequences: an element contributes nothing along its own axis,
since $d\vec{\ell}\times\hat{r}$ vanishes when the two are parallel, and the
contribution is largest broadside.

## The straight wire

Integrate along an infinite straight wire at perpendicular distance $d$. Every
element pushes the field in the same circumferential direction, so the vector
integral becomes a scalar one, and it evaluates to

$$B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi d}$$

circling the wire by the right-hand rule. For a finite segment the same
integral, cut off at the ends, gives

$$B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi d}\left(\sin\theta_2 - \sin\theta_1\right)$$

with the angles measured from the perpendicular to the two ends. Keep this
form. Polygon problems — the field at the centre of a square or triangular
loop — are just this result applied once per side and added.

## On the axis of a loop

For a circular loop of radius $R$, at height $z$ on the axis, symmetry kills
every component except the axial one, and each element sits the same distance
from the field point:

$$B = \frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2\left(R^2 + z^2\right)^{3/2}}$$

At the centre this reduces to $\mu_0 I / 2R$. Far away it falls as $1/z^3$,
the dipole signature.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Do not integrate magnitudes. $dB$ is a vector, and adding $|d\vec{B}|$ over a
  loop gives a number several times too large. Choose axes first, argue from
  symmetry which components cancel, and integrate only the surviving component.
  Off the axis of a loop the components do not cancel and there is no closed
  form — that integral is elliptic, and an olympiad problem will not ask for it.
</Aside>

## When you actually need it

Ampere's law is faster whenever the geometry has enough symmetry to make
$\oint\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{\ell}$ trivial. Biot-Savart is what remains for
everything else: finite segments, loops, arcs, polygons, and any point where
the field magnitude is not constant along a convenient closed path. Reach for
symmetry first, and for this integral when symmetry runs out.
