## A charge near a conductor is a hard problem in disguise

Put a point charge $q$ a distance $d$ above a large grounded conducting plane.
The charge attracts electrons in the metal, they rearrange, and the induced
surface charge density is not uniform. That induced charge produces its own
field, which changes the potential everywhere, so integrating over an unknown
distribution to find the field looks circular.

What you actually know is a *boundary condition*: the plane is a grounded
conductor, so $V = 0$ everywhere on it. The method of images turns that single
fact into the whole answer.

## The method of images

Delete the conductor. Put a charge $-q$ at the mirror position, a distance $d$
on the far side of where the plane was. Two point charges, no metal, a problem
you already know how to solve.

Check the boundary. Any point on the old plane is equidistant from $+q$ and
$-q$, so the two potentials $kq/r$ and $-kq/r$ cancel exactly and $V = 0$
across the entire plane. The image reproduces the boundary condition the
conductor was enforcing.

Everything above the plane now follows. The force on the real charge is the
attraction to its image,

$$F = \frac{kq^2}{(2d)^2}$$

The field lines meet the plane perpendicularly, as they must at a conductor
surface, and the induced surface density is $\sigma = -\varepsilon_0 E$ at
each point, which integrates to exactly $-q$ in total.

For a **grounded sphere** of radius $R$ with a charge $q$ at distance $a$ from
its center, one image charge again suffices, but it is neither equal in
magnitude nor at the mirror point:

$$q' = -\frac{R}{a}q \qquad \text{at} \qquad b = \frac{R^2}{a}$$

placed on the line from the center to $q$. Those two numbers are exactly what
makes $V = 0$ over the whole sphere. If the sphere is insulated and neutral
rather than grounded, add a second image $+\tfrac{R}{a}q$ at the center: that
restores zero net charge while keeping the surface an equipotential, since a
charge at the center contributes the same potential everywhere on it.

## Why you are allowed to do this

The justification is the **uniqueness theorem**: in a region where the charge
distribution is specified and the potential is specified on every boundary,
the solution to Laplace's or Poisson's equation is unique.

So the reasoning is not a lucky guess but a proof. The image configuration
satisfies Poisson's equation in the region of interest with the same real
charge $q$, and it matches the same boundary condition $V = 0$ on the
conductor. By uniqueness it must be the solution, and there is nothing left to
verify.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  The image solution is valid *only in the region containing the real charge*,
  outside the conductor. Inside the conductor the true field is zero, while
  the image construction would predict a nonzero field from a charge that does
  not exist there. A common error is computing the interaction energy as
  $-kq^2/2d$, the energy of two real charges. The correct work to bring the
  charge in from infinity is half that, $-kq^2/4d$, because the image moves as
  the real charge moves rather than sitting fixed.
</Aside>
