## Three ways to shed instability

A nucleus is protons and neutrons held by the strong force against electrical
repulsion. When the balance is wrong the nucleus rearranges itself, and there
are three standard routes. Write the nucleus as $^A_Z X$, with $A$ nucleons and
$Z$ protons.

**Alpha decay** ejects a helium nucleus. Heavy nuclei do this because the
Coulomb repulsion across a large nucleus outgrows the short-range strong force.

$$^A_Z X \to\ ^{A-4}_{Z-2} Y + ^4_2\alpha$$

**Beta-minus decay** converts a neutron into a proton, emitting an electron and
an antineutrino. Nothing is knocked out of the nucleus — the electron is
created at the moment of decay.

$$n \to p + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e, \qquad ^A_Z X \to\ ^{\ \ A}_{Z+1} Y + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e$$

Beta-plus decay runs the other way, a proton becoming a neutron plus a positron
and a neutrino, lowering $Z$ by one.

**Gamma decay** emits a photon from an excited nucleus and changes neither $A$
nor $Z$. It usually follows an alpha or beta decay that left the daughter
excited.

In every case the sums of $A$ and of $Z$ must balance across the arrow. That
bookkeeping alone identifies most daughter nuclei.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  A beta particle is not an orbital electron, and beta decay does not ionise
  the atom in the ordinary sense. Also, the antineutrino is not optional
  padding — without it, energy and angular momentum both fail to balance, and
  the observed beta energy spectrum would be a single line rather than a
  continuum.
</Aside>

## The decay law

Each nucleus decays independently, with a fixed probability per unit time. It
does not age and it does not remember. That gives

$$\frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N \quad \Longrightarrow \quad N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$$

where $\lambda$ is the decay constant. Setting $N = N_0/2$ gives the half-life:

$$T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} \approx \frac{0.693}{\lambda}$$

The mean lifetime is $\tau = 1/\lambda$, longer than the half-life by a factor
of 1.44. Both appear in problems; check which one a stated number is.

## Activity

Activity is the count of decays per second, $A = \lambda N$, measured in
becquerels. It obeys the same exponential, since it is proportional to $N$:

$$A(t) = A_0 e^{-\lambda t} = A_0 \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/T_{1/2}}$$

The second form is faster by hand when the elapsed time is a tidy multiple of
the half-life. Three half-lives leave one eighth; ten leave about a thousandth.

Note that a long half-life means a low activity for a given sample. Uranium-238
is barely radioactive per gram precisely because it lasts billions of years.
Short-lived isotopes are the dangerous ones while they last.
