## A constant total, traded back and forth

A mass on a spring carries two kinds of energy. Stretching stores potential
energy in the spring; moving carries kinetic energy in the mass. With no
friction their sum never changes:

$$E = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2$$

The right-hand form comes from evaluating $E$ at the turning point, where
$x = A$ and $v = 0$. That single line is the most useful equation in
oscillations, because it relates position and speed without ever mentioning
time.

Evaluating instead at the center, where $x = 0$, gives the maximum speed
directly: $v_{\max} = A\sqrt{k/m} = \omega A$.

## Energy goes as amplitude squared

$E = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2$ says the stored energy scales with the *square* of the
amplitude. Doubling the swing costs four times the energy. This is why damping
looks the way it does: a system losing a fixed fraction of its energy per cycle
loses only half that fraction of its amplitude.

The same quadratic shows up in waves, in circuit oscillations, and in radiated
power. It is a property of the potential $U = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$, not of springs
specifically.

## Averages over a cycle

Substitute $x = A\cos\omega t$ into the two energies:

$$U = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2\cos^2\omega t, \qquad K = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2\sin^2\omega t$$

Each oscillates, and the average of $\cos^2$ or $\sin^2$ over a full cycle is
$\tfrac{1}{2}$. So

$$\langle U \rangle = \langle K \rangle = \tfrac{1}{4}kA^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}E$$

On average the energy splits evenly. Note also the frequency: both $\cos^2$ and
$\sin^2$ complete two cycles for every one of the motion, so the energies
oscillate at $2\omega$ while the mass oscillates at $\omega$.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Half the total energy is not the same as half the time spent fast. The mass
  moves slowly near the turning points and quickly through the center, so it
  spends more of each cycle out near the extremes. Equal *average* energies do
  not mean the oscillator is kinetic-dominated half the time.
</Aside>
