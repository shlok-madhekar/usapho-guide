## A definition you can compute with

Heat is not a state function, but heat divided by the temperature at which it
is transferred turns out to be. Along a reversible path,

$$dS = \frac{dQ_{rev}}{T}, \qquad \Delta S = \int_i^f \frac{dQ_{rev}}{T}$$

The subscript matters. The formula only defines $\Delta S$ when evaluated
along a reversible path; the temperature in the denominator has to be a
well-defined temperature of the system, which an irreversible process does not
have.

Two cases cover most of what you will need. At constant temperature the
integral is trivial, $\Delta S = Q/T$ — this is the melting-ice case and the
isothermal-expansion case. For an ideal gas going anywhere at all, substitute
$dQ = nC_V\,dT + P\,dV$ and integrate:

$$\Delta S = nC_V\ln\frac{T_f}{T_i} + nR\ln\frac{V_f}{V_i}$$

Two logs. Memorize the shape, and note that it depends only on the endpoints.

## Invent a path

Because $S$ is a state function, that last observation is a licence. An
irreversible process — a free expansion, two blocks brought into contact, a
gas stirred by a paddle — has no reversible path of its own, but you do not
need one. Identify the initial and final states, then compute along any
reversible path connecting them.

For a free expansion of an ideal gas from $V$ to $2V$, no heat flows and no
work is done, so $T$ is unchanged. Replace it with a slow isothermal expansion
between the same two states: $\Delta S = nR\ln 2 > 0$. The gas gained entropy
even though $Q = 0$ in the actual process, because $Q = 0$ was true of the
real path, not the substitute one.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Setting $\Delta S = 0$ for a free expansion because no heat was exchanged.
  The formula requires a reversible path, and free expansion is the textbook
  irreversible process. Whenever a process is sudden, unresisted, or involves
  a finite temperature difference, build a reversible substitute first.
</Aside>

## Why heat flows one way

Nothing in the first law forbids a cold block spontaneously warming a hot one;
energy would still balance. What forbids it is that the total entropy would
drop. Move $Q$ from a body at $T_H$ to one at $T_C$:

$$\Delta S_{total} = \frac{Q}{T_C} - \frac{Q}{T_H}$$

This is positive when $T_H > T_C$ and negative if you try to run it backwards.
The same expression tells you the direction is only marginal when the two
temperatures are nearly equal — which is exactly the condition for a transfer
to be reversible, and exactly why reversible engines have to run infinitely
slowly.
