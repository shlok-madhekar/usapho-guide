Physics is the habit of asking how big something is. Before you can ask that,
you have to agree on what "big" means — and that agreement is the whole content
of units. It sounds like bookkeeping. It isn't. Units carry real information
about how the world is put together, and if you learn to listen to them they
will hand you the shape of an answer before you have written a single line of
algebra.

Here is the promise, and it is not an exaggeration. You will be able to work
out how the period of a swinging pendulum depends on its length without knowing
any mechanics, and estimate the number of piano tuners in Chicago to within a
factor of two without looking anything up. Both come from the same two habits.
This is the first lesson of the course because everything after it quietly
assumes you have these.

## Dimensions are not units

A **unit** is a particular agreed-upon amount: the metre, the foot, the second,
the hour. A **dimension** is the kind of thing being measured: length, time,
mass. The metre and the foot are different units of the same dimension.

Almost all of mechanics is built from three dimensions. Write them as $M$ for
mass, $L$ for length, and $T$ for time. Every other mechanical quantity is a
product of powers of these, and you can read the combination straight off the
definition. Speed is a distance over a time, so its dimensions are $LT^{-1}$.
Acceleration is a speed over a time, so $LT^{-2}$. Force is mass times
acceleration, so $MLT^{-2}$. Energy is a force times a distance, so
$ML^2T^{-2}$. Pressure is a force over an area, so $MLT^{-2}/L^2 = ML^{-1}T^{-2}$.
Density is mass over volume: $ML^{-3}$.

The notation $[X]$ means "the dimensions of $X$". So $[F] = MLT^{-2}$.

Some quantities have no dimensions at all: a pure ratio like $L_1/L_2$, an
angle in radians (it's an arc length divided by a radius), $\pi$, or the number
$2$. These are called **dimensionless**, and they are the one thing this whole
method is blind to.

Two rules do all the work.

First, **both sides of any correct equation have the same dimensions**, and so
does every term inside a sum. You cannot add a length to a time, any more than
you can add three metres to Tuesday. Second, **the argument of $\sin$, $\cos$,
$\exp$, or $\log$ must be dimensionless**. Think about why: $e^x = 1 + x +
x^2/2 + \dots$, and that sum adds $x$ to $x^2$. Those can only be added if $x$
has no dimensions at all.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Dimensions live in the physics; units live in the notation. When you check an
  expression you can work in either. $[\,\sqrt{L/g}\,] = \sqrt{L/(LT^{-2})} =
  \sqrt{T^2} = T$, or equivalently $\sqrt{\text{m}/(\text{m}/\text{s}^2)} =
  \text{s}$. Same statement.
</Aside>

## Using dimensions to check

The cheapest use of all this is as an error detector. You derive an expression,
you put dimensions in, and if the two sides disagree you have found a mistake
without checking a single algebraic step.

Suppose you produce $T = 2\pi\sqrt{g/L}$ for a pendulum period. Check:
$g/L$ has dimensions $LT^{-2}/L = T^{-2}$, so the square root is $T^{-1}$ —
a per-second, not a second. The fraction is upside down. Ten seconds of work,
and you caught it.

This check is honest but limited in one way that trips people up. A statement
like "$T$ is proportional to $\sqrt{r}$" has a hidden constant of
proportionality, and that constant will absorb whatever dimensions it needs to.
Proportionalities can never be checked dimensionally. Only full expressions can.

## Using dimensions to build an answer

Now the useful part. If you know *which* quantities an answer can depend on,
dimensions often pin down the answer up to a pure number.

The recipe: write the unknown as a product of powers of the given quantities
with unknown exponents, demand that the dimensions match, and solve the
resulting small linear system — one equation for $M$, one for $L$, one for $T$.

Take a wave on a guitar string. Physically its speed can only be set by the
tension $T_s$ in the string and the mass per unit length $\mu$. (Calling
tension $T_s$ here so it doesn't collide with $T$ for the time dimension.)
Their dimensions are $[T_s] = MLT^{-2}$ and $[\mu] = ML^{-1}$. Write

$$v = C\,T_s^{a}\,\mu^{b}$$

with $C$ a pure number. Matching dimensions:

$$LT^{-1} = (MLT^{-2})^a (ML^{-1})^b = M^{a+b}\,L^{a-b}\,T^{-2a}$$

Now read off one equation per dimension. Mass: $0 = a+b$. Length: $1 = a-b$.
Time: $-1 = -2a$, so $a = 1/2$ immediately. Then $b = -1/2$ from the mass
equation, and the length equation $1 = 1/2 - (-1/2) = 1$ checks out — a free
consistency test that the answer exists at all. So

$$v = C\sqrt{T_s/\mu}$$

<Figure caption={<><b>Figure 2.</b> The three dimension equations, one per row. The orange
bar is what your exponents give; the outlined marker is what a speed needs. Start from
a = 1, b = 0 (that is just the tension, which is a force) and watch all three rows
disagree at once. Then walk a down to 0.5 and b down to -0.5 and see them lock.</>}>
  <Sim id="units-estimation-exponents" />
</Figure>

and the true answer has $C=1$. With $T_s = 80.0$ N and $\mu = 5.00\times10^{-3}$
kg/m:

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{80.0}{5.00\times10^{-3}}} = \sqrt{1.60\times10^{4}} = 126\ \text{m/s}$$

Notice what you got for free even before the numbers: doubling the tension does
not double the speed, it multiplies it by $\sqrt{2}$. That's physics, extracted
from nothing but bookkeeping.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  The method is blind to dimensionless factors, and that includes ratios built
  from the problem itself. If two lengths $L_1$ and $L_2$ appear, then
  $\sqrt{L_1/g}$ and $\sqrt{L_1/g}\cdot f(L_1/L_2)$ have identical dimensions
  for *any* function $f$, so the answer is not unique. The method also can't
  produce $2\pi$ or $\sqrt{1.4}$. Problems that want you to use it will tell
  you the numerical factor, or tell you to take it as $1$. If a problem gives
  you exactly one quantity per dimension you need, the combination is unique.
</Aside>

## Worked example: the Planck length

Three constants, three dimensions, one length. Take $\hbar = 1.0546\times
10^{-34}$ J·s, $G = 6.674\times10^{-11}$ m³kg⁻¹s⁻², and $c = 2.998\times10^{8}$
m/s.

First, dimensions. A joule is $ML^2T^{-2}$, so $[\hbar] = ML^2T^{-1}$. From the
units given, $[G] = L^3M^{-1}T^{-2}$. And $[c] = LT^{-1}$. Write
$\ell = \hbar^{a}G^{b}c^{d}$ and match:

$$L = (ML^2T^{-1})^a (L^3M^{-1}T^{-2})^b (LT^{-1})^d$$

Mass: $0 = a - b$. Length: $1 = 2a + 3b + d$. Time: $0 = -a - 2b - d$.

From the mass equation $b = a$. Put that into the time equation:
$0 = -a - 2a - d$, so $d = -3a$. Put both into the length equation:
$1 = 2a + 3a - 3a = 2a$, so $a = 1/2$, hence $b = 1/2$ and $d = -3/2$.

$$\ell_P = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar G}{c^3}}$$

Numbers. The top is $(1.0546\times10^{-34})(6.674\times10^{-11}) =
7.038\times10^{-45}$. The bottom is $(2.998\times10^{8})^3 = 2.694\times10^{25}$.
The ratio is $2.612\times10^{-70}$, and its square root is
$1.62\times10^{-35}$ m.

When taking a square root of a power of ten, make the exponent even first:
$2.612\times10^{-70} = 26.12\times10^{-71}$ is awkward, but $-70$ is already
even, so $\sqrt{2.612}\times10^{-35} = 1.616\times10^{-35}$. Do that and you
will stop losing factors of ten here.

## Converting units without guessing

Never try to remember whether to multiply or divide. Instead multiply by
fractions that equal one, and cancel.

A car at $65$ miles per hour, with $1\ \text{mile} = 1609.344$ m and
$1\ \text{h} = 3600$ s:

$$

65\ \frac{\text{mi}}{\text{h}} \times \frac{1609.344\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{mi}}
\times \frac{1\ \text{h}}{3600\ \text{s}} = \frac{65 \times 1609.344}{3600}
= \frac{104607}{3600} = 29.1\ \text{m/s}

$$

The "mi" cancels top and bottom, so does the "h", and only m/s survives. If you
had set a fraction the wrong way up you would have been left with mi²/h², and
you'd see it.

Powers need care. Water at $1.00\ \text{g/cm}^3$: you need
$1\ \text{g} = 10^{-3}$ kg and $1\ \text{cm} = 10^{-2}$ m, so
$1\ \text{cm}^3 = (10^{-2}\ \text{m})^3 = 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3$. Then

$$

1.00\ \frac{\text{g}}{\text{cm}^3} \times \frac{10^{-3}\ \text{kg}}{1\ \text{g}}
\times \frac{1\ \text{cm}^3}{10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3} = \frac{10^{-3}}{10^{-6}}
= 10^{3}\ \text{kg/m}^3

$$

The factor is $10^3$, not $10^2$ — cubing the length conversion is exactly
where people lose it.

## Scaling arguments

Often you don't want a number — you want a ratio. Then you don't need any
constants at all, because they cancel.

Two hailstones have the same shape and density, but one is eight times heavier.
Drag is $F = \tfrac12 C\rho A v^2$. At terminal speed drag balances weight:
$mg = \tfrac12 C\rho A v^2$, so $v^2 \propto m/A$.

Same density means mass goes as volume, so $m \propto r^3$ where $r$ is any
length across the stone. Eight times the mass means $r$ is doubled, since
$2^3 = 8$. Same shape means area goes as $r^2$, so the area is $4$ times
larger. Then

$$

\frac{v_2^2}{v_1^2} = \frac{m_2/A_2}{m_1/A_1} = \frac{8}{4} = 2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{v_2}{v_1} = \sqrt{2} = 1.41

$$

The bigger stone falls only $41\%$ faster despite being eight times heavier.
The same move handles orbits: if $T^2 \propto r^3$ and $r$ goes up by $4$, then
$T^2$ goes up by $4^3 = 64$, so $T$ goes up by $8$.

## Estimation and Fermi chains

Sometimes you have no formula and no data, only a question: how many piano
tuners work in Chicago? The trick is to break the answer into a chain of
factors you can each guess within a factor of a few. Errors in the factors are
independent and partly cancel, so the product usually lands within a factor of
ten of the truth.

Population $2.7\times10^6$, at $2.5$ people per household, gives
$2.7\times10^6/2.5 = 1.08\times10^6$ households. One in twenty owns a piano:
$1.08\times10^6/20 = 5.4\times10^4$ pianos. Each tuned once a year, so
$5.4\times10^4$ tunings per year. A tuner does $4$ a day for $250$ days, which
is $1000$ tunings a year. So

$$\frac{5.4\times10^4}{1.0\times10^3} = 54\ \text{tuners}$$

The structure matters more than the arithmetic: each step converts one unit
into the next (people → households → pianos → tunings per year → tuners), and
the units cancel down the chain exactly as they did in the speed conversion.

The habit worth building is to guess the magnitude *before* you solve. When the
algebra later produces a car accelerating at $10^4\ \text{m/s}^2$, you notice.

## Anchor numbers

Estimates need somewhere to start. Carry these:
$g \approx 10\ \text{m/s}^2$; air density $\approx 1.2\ \text{kg/m}^3$; water
density $10^3\ \text{kg/m}^3$; atmospheric pressure $\approx 10^5$ Pa; speed of
sound $\approx 340$ m/s; Earth's radius $6.4\times10^6$ m; a year is about
$\pi\times10^7$ s (really $3.15\times10^7$); Avogadro's number
$6.0\times10^{23}$; a mole of gas at room conditions fills about $0.022\ \text{m}^3$.

Two more habits. Report answers with a sensible number of significant figures:
a product carries as many as its *least* precise input, so $2.50\ \text{m}
\times 3.1\ \text{m} = 7.75 \to 7.8\ \text{m}^2$, two figures, because $3.1$
has two. And on a graded exam, an answer with the wrong units earns nothing, so
check them last, every time.

## How to spot that this is the technique

Reach for dimensional analysis when a problem says an answer "depends only on"
a short list of quantities, or gives you an expression like $F = C\rho^a A^b
v^c$ and asks for an exponent, or hands you a dimensionless factor
($\sqrt{\gamma}$, $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$, "take the constant to be 1"). That gifted
factor is the tell: the problem is telling you it knows you can only get the
answer up to a pure number.

Reach for a scaling argument when the problem gives a ratio instead of a value
— "eight times the mass", "four times the radius" — and asks by what factor
something changes. Never compute both cases; divide.

Reach for estimation when the problem supplies a pile of everyday facts with no
governing equation in sight, and asks "how many" or "estimate". Build the chain
and let the units guide each link.
