## Physics in a lurching bus

Newton's second law only holds in an inertial frame. Stand in a bus that brakes
hard and, from your own point of view, a bag on a smooth floor accelerates
forward with nothing touching it. Either the law fails, or the frame is wrong.

The standard resolution is to keep the law and pay a fee. In a frame
accelerating at $\vec{a}_f$, add to every mass $m$ a pseudo-force

$$\vec{F}_{\text{pseudo}} = -m\vec{a}_f$$

directed opposite the frame's acceleration. With that term included,
$\sum \vec{F} = m\vec{a}'$ works exactly as usual, where $\vec{a}'$ is the
acceleration measured inside the frame. The bag has no real forward force; the
pseudo-force accounts for the bus decelerating out from under it.

Pseudo-forces are proportional to mass and have no source object — no third-law
partner. That is what marks them as bookkeeping rather than interaction.

## The rotating case

A frame rotating at $\omega$ accelerates toward the centre at $\omega^2 r$, so
the pseudo-force points outward:

$$F_{\text{cf}} = m\omega^2 r$$

This is centrifugal force, and it exists only in the rotating frame. On a
carousel you feel flung outward; a friend on the ground sees the floor pushing
you inward and nothing else. Both descriptions are correct in their own frames.

Never mix them. Writing centripetal *and* centrifugal force into one equation
double-counts and yields nonsense.

## When it pays

The accelerating frame is worth the trouble when it turns motion into
statics.

A pendulum hangs in a truck accelerating at $a$. In the ground frame it has a
horizontal acceleration and you must resolve tension into components and solve
two coupled equations. In the truck's frame the bob simply hangs at rest under
gravity plus a backward pseudo-force $ma$; the string lines up with their sum,
and $\tan\theta = a/g$ falls out in one line. The combination $\sqrt{a^2+g^2}$
behaves as an effective gravity for everything inside — including the period of
that pendulum.

Use the same trick for a block on an accelerating wedge, or for a bead in a
spinning tube, where the ground frame requires tracking a moving contact point.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  A frame moving in a circle at constant speed is still non-inertial — its
  velocity changes direction. And $\vec{a}_f$ means the frame's acceleration,
  not the object's; the sign error from confusing the two flips the pseudo-force
  and quietly doubles the answer.
</Aside>
