## A shortcut past the middle of the problem

A child starts at rest at the top of a slide 3.2 m high and arrives at the
bottom moving. If you attack that with forces you need the shape of the slide,
the angle at every point, the normal force at every point, and an integration
you cannot do by hand. Yet the answer is one line long, and it does not depend
on the shape of the slide at all.

That is what conservation of energy buys you. It ignores the middle of the
problem. You take a snapshot at the start, a snapshot at the end, and demand
that one number match. Everything that happened in between — the curving, the
speeding up, the changing direction — never has to be described.

The previous lesson, [conservative forces and potential energy](/guide/potential-energy),
built the tool: a force is conservative when the work it does depends only on
where you start and where you finish, and for such a force you can define a
potential energy $U$ whose drop equals the work done. Gravity near the ground
gives $U_g = mgy$. An ideal spring gives $U_s = \tfrac12 k x^2$. This lesson is
about using those to run a problem from end to end.

## The ledger

Start from the work-energy theorem, which you already have: the total work
done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy,

$$W_{\text{total}} = \Delta K = \tfrac12 m v_f^2 - \tfrac12 m v_i^2 .$$

Here $K = \tfrac12 m v^2$ is the kinetic energy in joules, $m$ the mass in kg,
$v$ the speed in m/s. Now split the total work into two piles: work done by
conservative forces, and work done by everything else.

$$W_{\text{cons}} + W_{\text{other}} = \Delta K .$$

The whole point of a potential energy is that $W_{\text{cons}} = -\Delta U$.
Gravity doing $+50$ J of work on a falling rock is the same statement as the
gravitational potential energy dropping by 50 J. Substitute that in and move it
across:

$$W_{\text{other}} = \Delta K + \Delta U .$$

Define the **mechanical energy** $E = K + U$, the sum of kinetic and all
potential energies. Then

$$\boxed{\,E_f = E_i + W_{\text{other}}\,}$$

Read that as an account statement. $E_i$ is what you start with. $W_{\text{other}}$
is everything deposited or withdrawn by forces that have no potential energy —
friction, air drag, a hand pushing, a rope being pulled by a motor. $E_f$ is
what you end with. When no such force does work, $W_{\text{other}} = 0$ and

$$\tfrac12 m v_i^2 + U_i = \tfrac12 m v_f^2 + U_f .$$

Two snapshots, one equation. Notice what is *not* in it: time, distance along
the path, the shape of the track, or the direction of motion. Energy is a
scalar. It has no components and no signs to chase.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  The zero of potential energy is yours to choose. Put $y = 0$ at the bottom of
  the slide, at the top, at sea level — the answer cannot depend on it, because
  only the *difference* $U_f - U_i$ ever appears. Choose the level that makes
  the most terms vanish, then write it down explicitly so you do not forget it
  halfway through.
</Aside>

**Worked example 1.** A 25 kg child starts from rest at the top of a
frictionless slide 3.2 m high. How fast is she moving at the bottom?

Put $y = 0$ at the bottom. Snapshot one: $v_i = 0$, $y_i = 3.2$ m. Snapshot
two: $v_f = ?$, $y_f = 0$. Nothing but gravity and the normal force acts, and
the normal force is perpendicular to the motion so it does no work. So

$$\tfrac12 m v_i^2 + m g y_i = \tfrac12 m v_f^2 + m g y_f$$

$$0 + m g (3.2) = \tfrac12 m v_f^2 + 0 .$$

Every term has an $m$, so cancel it. This happens constantly, and it is why
the mass of the child was never needed.

$$v_f = \sqrt{2 g h} = \sqrt{2(9.8)(3.2)} = \sqrt{62.72} = 7.92\ \text{m/s}.$$

The same single line handles a dropped stone ($h = 5.0$ m gives 9.90 m/s), a
pendulum bob released 0.45 m above its lowest point (2.97 m/s), and a
skateboarder who enters a bowl at 8.0 m/s and rises until all her kinetic
energy is potential: $h = v^2/2g = 64/19.6 = 3.27$ m. Different pictures, one
equation.

## When energy leaks out

$W_{\text{other}}$ is usually friction, and friction is the one non-conservative
force you will meet constantly. A kinetic friction force of magnitude
$f_k = \mu_k N$ opposes the motion at every instant, so over a path of length
$d$ it does work $-f_k d$. The minus sign is the whole story: friction always
withdraws from the account.

Notice the $d$. Unlike gravity, friction cares about the *length of the path*,
not the displacement. Take a longer route and you lose more. That is exactly
the property that makes it non-conservative, and it means you cannot define a
"friction potential energy".

**Worked example 2.** A 1.0 kg block slides down a frictionless ramp from 2.0 m
high, then crosses a rough floor with $\mu_k = 0.40$. How far does it go before
stopping?

Take the two snapshots as far apart as possible: at the top of the ramp, and
where the block stops. Both have $v = 0$, so both kinetic energies are zero.
There is no need to find the speed at the bottom of the ramp at all.

$$E_f - E_i = W_{\text{other}}$$
$$(0 + 0) - (0 + mgh) = -\mu_k m g d$$
$$mgh = \mu_k m g d \quad\Rightarrow\quad d = \frac{h}{\mu_k} = \frac{2.0}{0.40} = 5.0\ \text{m}.$$

On the flat floor the normal force is $N = mg$, which is why $f_k = \mu_k mg$.
On an incline it is not: there $N = mg\cos\theta$, and forgetting the cosine is
the most common way this goes wrong.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Energy conservation is not a law you may apply through a collision. When a
  bullet embeds in a block, or two carts couple together, kinetic energy
  disappears into deformation and heat and you have no way to compute how much.
  Use momentum for the impact — $p = mv$ is conserved because the impact is too
  brief for external forces to matter — and then energy for the smooth motion
  before and after. A 10 g bullet at 400 m/s into a 1.99 kg block gives
  $v = (0.010)(400)/2.00 = 2.0$ m/s by momentum. Try to get that from energy
  and you will get a badly wrong number with no warning.
</Aside>

**Worked example 3.** A 2.0 kg block is released from rest on a $35^\circ$
incline, slides 1.5 m down against $\mu_k = 0.20$, and then compresses a spring
of stiffness $k = 2000$ N/m mounted along the incline. Find the maximum
compression $x$.

Take snapshot one at release and snapshot two at maximum compression. At
maximum compression the block is momentarily at rest, so both kinetic energies
are zero again. Between them the block travels a total distance $1.5 + x$ along
the incline, dropping a height $(1.5 + x)\sin 35^\circ$, and friction acts over
that whole distance including the part inside the spring. That "$+x$" is the
part people drop.

Gravity releases $mg\sin\theta\,(1.5+x)$. With $mg = 19.6$ N and
$\sin 35^\circ = 0.5736$, that coefficient is $19.6 \times 0.5736 = 11.24$ N.
Friction takes $\mu_k m g\cos\theta\,(1.5+x)$, and with
$\cos 35^\circ = 0.8192$ that coefficient is
$0.20 \times 19.6 \times 0.8192 = 3.21$ N. The spring stores $\tfrac12 k x^2$.
So

$$(11.24 - 3.21)(1.5 + x) = \tfrac12 (2000) x^2$$
$$8.03(1.5 + x) = 1000 x^2$$
$$1000x^2 - 8.03x - 12.05 = 0 .$$

Quadratic formula: $x = \dfrac{8.03 + \sqrt{8.03^2 + 4(1000)(12.05)}}{2000}
= \dfrac{8.03 + \sqrt{64.5 + 48200}}{2000} = \dfrac{8.03 + 219.6}{2000}
= 0.114\ \text{m}.$

Take the positive root; the negative one describes a spring stretched the wrong
way, which the setup does not allow.

## When the track pushes back: loops and spheres

Some problems need one more equation, because energy alone cannot tell you
whether an object is still touching the track. Energy gives you speed; contact
is a statement about the normal force, and for that you need Newton's second
law in the radial direction.

At the top of a vertical loop of radius $R$, both gravity and the normal force
point down, toward the centre, and together they supply the centripetal force:

$$N + mg = \frac{m v_{\text{top}}^2}{R}.$$

The track can only push, never pull, so $N \ge 0$. The slowest the object can
go and still be held on the circle is the case $N = 0$, where gravity alone
does the job:

$$v_{\text{top}}^2 = gR .$$

That is the condition. Now, and only now, bring in energy to find where it must
start. Releasing from rest at height $h$ above the bottom of the loop, on a
frictionless track:

$$mgh = \tfrac12 m v_{\text{top}}^2 + mg(2R) .$$

Substitute $v_{\text{top}}^2 = gR$, cancel $m$, and divide by $g$:

$$h = \tfrac12 R + 2R = 2.5R .$$

**Worked example 4.** For a loop of radius 2 m with $g = 10$ m/s², the minimum
release height is $h = 2.5(2) = 5$ m. For $R = 2.5$ m it is 6.25 m. The answer
is always $2.5R$ and never involves the mass.

If friction dissipates a known amount $Q$ around the loop, the ledger just
gains one term. A 0.40 kg bead needing to clear a loop of $R = 0.50$ m while
losing 0.30 J to friction needs, at the bottom,

$$\tfrac12 m v_b^2 = \tfrac12 m (gR) + mg(2R) + Q = \tfrac12(0.40)(9.8)(0.50) + (0.40)(9.8)(1.0) + 0.30$$
$$\tfrac12(0.40)v_b^2 = 0.98 + 3.92 + 0.30 = 5.20 \quad\Rightarrow\quad v_b^2 = 26.0,\ v_b = 5.10\ \text{m/s}.$$

The block sliding off a sphere is the same idea turned inside out. On the
outside of a sphere of radius $R$, gravity points partly inward and the normal
force points outward, so at an angle $\theta$ from the top,

$$mg\cos\theta - N = \frac{mv^2}{R},$$

and the block leaves when $N = 0$, that is when $v^2 = gR\cos\theta$. Energy
supplies the other relation: it has fallen a height $R(1 - \cos\theta)$, so
$v^2 = 2gR(1-\cos\theta)$. Set them equal:

$$2gR(1 - \cos\theta) = gR\cos\theta \quad\Rightarrow\quad \cos\theta = \tfrac23 .$$

The block always leaves at the height $\tfrac23 R$ above the centre, whatever
$R$ and whatever $m$. For $R = 1.6$ m that is 1.07 m. Its speed there is
$v = \sqrt{gR\cos\theta} = \sqrt{2gR/3}$; for $R = 2.4$ m,
$v = \sqrt{2(9.8)(2.4)/3} = \sqrt{15.68} = 3.96$ m/s.

## More than one moving thing

When several objects move, put every one of them in the same ledger. The
kinetic energy term becomes a sum, and so does the potential energy term.

**Worked example 5.** A 3.0 kg block on a frictionless table is joined over a
massless pulley to a 2.0 kg hanging block. Find their common speed after the
hanging block descends 0.80 m from rest.

The rope is inextensible, so both blocks always move at the same speed $v$.
Only the hanging block changes height. The rope tension does positive work on
one block and exactly the same amount of negative work on the other, so it
cancels out of the total — which is why treating the two blocks as one system
is worth doing.

$$m_2 g d = \tfrac12 (m_1 + m_2) v^2$$
$$(2.0)(9.8)(0.80) = \tfrac12 (5.0) v^2$$
$$15.68 = 2.5 v^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad v^2 = 6.272,\ v = 2.50\ \text{m/s}.$$

Two more wrinkles show up in harder versions of this. First, if the system can
recoil — a block sliding down a wedge that is itself free to slide — momentum
in the free direction is conserved too, and you need both equations, because
the wedge carries away some of the kinetic energy. Second, for an extended
object like a chain or a rope, the gravitational potential energy is
$Mgy_{\text{cm}}$, using the height of the **centre of mass**.

**Worked example 6.** A uniform chain of length 1.8 m lies along a frictionless
table with one end at the edge, and slides off over a smooth peg. How fast is
it moving when the last link leaves the table?

You cannot track the links one at a time. But you do not have to: the whole
chain always moves at one speed, so $K = \tfrac12 M v^2$ no matter how it is
draped. Initially it lies flat on the table, so its centre of mass is at table
height, $y_{\text{cm},i} = 0$. Finally it hangs straight down from the edge, a
length 1.8 m, so its centre of mass is at $-0.9$ m. The drop is $L/2$:

$$Mg\frac{L}{2} = \tfrac12 M v^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{gL} = \sqrt{(9.8)(1.8)} = \sqrt{17.64} = 4.2\ \text{m/s}.$$

The mass cancels again, and the answer is just the free-fall speed for a drop
of $L/2$.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  A leaky bucket, a chain piling onto the floor, a rocket — anything shedding or
  gathering mass — is not a closed energy account. Material that lands and stops
  dead loses its kinetic energy to the impact, exactly like a collision. If a
  problem says mass leaves or joins the moving system, do not assume mechanical
  energy is conserved. Write momentum or force equations for the mass transfer
  and use energy only on the stretches where the system is intact.
</Aside>

## How to recognise that this is the technique

Reach for energy conservation when a problem gives you **two states and asks
for a speed, a height, a compression, or a distance** connecting them, and says
nothing about time. The giveaway phrases: "released from rest", "how fast at
the bottom", "how high does it rise", "maximum compression", "how far before it
stops". If the word *time* or *acceleration* appears in the question, energy is
probably the wrong tool and kinematics or $F = ma$ is the right one.

A second signal is a track whose shape you are not told. A curved slide, a
bowl, a ski jump, a rope swing — the problem is deliberately withholding the
geometry because the answer does not need it. That is energy telling you what
it is.

The procedure, every time: pick your two snapshots and put them as far apart as
you can, choose and write down $y = 0$, list every energy term at each snapshot
including the zeros, then add $W_{\text{other}}$ for friction or an applied
force. If the problem asks whether something stays on a track, you will need
one extra line from Newton's second law in the radial direction. If it contains
a collision or a sudden grab, split the problem at that instant and use
momentum across it.

What energy will never tell you is how long anything took, or how hard you had
to work per second to do it. That question — the rate of energy transfer — is
what [power](/guide/power) is for.
