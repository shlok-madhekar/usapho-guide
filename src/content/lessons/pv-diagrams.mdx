A gas sealed under a piston is a machine with exactly two knobs. You can change
how much room it has, and you can change how hot it is. Everything else — the
pressure it pushes with, the energy stored inside it, the work it delivers to
the crankshaft — follows from those two. So if you draw a picture with volume
across the bottom and pressure up the side, one dot on that picture is the
complete story of the gas at one instant, and a curve is the complete story of
a process.

That picture is the PV diagram, and it is the single most useful drawing in
thermodynamics. Once you can read it, a question like "how much heat does this
engine throw away every cycle" stops being a wall of algebra and becomes a walk
around a closed loop, one leg at a time, with a small amount of arithmetic at
each corner.

The last lesson, [entropy](/guide/entropy), gave you the rule that decides which
processes can happen at all. This one is the bookkeeping: given a cycle that
does happen, how much work comes out and how much heat goes in.

## One dot is one state

Every point on a PV diagram satisfies the ideal gas law,

$$PV = nRT$$

where $n$ is the number of moles, $R = 8.31$ J/mol·K is the gas constant, and
$T$ is the absolute temperature in kelvin. Read that as a statement about the
diagram: if you know where the dot is, you know $T$, because $T = PV/nR$. You
never have to be told the temperature at a corner. You can always compute it.

Try it on a corner you will meet again. A state at $P = 2.0\times10^5$ Pa and
$V = 1.0\times10^{-3}$ m³ holding $0.50$ mol has

$$T = \frac{PV}{nR} = \frac{(2.0\times10^5)(1.0\times10^{-3})}{(0.50)(8.31)} = \frac{200}{4.155} = 48.1 \text{ K}$$

The product $PV$ has units of joules — a pascal is a newton per square metre and
a cubic metre times that is a newton-metre. That is worth remembering, because
it means an area on this diagram is an energy in joules with no conversion
factor at all.

The second thing a dot tells you is the internal energy. For a monatomic ideal
gas the molecules have only translational motion, three directions to move in,
and the kinetic theory result is

$$U = \tfrac{3}{2}nRT = \tfrac{3}{2}PV$$

Again, no temperature needed. Multiply the coordinates of the dot, take three
halves of it, and you have $U$ in joules. For a diatomic gas the two rotational
modes add another $nRT$ and you get $U = \tfrac{5}{2}PV$. Write $U = nC_V T$ if
you prefer symbols, with $C_V = \tfrac{3}{2}R$ for monatomic gases.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  $\Delta U$ depends only on where you start and where you finish, never on the
  path between them. Two states with the same product $PV$ have the same
  internal energy, even if one is reached by a wild detour. A closed loop
  therefore has $\Delta U = 0$, always.
</Aside>

## The four legs you will actually see

Almost every cycle in a problem is built from four kinds of leg, and each has a
recognisable shape.

An **isochoric** leg holds the volume fixed. The piston is locked, so the dot
moves straight up or down. The gas cannot push anything through a distance, so
the work is zero.

An **isobaric** leg holds the pressure fixed. The dot moves horizontally. The
gas pushes with a steady force while the piston moves, so the work is
$P\,\Delta V$.

An **isothermal** leg holds the temperature fixed. Then $PV$ is a constant, so
the dot traces a hyperbola — pressure falls as volume grows, in exact inverse
proportion. Since $T$ never changes, $\Delta U = 0$ on this leg, so every joule
of heat that enters comes straight back out as work.

An **adiabatic** leg lets no heat in or out at all, $Q = 0$. The gas does work
only by spending its own internal energy, so it cools as it expands. That means
it drops off a temperature curve as it goes, and the resulting path
$PV^\gamma = \text{constant}$ is steeper than the isotherm through the same
point. Here $\gamma = C_P/C_V$, which is $5/3$ for a monatomic gas.

## Work is the area under the path

Push a piston of area $A$ out by a small distance $dx$ against pressure $P$. The
force on the piston is $PA$, so the work the gas does is $PA\,dx$. But $A\,dx$ is
exactly the extra volume the gas gained. So the work done by the gas in a small
step is $P\,dV$, and over a whole leg,

$$W = \int_{V_i}^{V_f} P\,dV$$

That integral is the area under the curve, measured down to the volume axis.
Reading rightwards means expanding, and the work counts positive: the gas is
paying out energy. Reading leftwards means being squeezed, and the same area
counts negative.

Three of the four legs give you this area without any calculus. On an isochore
there is no width, so $W = 0$. On an isobar the region is a rectangle, so
$W = P(V_f - V_i)$. On a straight slanted line the region is a trapezium, and
the area is the average height times the width:

$$W = \tfrac{1}{2}(P_i + P_f)(V_f - V_i)$$

Only the isotherm needs the integral, and it is a short one. Substitute
$P = nRT/V$ and pull the constants out:

$$W = \int_{V_i}^{V_f} \frac{nRT}{V}\,dV = nRT\ln\frac{V_f}{V_i}$$

### Worked example 1: work along three simple legs

A gas expands at constant pressure $P = 2.0\times10^5$ Pa from
$V = 1.0\times10^{-3}$ m³ to $3.0\times10^{-3}$ m³. The path is a horizontal
segment traced left to right, so

$$W = P\,\Delta V = (2.0\times10^5)(3.0\times10^{-3} - 1.0\times10^{-3}) = (2.0\times10^5)(2.0\times10^{-3}) = 400 \text{ J}$$

Now heat a sealed rigid tank so the state runs straight up from
$1.0\times10^5$ Pa to $3.0\times10^5$ Pa at $V = 2.0\times10^{-3}$ m³. The
volume never changes, so $\Delta V = 0$ and $W = 0$ J. The pressure tripling is
irrelevant to the work; nothing moved.

Finally, take 1.0 mol at 300 K from $1.0\times10^{-3}$ m³ to
$2.0\times10^{-3}$ m³ along an isotherm:

$$W = nRT\ln\frac{V_f}{V_i} = (1.0)(8.31)(300)\ln 2 = 2493 \times 0.6931 = 1728 \text{ J}$$

Compare that with the isobaric answer you would get at the starting pressure,
$P_i = nRT/V_i = 2.49\times10^6$ Pa, which would be 2493 J. The isotherm gives
less because the pressure sags as the gas expands.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Reaching for $W = P\,\Delta V$ on a leg where the pressure is not constant.
  That formula is the isobaric case and nothing else. On an isotherm use
  $nRT\ln(V_f/V_i)$; on a straight slanted line use the trapezium; on an adiabat
  use $W = -\Delta U = -nC_V\Delta T$, which is far less painful than
  integrating $PV^\gamma$.
</Aside>

## Heat comes from the first law

You now have $W$ from the shape and $\Delta U$ from the endpoints. The first law
of thermodynamics ties them to the heat:

$$\Delta U = Q - W$$

with $Q$ the heat that flows into the gas and $W$ the work the gas does. The
signs matter and this is the convention every problem in the bank uses. So the
heat on any leg is $Q = \Delta U + W$, and for a monatomic gas that is

$$Q = \tfrac{3}{2}(P_fV_f - P_iV_i) + W$$

Three consequences drop out at once. On an isochore, $W = 0$, so
$Q = \Delta U = \tfrac{3}{2}V\,\Delta P$. On an isotherm, $\Delta U = 0$, so
$Q = W$. On an adiabat, $Q = 0$, so $W = -\Delta U$.

Try the isochore: a monatomic gas at $V = 2.0\times10^{-3}$ m³ whose pressure
drops from $3.0\times10^5$ Pa to $1.0\times10^5$ Pa has

$$Q = \tfrac{3}{2}(2.0\times10^{-3})(1.0\times10^5 - 3.0\times10^5) = 1.5 \times (200 - 600) = -600 \text{ J}$$

Negative, so 600 J leaves the gas. That is what cooling at fixed volume looks
like.

## Around a closed loop

Take the gas all the way around a cycle and it comes home to the state it
started in. Its internal energy is a property of the state, so $\Delta U = 0$
for the whole trip, and the first law collapses to

$$Q_{\text{net}} = W_{\text{net}}$$

Every joule of net heat absorbed comes out as net work. And the net work is the
area enclosed by the loop, because the outbound leg contributes the area under
it as positive and the return leg subtracts the area under itself, leaving the
sandwiched region between them.

Go clockwise and the expansion happens along the upper, high-pressure part of
the loop, so the positive contribution wins and $W_{\text{net}} > 0$. That is an
engine. Go counterclockwise and the sign flips: you feed work in, and the device
moves heat from cold to hot. That is a refrigerator.

The way to work a cycle without drowning is a table: one row per leg, three
columns headed $Q$, $W$ and $\Delta U$. Each process type hands you one entry
free. Compute a second from the geometry or the endpoints. Get the third from
$\Delta U = Q - W$. Then check that the $\Delta U$ column sums to zero. That
check catches sign errors before they poison an efficiency.

### Worked example 2: heat rejected by a rectangular cycle

A monatomic gas goes clockwise around
A $(2.0\times10^{-3}$ m³, $4.0\times10^5$ Pa$)$ →
B $(5.0\times10^{-3}$ m³, $4.0\times10^5$ Pa$)$ →
C $(5.0\times10^{-3}$ m³, $1.0\times10^5$ Pa$)$ →
D $(2.0\times10^{-3}$ m³, $1.0\times10^5$ Pa$)$ → A. How much heat leaves per
cycle?

Heat leaves on the two legs where the dot moves down or left, so B→C and C→D.
Do them one at a time.

B→C is isochoric at $V = 5.0\times10^{-3}$ m³ with the pressure falling by
$3.0\times10^5$ Pa. No work, so

$$Q_{BC} = \Delta U = \tfrac{3}{2}(5.0\times10^{-3})(-3.0\times10^5) = 1.5 \times (-1500) = -2250 \text{ J}$$

C→D is isobaric at $1.0\times10^5$ Pa, compressing from $5.0\times10^{-3}$ to
$2.0\times10^{-3}$ m³. The work is

$$W_{CD} = (1.0\times10^5)(2.0\times10^{-3} - 5.0\times10^{-3}) = -300 \text{ J}$$

negative because the gas is being squeezed. The internal energy change uses the
endpoints: $P_DV_D = 200$ J and $P_CV_C = 500$ J, so
$\Delta U = \tfrac{3}{2}(200 - 500) = -450$ J. Then

$$Q_{CD} = \Delta U + W = -450 + (-300) = -750 \text{ J}$$

Total heat rejected is $2250 + 750 = 3000$ J.

You can sanity-check this. The enclosed area is
$(3.0\times10^5)(3.0\times10^{-3}) = 900$ J of net work, so the heat absorbed on
the other two legs must be $3000 + 900 = 3900$ J. Adding A→B ($W = 1200$ J,
$\Delta U = 1800$ J, so $Q = 3000$ J) to D→A ($W = 0$, $\Delta U = 900$ J) gives
exactly 3900 J. The books balance.

## Efficiency, and cycles with slanted legs

An engine's **efficiency** is what you get divided by what you pay for:

$$\eta = \frac{W_{\text{net}}}{Q_{\text{in}}}$$

$Q_{\text{in}}$ is the sum of only the positive heats. Do not use the net heat
in the denominator — the whole point is that you paid for all the heat that went
in, including the part you later threw away. For the rectangle above,
$\eta = 900/3900 = 0.231$.

For a refrigerator the useful output is the heat pulled out of the cold space,
so the figure of merit is the coefficient of performance
$K = Q_c/W$, where $W$ is the work you put in. A cycle that absorbs 800 J from
the cold side while you supply 200 J of work has $K = 800/200 = 4.0$. There is
no contradiction with efficiency being under 1; these are different ratios.

### Worked example 3: a triangular cycle

Vertices A $(1.0\times10^{-3}$ m³, $1.0\times10^5$ Pa$)$,
B $(3.0\times10^{-3}$ m³, $1.0\times10^5$ Pa$)$,
C $(3.0\times10^{-3}$ m³, $4.0\times10^5$ Pa$)$, traversed A→B→C→A with the
closing leg a straight line. The gas is monatomic. Find the efficiency.

Net work first. The triangle has base $2.0\times10^{-3}$ m³ and height
$3.0\times10^5$ Pa, so its area is
$\tfrac{1}{2}(2.0\times10^{-3})(3.0\times10^5) = 300$ J. That is the magnitude
of the net work per cycle.

Now the legs. A→B is isobaric: $W = (1.0\times10^5)(2.0\times10^{-3}) = 200$ J,
and $\Delta U = \tfrac{3}{2}(300 - 100) = 300$ J, so $Q_{AB} = 500$ J, entering.

B→C is isochoric: $W = 0$ and
$\Delta U = \tfrac{3}{2}(1200 - 300) = 1350$ J, so $Q_{BC} = 1350$ J, also
entering.

So $Q_{\text{in}} = 500 + 1350 = 1850$ J and

$$\eta = \frac{300}{1850} = 0.162$$

The third leg is worth doing as a check, because it is the one that trips
people. C→A is a straight slanted line, so its work is the trapezium:

$$W_{CA} = \tfrac{1}{2}(4.0\times10^5 + 1.0\times10^5)(1.0\times10^{-3} - 3.0\times10^{-3}) = \tfrac{1}{2}(5.0\times10^5)(-2.0\times10^{-3}) = -500 \text{ J}$$

Its internal energy change is $\tfrac{3}{2}(100 - 1200) = -1650$ J, so

$$Q_{CA} = -1650 + (-500) = -2150 \text{ J}$$

Check the columns. Work: $200 + 0 - 500 = -300$ J, which matches the area to the
joule and tells you the sense of travel: going right along the bottom, up the
right-hand side, and back down the diagonal puts the enclosed region above the
outbound leg, so this loop runs counterclockwise and the signed net work is
$-300$ J. Internal energy: $300 + 1350 - 1650 = 0$, exactly as a closed loop
demands. The heat column sums to $500 + 1350 - 2150 = -300$ J, equal to the net
work, which is the closing check.

The efficiency the question wants is the useful output over the gross heat paid
for, $300/1850 = 0.162$, and the sense of travel does not change either number's
size.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Putting the net heat in the denominator of the efficiency. If a cycle absorbs
  3900 J and rejects 3000 J, the net heat is 900 J and $W_{\text{net}}$ is also
  900 J — you would get an efficiency of 1, which is nonsense. The denominator
  is the gross heat in, 3900 J.
</Aside>

## The Carnot cycle and the adiabatic shortcut

Two isotherms joined by two adiabats make a Carnot cycle. Because no heat
crosses the adiabats, all the heat in arrives on the hot isotherm at $T_h$ and
all the heat out leaves on the cold isotherm at $T_c$. On an isotherm
$Q = W = nRT\ln(V_f/V_i)$, and the geometry of the two adiabats forces the
volume ratios on the two isotherms to be equal. So the two heats are in the
ratio of the temperatures, and

$$\eta = 1 - \frac{T_c}{T_h}$$

Between 500 K and 300 K that is $1 - 300/500 = 0.400$. Run the same loop
backwards as a fridge between 400 K and 250 K and the coefficient of performance
is $K = T_c/(T_h - T_c) = 250/150 = 1.67$.

The net work follows just as quickly. For 2.0 mol running the hot isotherm at
600 K from $1.0\times10^{-3}$ to $3.0\times10^{-3}$ m³ and the cold one at
300 K, the volume ratio is 3 on both, so

$$W_{\text{net}} = nR(T_h - T_c)\ln 3 = (2.0)(8.31)(300)(1.0986) = 5478 \text{ J}$$

The other classic is the **Otto cycle**: adiabatic compression, isochoric
heating, adiabatic expansion, isochoric cooling. Heat is exchanged only on the
two constant-volume legs, where $Q = nC_V\Delta T$. Working through the ratios
gives

$$\eta = 1 - \left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right)^{\gamma - 1}$$

with $V_1/V_2$ the compression ratio. Compress a monatomic gas from
$8.0\times10^{-4}$ m³ to $1.0\times10^{-4}$ m³, a ratio of 8, and with
$\gamma - 1 = 2/3$ you get $8^{2/3} = 4$, so
$\eta = 1 - \tfrac{1}{4} = 0.750$.

## Recognising the technique

A problem wants PV-diagram bookkeeping when it hands you a list of states as
$(V, P)$ pairs, or describes a sequence of legs by name — isobaric, isochoric,
isothermal, adiabatic — or asks for work, heat, efficiency or coefficient of
performance "per cycle". Those phrases mean: draw the loop, then make the table.

Inside that, the sub-choices are decided by single words. "Rigid", "sealed
container", "constant volume" means $W = 0$ and $Q = \Delta U$. "At constant
pressure" or "the piston is free and the outside pressure is fixed" means
$W = P\Delta V$. "Isothermal" or "in contact with a reservoir at $T$" means
$\Delta U = 0$ and you need the logarithm. "Insulated", "adiabatic" or "too fast
for heat to flow" means $Q = 0$ and $W = -\Delta U$. "Straight line on the PV
diagram" means trapezium.

And three shortcuts save real time. If the question only asks for net work, find
the enclosed area and stop. If it only asks for $\Delta U$ between two states,
take $\tfrac{3}{2}(P_fV_f - P_iV_i)$ and ignore the path entirely — which is why
a monatomic gas going from $(3.0\times10^5$ Pa$, 2.0\times10^{-3}$ m³$)$ to
$(1.0\times10^5$ Pa$, 6.0\times10^{-3}$ m³$)$ has $\Delta U = 0$, both products
being 600 J. And if it asks for the temperature at a corner, that is $PV/nR$ and
nothing more.
