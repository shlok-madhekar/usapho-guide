## A skater who speeds up without pushing

A figure skater finishes a spin by pulling her arms in against her chest. She
spins faster. Nobody pushed her. The ice is slippery, so friction is not doing
it. She did not wind herself up. She just changed her shape, and the spin rate
went up by a factor of three.

Something about her motion refused to change while everything else did. Her
angular speed changed. Her kinetic energy changed. The quantity that stayed put
is called **angular momentum**, and this lesson is about finding it, knowing
when it is safe to assume it is fixed, and using that assumption to solve
problems that would otherwise need forces you cannot possibly know.

The previous lesson gave you $\tau_{\text{net}} = I\alpha$ — torque makes
angular acceleration, the rotational copy of $F = ma$. That is the right tool
when you know the torques. Here you will build the tool for when you don't:
during a collision, during a collapse, during any process where huge unknown
forces act for a very short time.

## Momentum, but measured about a point

Start with the version you already have. A particle of mass $m$ moving with
velocity $v$ has linear momentum $p = mv$. That number says nothing about
where the particle is. Angular momentum fixes that: it measures how much
"circulation" the particle has **about a chosen point**.

Pick a point $O$. Draw the straight line the particle is travelling along.
Measure the perpendicular distance from $O$ to that line — call it
$r_\perp$, the **lever arm**. The angular momentum of the particle about $O$ is

$$L = m v r_\perp$$

with units $\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}$. It gets a sign: positive if the particle
circulates counterclockwise around $O$, negative if clockwise. In full vector
form $\vec{L} = \vec{r} \times \vec{p}$, where $\vec{r}$ points from $O$ to the
particle, but for everything here the motion lies in a plane and
$L = mvr_\perp$ with a sign is all you need.

Two things about that formula surprise people. First, the particle does not
have to be going in a circle. A ball rolling in a straight line at
$4.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ past a point $3.0\ \mathrm{m}$ off to the side has
$L = (1.0)(4.0)(3.0) = 12\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}$ about that point, and since it
is not accelerating, that number never changes. Second, $L$ depends entirely
on which point you picked. About a point on the line of motion, $r_\perp = 0$
and the same ball has $L = 0$. Angular momentum is not a property of an
object. It is a property of an object *and* an origin.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  The lever arm $r_\perp$ is the perpendicular distance from your point to the
  *line the velocity lies along*, extended forwards and backwards as far as you
  like. If the velocity makes an angle $\theta$ with the position vector
  $\vec r$, then $r_\perp = r\sin\theta$ and $L = mvr\sin\theta$.
</Aside>

## A rigid body: L = Iω

Now take a solid object spinning about a fixed axis at angular speed $\omega$.
Chop it into tiny pieces. A piece of mass $m_i$ at distance $r_i$ from the axis
moves in a circle at speed $v_i = \omega r_i$, and its velocity is
perpendicular to its radius, so its lever arm is just $r_i$. Its angular
momentum is $m_i v_i r_i = m_i \omega r_i^2$. Add up every piece:

$$L = \sum m_i \omega r_i^2 = \left(\sum m_i r_i^2\right)\omega = I\omega$$

The sum in brackets is the moment of inertia $I$ from the last lesson. So
$L = I\omega$ is not a new law; it is $L = mvr_\perp$ summed over a rigid body.
A wheel with $I = 0.60\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ spinning at $25\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ has
$L = 15\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}$, and that is the whole calculation.

Keep both forms in your head. In almost every hard problem you will need
$mvr_\perp$ for the free-flying object and $I\omega$ for the spinning one, in
the same equation.

## Torque is what changes L

Differentiate $L = I\omega$ for a rigid body with fixed $I$:
$dL/dt = I\,d\omega/dt = I\alpha = \tau_{\text{net}}$. The general statement,
which holds even when $I$ changes, is

$$\tau_{\text{net}} = \frac{dL}{dt}, \qquad \text{so}\qquad \Delta L = \int \tau\,dt \approx \bar\tau\,\Delta t$$

That integral is the **angular impulse**. A torque of $5.0\ \mathrm{N\,m}$
applied for $3.0\ \mathrm{s}$ to a wheel at rest leaves it with
$L = (5.0)(3.0) = 15\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}$, whatever the wheel's $I$ happens to
be. Run it backwards and a flywheel whose $L$ climbs from $40$ to
$70\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}$ in $5.0\ \mathrm{s}$ felt an average torque of
$30/5.0 = 6.0\ \mathrm{N\,m}$.

The payoff is the contrapositive. **If the net external torque about a point is
zero, the total angular momentum about that point does not change.** That is
conservation of angular momentum, and it is the most useful sentence in
rotational mechanics.

## Pick the point before you write anything down

Here is the move that makes hard problems easy. Angular momentum is conserved
about *some* points and not others in the very same problem, so you choose the
point deliberately, before you write a single equation.

The rule: **pick the point that the forces you cannot evaluate pass through.**
A force pointing straight at your chosen point has zero lever arm, so it exerts
zero torque about it, so it cannot change $L$ about it — no matter how large it
is or how it varies in time.

A bullet embeds in a rod hinged at one end. During the impact, the hinge slams
back with an enormous unknown force, so linear momentum is definitely *not*
conserved. But that force acts at the hinge. Take angular momentum about the
hinge and the unknown vanishes:

$$\underbrace{m v r_\perp}_{\text{bullet, before}} = \underbrace{\left(I_{\text{rod}} + m r^2\right)\omega}_{\text{everything, after}}$$

One equation, one unknown. Every "something strikes a pivoted object" problem
is this problem.

For an object with no pivot at all — a rod floating on frictionless ice — the
right point is the **centre of mass of the whole system**, because gravity and
the normal force cancel and there is nothing else. Angular momentum about the
centre of mass is conserved, and so is linear momentum, and you may need both.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Do not switch origins partway through a problem. Compute $L$ before and after
  about the *same* fixed point. And count everything: after the bullet embeds,
  the bullet is still there, contributing $mr^2$ to the moment of inertia.
  Dropping that term is the single most common error in this topic. A second
  one: the pivot point is *not* generally the centre of mass, so
  $I_{\text{rod}}$ about a hinge at the end is $ML^2/3$, not $ML^2/12$.
</Aside>

## Worked example 1: two disks couple

A disk with $I_1 = 1.2\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ spins at
$\omega_1 = 10\ \mathrm{rad/s}$. It is dropped onto a stationary coaxial disk
with $I_2 = 0.80\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$. Friction between them brings them to a
common angular speed. Find $\omega_f$, and the kinetic energy lost.

The friction between the faces is internal to the two-disk system, so it
exerts equal and opposite torques and cannot change the total $L$ about the
common axis. Before:

$$L_i = I_1\omega_1 + I_2(0) = (1.2)(10) = 12\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}$$

After, they turn as one object with $I = I_1 + I_2 = 2.0\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$:

$$12 = (2.0)\,\omega_f \quad\Rightarrow\quad \omega_f = 6.0\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$

Energies. Before: $K_i = \tfrac12(1.2)(10)^2 = 60\ \mathrm{J}$. After:
$K_f = \tfrac12(2.0)(6.0)^2 = \tfrac12(2.0)(36) = 36\ \mathrm{J}$. So
$24\ \mathrm{J}$ went into heating the rubbing faces.

That energy loss is not a mistake. Write $K = \tfrac12 I\omega^2$ and
substitute $\omega = L/I$:

$$K = \frac{L^2}{2I}$$

With $L$ pinned, increasing $I$ *must* lower $K$, and decreasing $I$ must
raise it. That is why the skater's energy goes up when she pulls in: her
muscles do the work of hauling her arms inwards against their tendency to keep
going straight. With $I$ from $4.0$ to $1.6\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ and $\omega$
from $2.0$ to $5.0\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, she gains
$\tfrac12(1.6)(25) - \tfrac12(4.0)(4) = 20 - 8 = 12\ \mathrm{J}$.

## Worked example 2: a bullet in a pivoted rod

A $10\ \mathrm{g}$ bullet travelling at $300\ \mathrm{m/s}$ strikes the end of
a uniform rod ($M = 2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$, $L = 1.0\ \mathrm{m}$) pivoted at its
centre, perpendicular to the rod, and embeds. Find the angular speed just
after.

First write down what you would put on blank paper: a sketch, the pivot marked,
and the choice of origin. Take the pivot, because the pivot's reaction force is
unknown and passes through it.

The bullet's lever arm about the pivot is the half-length,
$r = 0.50\ \mathrm{m}$, since its velocity is perpendicular to the rod. Before
the impact the rod is at rest, so all the angular momentum is the bullet's:

$$L_i = m v r = (0.010)(300)(0.50) = 1.5\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}$$

After, the rod plus the lodged bullet rotate together. The rod is pivoted at
its centre, so $I_{\text{rod}} = \tfrac{1}{12}ML^2 = \tfrac{1}{12}(2.0)(1.0)^2
= 0.1667\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$. The bullet is a point mass at
$0.50\ \mathrm{m}$: $I_{\text{bullet}} = mr^2 = (0.010)(0.25) =
0.0025\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$. Total
$I = 0.1667 + 0.0025 = 0.1692\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$.

$$\omega = \frac{L_i}{I} = \frac{1.5}{0.1692} = 8.87\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$

Check the energy if you want to see how violent this is: before,
$\tfrac12(0.010)(300)^2 = 450\ \mathrm{J}$; after,
$\tfrac12(0.1692)(8.87)^2 = 6.7\ \mathrm{J}$. Over $98\%$ of the energy went
into deforming the wood. Good thing you did not try to conserve it.

## Worked example 3: the rotational ballistic pendulum

A $20\ \mathrm{g}$ bullet at $100\ \mathrm{m/s}$ embeds in the bottom end of a
$1.0\ \mathrm{m}$ uniform rod ($M = 1.5\ \mathrm{kg}$) hanging from a pivot at
its top. Through what angle does the rod swing?

This is a two-stage problem, and recognising that is the whole difficulty.
Stage one is the impact: fast, violent, energy destroyed, angular momentum
conserved. Stage two is the swing: slow, smooth, gravity does the work, energy
conserved. You must not mix them. Solve stage one for $\omega$, then hand
$\omega$ to stage two as an initial condition.

**Stage one.** About the pivot, the bullet has
$L_i = mvr = (0.020)(100)(1.0) = 2.0\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}$.
A rod pivoted at its end has $I_{\text{rod}} = \tfrac13 ML^2 =
\tfrac13(1.5)(1.0)^2 = 0.500\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, and the bullet adds
$mr^2 = (0.020)(1.0)^2 = 0.020$. So $I = 0.520\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ and

$$\omega = \frac{2.0}{0.520} = 3.846\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$

**Stage two.** The kinetic energy just after impact is

$$K = \tfrac12 I\omega^2 = \tfrac12(0.520)(3.846)^2 = 3.846\ \mathrm{J}$$

As the rod swings up by angle $\theta$, the rod's centre of mass (at
$L/2 = 0.50\ \mathrm{m}$ below the pivot) rises by
$0.50(1-\cos\theta)$, and the bullet at $1.0\ \mathrm{m}$ rises by
$1.0(1-\cos\theta)$. The gravitational potential energy gained is

$$

\Delta U = \left[Mg\tfrac{L}{2} + mgL\right](1-\cos\theta)
= \left[(1.5)(9.8)(0.50) + (0.020)(9.8)(1.0)\right](1-\cos\theta)

$$

$$\Delta U = [7.35 + 0.196](1-\cos\theta) = 7.546\,(1-\cos\theta)\ \mathrm{J}$$

Set $\Delta U = K$ at the top of the swing, where the rod is momentarily at
rest:

$$1-\cos\theta = \frac{3.846}{7.546} = 0.5097 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \cos\theta = 0.4903$$

$$\theta = \arccos(0.4903) = 60.6^\circ$$

The same setup answers a harder question: what bullet speed makes the rod go
all the way over the top? Over the top means $\theta = 180^\circ$, so
$1-\cos\theta = 2$ and you need $K = 2(7.546) = 15.09\ \mathrm{J}$. Then
$\omega = \sqrt{2K/I} = \sqrt{2(15.09)/0.520} = \sqrt{58.05} =
7.62\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, and running stage one backwards,

$$v = \frac{I\omega}{mr} = \frac{(0.520)(7.62)}{(0.020)(1.0)} = 198\ \mathrm{m/s}$$

A rigid rod is what makes "over the top" mean exactly $180^\circ$. If it hung
on a string you would need enough speed to keep the string taut, which is a
different and larger condition.

## When there is no pivot at all

Put a uniform rod ($M = 2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$, $L = 1.2\ \mathrm{m}$) at rest on
frictionless ice and hit one end with a perpendicular impulse of
$J = 3.0\ \mathrm{N\,s}$. Nothing holds the rod. What happens?

Two things at once, and you treat them separately. The linear impulse gives the
centre of mass a velocity: $v_{\text{cm}} = J/M = 3.0/2.0 = 1.5\ \mathrm{m/s}$.
The angular impulse about the centre of mass gives it a spin. The blow lands at
the end, a distance $L/2 = 0.60\ \mathrm{m}$ from the centre, so the angular
impulse is $J\,(L/2) = (3.0)(0.60) = 1.8\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}$. A free rod
spins about its own centre of mass, where $I = \tfrac{1}{12}ML^2 =
\tfrac{1}{12}(2.0)(1.44) = 0.24\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$. So

$$\omega = \frac{1.8}{0.24} = 7.5\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$

The rod drifts and spins, both at once, and the spin is about the centre of
mass no matter where you hit it.

The sticky version needs one more step. A $0.20\ \mathrm{kg}$ lump of putty at
$6.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ hits the end of a rod ($M = 1.0\ \mathrm{kg}$,
$L = 0.80\ \mathrm{m}$) on ice, perpendicular, and sticks. The right origin is
the centre of mass of the *combined* final object, because that point moves at
constant velocity throughout and the internal impact forces have no torque
about it. So find it first. Measuring from the rod's centre, the putty lands at
$0.40\ \mathrm{m}$, so

$$x_{\text{cm}} = \frac{(0.20)(0.40)}{1.0+0.20} = \frac{0.080}{1.20} = 0.0667\ \mathrm{m}$$

That puts the rod's centre $0.0667\ \mathrm{m}$ from the new pivot point and
the putty $0.400 - 0.0667 = 0.333\ \mathrm{m}$ from it. Now build $I$ about
that point, using the parallel axis theorem for the rod:

$$I = \tfrac{1}{12}(1.0)(0.80)^2 + (1.0)(0.0667)^2 + (0.20)(0.333)^2$$
$$I = 0.05333 + 0.00444 + 0.02222 = 0.0800\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$$

Before the hit, only the putty is moving, and its lever arm about that same
point is $0.333\ \mathrm{m}$:

$$L_i = (0.20)(6.0)(0.333) = 0.400\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}$$

$$\omega = \frac{0.400}{0.0800} = 5.0\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$

This one takes practice. The step people miss is locating the new centre of
mass before doing anything else.

## Two places this shows up in the sky

A star of radius $7.0\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m}$ turning once every $25$ days
collapses to a neutron star of radius $1.2\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{m}$ with no
mass lost. Both are uniform spheres, $I = \tfrac25 MR^2$, so $L = I\omega$
constant gives $R_1^2/T_1 = R_2^2/T_2$, and

$$T_2 = T_1\left(\frac{R_2}{R_1}\right)^2 = (25)(86400)\left(\frac{1.2\times10^{4}}{7.0\times10^{8}}\right)^2$$

The radius ratio is $1.714\times10^{-5}$, squared is $2.94\times10^{-10}$, and
$2.16\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{s}$ times that is about
$6\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s}$. The collapsed star spins over a thousand times a
second. That is a pulsar, and the mechanism is the skater.

The other place is precession. Support a spinning wheel at one end of its axle
and gravity applies a torque $\tau = mgd$, where $d$ is the distance from the
support to the centre of mass. That torque is perpendicular to $\vec L$, so it
cannot change $L$'s magnitude — only swing its direction. The axle sweeps
around a cone at rate

$$\Omega = \frac{\tau}{L} = \frac{mgd}{I\omega}$$

For $I = 0.20\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, $\omega = 40\ \mathrm{rad/s}$,
$m = 2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$, $d = 0.15\ \mathrm{m}$: $\Omega =
(2.0)(9.8)(0.15)/[(0.20)(40)] = 2.94/8.0 = 0.368\ \mathrm{rad/s}$. Spin the
wheel faster and it precesses slower, which is why a fast top stands up and a
slow one falls over.

## How to recognise an angular momentum problem

Reach for $L$ when you see any of these.

**Something sticks, catches, or couples.** Putty, clay, bullets, a child
jumping onto a merry-go-round, a disk dropped onto another disk. Sticking means
energy is gone, so momentum-type conservation is your only survivor. If there
is a pivot, use angular momentum about it.

**A pivot or axle exerts a force you were never told.** That is a deliberate
signal. The problem is telling you to take moments about the pivot so the
unknown drops out.

**The object changes shape or radius while spinning.** Arms in, a rope pulled
through a hole in a table, a collapsing star, a satellite tightening its orbit.
$I$ changes, $L$ doesn't, so $\omega$ must.

**The problem gives you an object on frictionless ice with no pivot.** Then you
need both conservation laws: linear momentum for $v_{\text{cm}}$, angular
momentum about the system's centre of mass for $\omega$.

**Two stages, one violent and one gentle.** Impact then swing, impact then
climb. Angular momentum across the impact, energy across the swing. Never one
law for both.

And the counter-signal: if you know all the forces and they act for a known
stretch of time, you do not need conservation at all — go back to
$\tau = I\alpha$ from the previous lesson and integrate.
