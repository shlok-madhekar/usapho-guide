## Pressure from collisions

The ideal gas law is usually handed over as an experimental fact, but it comes
out of counting molecular collisions with a wall. Each impact reverses one
momentum component, and averaging over a large number of molecules gives

$$PV = \tfrac13 Nm\overline{v^2} = Nk_BT = nRT$$

Comparing the two right-hand expressions connects the microscopic to the
macroscopic: temperature is a measure of average translational kinetic energy,
nothing more.

$$\tfrac12 m\overline{v^2} = \tfrac32 k_BT \quad \Longrightarrow \quad v_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_BT}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}}$$

At room temperature that is roughly 500 m/s for nitrogen, which is a useful
number to carry: air molecules are moving about as fast as sound, because
sound is carried by them.

## Equipartition and heat capacity

Each quadratic degree of freedom holds $\tfrac12 k_BT$ of energy on average.
A monatomic gas has three translational degrees of freedom and internal energy
$U = \tfrac32 nRT$; a diatomic gas adds two rotational ones at ordinary
temperatures, giving $\tfrac52 nRT$.

That single fact produces the molar heat capacities:

$$C_V = \tfrac32 R, \qquad C_P = C_V + R = \tfrac52 R$$

for a monatomic gas. The relation $C_P - C_V = R$ holds for any ideal gas,
because heating at constant pressure also does expansion work.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Equal temperature means equal average kinetic energy per molecule, not equal
  speed. In a mixture of helium and nitrogen at one temperature the helium is
  moving noticeably faster, because speed goes as the inverse square root of
  mass.
</Aside>
