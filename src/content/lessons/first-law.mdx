## Three quantities, one equation

Mechanics gave you one conserved thing to track. Thermodynamics adds a second
channel for energy to enter a system — disordered transfer driven by a
temperature difference — and calls it heat. Work is the ordered channel: a
piston moving against pressure. Internal energy $U$ is what the system has
stored once both channels are accounted for.

$$\Delta U = Q - W$$

The important word is *state*. $U$ is a property of the gas: give its
temperature, volume and amount, and $U$ is fixed. $Q$ and $W$ are not
properties of anything. They describe a process, and two different processes
connecting the same two states will generally split the same $\Delta U$ into
different amounts of heat and work.

## The sign convention

$W$ here is work done *by* the gas on its surroundings. Gas expands, $W > 0$,
and the gas loses energy unless heat makes it up. $Q$ is heat added *to* the
gas. Both conventions point outward from the system's point of view, which is
why the signs in $\Delta U = Q - W$ are opposite.

Chemistry texts often write $\Delta U = Q + W$ with $W$ meaning work done *on*
the gas. Both are correct; mixing them mid-problem is not.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  A gas is compressed and its temperature rises, and the answer comes out with
  the wrong sign. Compression means $dV < 0$, so $W = \int P\,dV$ is negative,
  and $-W$ in the first law is a positive contribution to $U$. Check the sign
  of $\Delta V$ before you evaluate the integral, not after.
</Aside>

## Why $U$ depends only on $T$

In an ideal gas the molecules do not interact except during collisions, so
there is no potential energy stored in their separation. All the internal
energy is kinetic energy of the molecules, and that is set by temperature
alone. Pull the walls apart and the molecules spread out without changing
speed: volume drops out.

$$U = U(T) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \Delta U = nC_V\,\Delta T$$

That second form holds for *any* ideal gas process, not just constant volume.
$C_V$ earns its subscript from how it is measured, not from where it applies.
This is what makes the first law tractable: compute $\Delta T$, get $\Delta U$
immediately, and let the process details decide how $Q$ and $W$ divide it up.

A free expansion into vacuum makes the point sharply. No work is done — there
is nothing to push against — and no heat enters, so $\Delta U = 0$ and the
temperature is unchanged, even though the volume doubled.
