## Where the mass sits

Spin a hammer about its handle and it turns easily. Spin the same hammer about
its head end and it resists. Nothing about the mass changed — only its distance
from the axis.

Moment of inertia captures that. For a collection of point masses,

$$I = \sum m_i r_i^2$$

and for a continuous body, $I = \int r^2\,dm$. The $r^2$ is the whole story:
mass twice as far out contributes four times as much. This is why a flywheel
puts its material in the rim and why tightrope walkers carry a long pole.

Note that $r$ is the distance from the *axis*, measured perpendicular to it,
not the distance from the center of the body. Change the axis and $I$ changes.

## The shapes worth knowing

All about the axis of symmetry through the center of mass:

$$I_{\text{hoop}} = mr^2, \qquad I_{\text{disk}} = \tfrac12 mr^2$$

$$I_{\text{solid sphere}} = \tfrac25 mr^2, \qquad I_{\text{shell}} = \tfrac23 mr^2$$

$$I_{\text{rod, center}} = \tfrac{1}{12}mL^2$$

A useful sanity check: the coefficient rises as mass moves outward. Solid
sphere $0.4$, hollow sphere $0.67$, disk $0.5$, hoop $1$ — every one of them
between $0$ and $1$, because no mass can be farther than $r$ from the axis.

Moments of inertia about the same axis add. A disk with a rod stuck through it
has $I = \tfrac12 m_d r^2 + \tfrac{1}{12}m_r L^2$. Drilling a hole out is the
same rule with a minus sign: compute the full body and subtract the removed
piece.

## Shifting the axis

To get $I$ about an axis parallel to one through the center of mass and a
distance $d$ away:

$$I = I_{cm} + md^2$$

A rod about its end: $\tfrac{1}{12}mL^2 + m(L/2)^2 = \tfrac13 mL^2$. That single
theorem replaces most of the table you would otherwise memorize.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  The parallel axis theorem only works starting from the center of mass. Going
  from a rod's end to a point a quarter along it by adding $md^2$ is wrong —
  return to the center of mass first, then step out to the new axis.
</Aside>
