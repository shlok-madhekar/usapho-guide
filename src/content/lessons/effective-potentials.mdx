## Force is a downhill slope

For any one-dimensional conservative system, the force is minus the slope of
the potential energy:

$$F(x) = -\frac{dU}{dx}$$

A particle is pushed toward lower $U$, the way a ball rolls downhill on a graph
of $U$ against $x$. So the shape of the $U$ curve tells you everything about
where the system will sit and how it will move near there.

Equilibrium means zero net force, which means a flat spot:

$$\frac{dU}{dx} = 0$$

## Stable or unstable

Flat is not enough. Whether a small nudge brings the particle back or drives it
away depends on the curvature.

If $U''(x_0) > 0$ the point is a minimum, a valley, and the particle is pushed
back. That is stable equilibrium. If $U''(x_0) < 0$ it is a maximum, a hilltop,
and any displacement grows. That is unstable. If $U''(x_0) = 0$ the test is
inconclusive and you have to look at higher derivatives or just sketch the
curve.

The picture is literal. Draw $U$, find the flat places, and ask whether each
one holds water.

## Small oscillations

Near a minimum, expand $U$ in a Taylor series about $x_0$. The constant term
does nothing, the linear term vanishes because $U'(x_0) = 0$, and what is left
is:

$$U(x) \approx U(x_0) + \tfrac{1}{2}U''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2$$

That is a spring potential with $k = U''(x_0)$. So every stable equilibrium in
physics is a harmonic oscillator when you look closely enough, and its
frequency is:

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{U''(x_0)}{m}}$$

Take the Lennard-Jones-style potential
$U(r) = U_0\left[(r_0/r)^{12} - 2(r_0/r)^6\right]$. Setting $U' = 0$ gives
$r = r_0$, and evaluating the second derivative there gives
$U''= 72U_0/r_0^2$, so a nearby atom vibrates at
$\omega = \sqrt{72U_0/mr_0^2}$. No new physics was needed, only two
derivatives.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  When a rotating system is involved, the centrifugal term belongs *inside* the
  effective potential, not outside it. For a central force at fixed angular
  momentum $\ell$, use
  $U_{\text{eff}}(r) = U(r) + \ell^2/2mr^2$ and take derivatives of that. Using
  the bare $U(r)$ puts the equilibrium radius in the wrong place, and often
  reports no equilibrium where a stable circular orbit exists.
</Aside>

For rotational or generalized coordinates the same machinery applies. Replace
$m$ with the appropriate inertia and differentiate with respect to the
coordinate you are using.
