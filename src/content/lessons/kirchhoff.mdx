## Two rules, then bookkeeping

Kirchhoff's laws are conservation statements. Charge does not pile up at a
junction, and energy is single-valued around a loop.

$$\sum I_{in} = \sum I_{out}, \qquad \sum \Delta V_{loop} = 0$$

Everything else about resistive circuits is applying those two carefully.
Series resistors add; parallel conductances add:

$$R_{series} = R_1 + R_2, \qquad \frac{1}{R_{parallel}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}$$

## Symmetry, before algebra

Olympiad circuits are usually designed so that brute-force loop equations are
unpleasant and symmetry is quick. If two nodes are at the same potential, no
current flows between them: you can merge them or delete the connecting
element without changing anything.

The resistor cube is the standard example. Entering at one corner, current
splits three ways, then six, then three, giving $\tfrac56 R$ between opposite
corners without solving a single simultaneous equation.

Infinite ladders use the other trick: the network beyond the first stage is
identical to the whole, so setting $R_{total}$ equal to that self-similar
expression gives a solvable equation.

## Capacitors and time

A capacitor stores charge, $Q = CV$, and energy $U = \tfrac12 CV^2$. In an RC
circuit the charging is exponential with time constant $\tau = RC$:

$$V(t) = V_0\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right)$$

Two limits are usually all you need. Immediately after a switch closes, an
uncharged capacitor behaves like a wire. Long afterward, it behaves like a
break in the circuit. Sketching those two states answers most transient
questions before any exponential is written down.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Sign errors, almost every time. Fix a direction for each loop before you
  start and keep it, even when a current comes out negative: a negative answer
  simply means the current runs the other way, and it is still correct. Going
  back and flipping signs mid-solution is what actually loses the marks.
</Aside>
