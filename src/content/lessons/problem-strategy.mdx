Two students get the same problem: a block on a ramp, a string over a pulley, a
hanging weight. One of them fills three pages with sines and cosines, makes a
sign error on page two, and gets a number that is wrong by a factor of two. The
other writes four lines and is done. They know the same physics. The difference
happened before either of them wrote an equation.

The last lesson gave you a way to make hard expressions
[small and manageable](/guide/approximations) once you have them. This one is
about the step before that: how to set a problem up so that the expressions are
never hard in the first place. Three decisions do most of the work — what you
draw a boundary around, which directions you call $x$ and $y$, and which law
you reach for. Get those three right and most olympiad mechanics becomes
bookkeeping.

## Draw the boundary first

Every equation in mechanics is about a **system**: a specific collection of
stuff that you have mentally cut out of the world. Everything inside the
boundary is the system. Everything outside can only touch the system through
**external forces**, and only those appear in $\sum F = ma$.

That word *only* is the whole trick. Forces between two parts of the same
system come in equal and opposite pairs, so they cancel inside the sum and
vanish from your equation. Move the boundary and a force that was giving you
trouble can disappear.

Take two blocks touching each other on a frictionless table, $m_A = 3.0$ kg in
front and $m_B = 5.0$ kg behind it, with a horizontal force $F = 12$ N pushing
on the front block so that both slide together. The blocks push on each other
with some contact force $C$ that you do not know. If you draw the boundary
around *both* blocks, $C$ is internal and never appears:

$$F = (m_A + m_B)\,a \quad\Rightarrow\quad a = \frac{12\ \text{N}}{8.0\ \text{kg}} = 1.5\ \text{m/s}^2.$$

One line. Now suppose the contact force is what you actually want. Move the
boundary so it cuts between the blocks, keeping only the front block inside.
The cut turns $C$ from internal into external, which is exactly why you make
it. The front block feels $F$ forward and $C$ backward from the block behind
it, and it has the acceleration you just found:

$$F - C = m_A a \quad\Rightarrow\quad C = 12 - (3.0)(1.5) = 7.5\ \text{N}.$$

Two systems, two lines, no simultaneous equations. The general rule: **put the
boundary around everything when you want the motion, cut through the thing you
want to know when you want a force.**

## Axes are free, so choose selfishly

Nothing in physics says $x$ must be horizontal. Axes are a bookkeeping choice
you make, and the only rule is that they be perpendicular. So choose the pair
that makes the most vectors lie flat along an axis.

A block of mass $m = 2.0$ kg on a frictionless incline of angle
$\theta = 30^\circ$ makes the point. With horizontal and vertical axes, the
normal force and the acceleration both have two components, and you end up
solving a pair of coupled equations. Tilt the axes instead: let $x$ point down
along the surface and $y$ point out perpendicular to it. Now the normal force
is entirely along $y$, and the acceleration is entirely along $x$, because the
block cannot burrow into the ramp or lift off it. Only the weight needs
splitting.

The weight $mg$ points straight down, at angle $\theta$ from your new $y$ axis.
So its component along the surface is $mg\sin\theta$ and its component into the
surface is $mg\cos\theta$. Watch which one gets the sine: the piece that pushes
the block along the ramp has to vanish when $\theta = 0$, and $\sin 0 = 0$,
so it is the sine. That check takes a second and saves the most common error in
mechanics.

Along $x$: $\;mg\sin\theta = ma$, so

$$a = g\sin\theta = (9.8)(0.500) = 4.9\ \text{m/s}^2.$$

The mass cancelled, which it had to — every object falls the same way on a
frictionless slope. Along $y$ the block does not accelerate, so the components
must balance:

$$N = mg\cos\theta = (2.0)(9.8)(0.866) = 17.0\ \text{N}.$$

Less than the $19.6$ N weight, as it should be: the ramp only has to support
the part of the weight pressing into it.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  When you tilt the axes, tilt them for *everything*. A very common wreck is
  resolving the weight along the slope, then writing the normal force as $mg$
  anyway because that is what it was on flat ground. Every vector in the
  problem gets resolved in the same frame, or none do.
</Aside>

## Let symmetry answer the question for you

If the whole setup is unchanged by some operation — a rotation, a reflection,
a swap of two labels — then the answer must be unchanged by it too. That sounds
abstract until you see how much arithmetic it deletes.

A point charge $q = 6.0\ \mu\text{C}$ sits at one corner of a cube. What is the
electric flux through one of the three faces that do not touch that corner?

The honest way is a surface integral over a square with a charge at an awkward
angle, which is unpleasant. Instead, build symmetry into the picture. Surround
the charge with eight identical cubes, stacked to make one big cube with the
charge at its centre. By Gauss's law the total flux out of that big cube is
$q/\varepsilon_0$. The eight little cubes are interchangeable by reflection, so
each takes an eighth: $q/8\varepsilon_0$ through your cube.

Now look at one little cube. Three of its faces touch the charge, and the flux
through those is zero because the field skims along them. The other three faces
are interchangeable with each other by rotating the cube about its long
diagonal, so they must split the $q/8\varepsilon_0$ equally, a third each:

$$\Phi = \frac{q}{24\varepsilon_0} = \frac{6.0\times10^{-6}}{24\,(8.854\times10^{-12})} = \frac{6.0\times10^{-6}}{2.125\times10^{-10}} = 2.82\times10^{4}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}.$$

No integral. The same reflex answers a whole family of questions in one step.
Two identical charges, field at the midpoint between them: reflect the picture
through that midpoint and it is unchanged, but every field vector flips, so the
field must equal minus itself, so it is zero. A Wheatstone bridge whose left
and right branches have the same ratio: the two midpoints sit at the same
potential, so the current through the bridge resistor is zero and you can
delete it from the circuit.

## When the ground moves under you

Some problems hide their difficulty in the choice of frame. A block rests on
the frictionless $25^\circ$ face of a wedge, and the wedge is pushed
horizontally so that the block rides along without sliding. What acceleration
does that take?

The block has only two forces on it: gravity $mg$ down and the normal force $N$
perpendicular to the face. Here tilted axes are the wrong choice, because the
acceleration is horizontal, not along the slope. Use horizontal and vertical
axes and let $N$ be the one that splits. Vertically the block does not
accelerate, so the vertical part of $N$ carries the weight:
$N\cos\theta = mg$. Horizontally, the leftover part of $N$ is the only force,
so it supplies the acceleration: $N\sin\theta = ma$. Divide the second by the
first and $N$ cancels:

$$a = g\tan\theta = (9.8)\tan 25^\circ = (9.8)(0.4663) = 4.57\ \text{m/s}^2.$$

And if the block has mass $m = 1.5$ kg, the normal force follows from the
vertical equation:

$$N = \frac{mg}{\cos\theta} = \frac{(1.5)(9.8)}{0.9063} = 16.2\ \text{N},$$

larger than the $14.7$ N weight, because the surface is doing two jobs at once.

Now the harder cousin, and this one takes practice. A block of $m = 1.0$ kg
slides down the frictionless $30^\circ$ face of a wedge of $M = 4.0$ kg that is
itself free to slide on a frictionless floor. Find the wedge's acceleration.

Choose the system generously first: block plus wedge together. The floor is
frictionless and gravity is vertical, so there is **no external horizontal
force at all**, and the horizontal momentum of the pair is conserved. Starting
from rest, that gives $m\,v_x + M\,V = 0$ at every instant, and differentiating,
$m\,a_x = -M\,A$ where $A$ is the wedge's acceleration. One unknown expressed
in terms of the other, for free.

For the second equation you need the block alone, and the key is that the
block's acceleration is measured in the ground frame while the constraint —
that it stays on the face — lives in the wedge's frame. Write the block's
acceleration as the wedge's acceleration plus a sliding acceleration $a_r$
along the face. Doing the two component equations for the block and eliminating
$N$ gives the standard result

$$A = \frac{m g \sin\theta\cos\theta}{M + m\sin^2\theta}.$$

Before plugging in, test it. If $M \to \infty$ the denominator blows up and
$A \to 0$: a wedge nailed to the earth does not move. If $\theta \to 0$ the
face is flat, nothing slides, and $\sin\theta\cos\theta \to 0$ kills $A$. If
$\theta \to 90^\circ$ the block falls vertically past a vertical face, pushing
on nothing, and $\cos\theta \to 0$ kills $A$ again. Three checks passed, so
plug in:

$$A = \frac{(1.0)(9.8)(0.500)(0.866)}{4.0 + (1.0)(0.500)^2} = \frac{4.244}{4.25} = 1.0\ \text{m/s}^2.$$

## Check the formula before you trust the number

Those three limit checks were not decoration. A symbolic answer is a machine
that makes predictions, and you can test it in ten seconds against cases whose
answers you already know.

Run the checks in this order. **Dimensions**: both sides must carry the same
units, and if they do not, stop. **Limits**: send each variable to zero, to
infinity, or to equality with another, and ask whether what comes out is what
physics demands. **Symmetry**: if swapping two labels leaves the problem
unchanged, it must leave the answer unchanged. **Sign**: does a positive answer
point where you said positive points? **Magnitude**: a $2$ kg block moving at
$300$ m/s after a nudge, an efficiency of $1.4$, a negative kinetic energy —
each one means go back.

The Atwood machine is the cleanest place to practise. Two masses on a string
over an ideal pulley give $a = (m_1 - m_2)g/(m_1 + m_2)$ and
$T = 2m_1m_2g/(m_1 + m_2)$. Set $m_1 = m_2$: the acceleration vanishes, correct.
Let $m_2 \to 0$: $a \to g$, free fall, correct. Swap the labels: $a$ changes
sign, which is right because "down on the left" becomes "up on the left". For
$m_1 = 5.0$ kg and $m_2 = 3.0$ kg,

$$a = \frac{(5.0-3.0)(9.8)}{8.0} = 2.45\ \text{m/s}^2, \qquad T = \frac{2(5.0)(3.0)(9.8)}{8.0} = 36.8\ \text{N},$$

and $T$ sits between the two weights, $29.4$ N and $49$ N, exactly as it must:
the string has to hold back the heavy one and drag up the light one.

## Recognising which move a problem wants

The problem statement usually tells you, if you read it for structure rather
than for numbers.

If two objects are **joined and forced to move together** — touching blocks, a
string, a pulley — do the pair first, then cut. If the question asks for a
tension, a contact force, or a force in a hinge, that is the signal to cut
exactly there.

If the motion is **constrained to a surface**, tilt the axes to that surface.
If instead the acceleration is along some other fixed direction, as with the
driven wedge, keep the axes along the acceleration and resolve the normal force
instead. The axis you refuse to tilt is the one the acceleration lies on.

If the setup has a **repeated or interchangeable piece** — identical charges,
identical resistors on the edges of a cube, an infinite lattice that looks the
same from every node — look for a symmetry or superposition argument before
computing anything. Those problems are usually unfeasible by brute force and
one line by symmetry, which is a strong hint about what was intended.

If the floor or the pivot is **frictionless and nothing external pushes
sideways**, horizontal momentum is conserved and that is a free equation. If
the question names a "before" and an "after" and asks nothing about the time in
between, reach for a conservation law rather than for $F = ma$.

And when the answer is a formula, spend the ten seconds on limits. That habit
catches more mistakes than any amount of care in the algebra, and it is the one
thing that separates the four-line solution from the three-page one.
