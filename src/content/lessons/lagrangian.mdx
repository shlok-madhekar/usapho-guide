## The problem with free-body diagrams

Newton's laws are vector equations, so a constrained system forces you to
track forces you do not care about. A bead on a wire needs the normal force
from the wire; a pendulum needs the rod tension. Neither does any work, and
neither appears in the answer, but both appear in the algebra.

Lagrangian mechanics starts from energy instead, which is a scalar, and
constraint forces drop out because they do no work. Define

$$L = T - V$$

the kinetic energy minus the potential energy, written in terms of whatever
coordinates actually describe the motion.

Those are the *generalized coordinates*: one for each independent way the
system can move. A pendulum has one, the angle $\theta$. A double pendulum has
two. A bead on a fixed parabolic wire has one, because specifying $x$ fixes
$y$. Choose coordinates that already build the constraint in, and the
constraint force never has to be named.

## The Euler-Lagrange equation

For each generalized coordinate $q$,

$$\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q} = 0$$

Read the two terms physically. The quantity $\partial L / \partial \dot q$ is
the generalized momentum conjugate to $q$, and $\partial L / \partial q$ is
the generalized force. The equation says momentum changes at the rate the
force supplies, which is Newton's second law wearing different clothes.

The procedure is mechanical. Pick coordinates, write $T$ and $V$ in them, form
$L$, take the two derivatives, set them equal. No diagram, no components, no
sign hunting.

## A pendulum, end to end

A mass $m$ on a massless rod of length $\ell$, with $\theta$ measured from
straight down.

Kinetic energy: the mass moves on a circle at speed $\ell\dot\theta$, so
$T = \tfrac12 m \ell^2 \dot\theta^2$.

Potential energy: height above the lowest point is
$\ell(1 - \cos\theta)$, so $V = mg\ell(1 - \cos\theta)$.

$$L = \tfrac12 m \ell^2 \dot\theta^2 - mg\ell(1 - \cos\theta)$$

Now the derivatives:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot\theta} = m\ell^2 \dot\theta,
\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta} = -mg\ell \sin\theta$$

Substituting into Euler-Lagrange gives
$m\ell^2 \ddot\theta + mg\ell\sin\theta = 0$, that is,

$$\ddot\theta = -\frac{g}{\ell}\sin\theta$$

The rod tension never appeared. For a double pendulum, where the free-body
approach becomes genuinely painful, the same three steps produce both
equations of motion with no extra ideas.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  If a coordinate does not appear in $L$ at all, only its time derivative
  does, then $\partial L/\partial q = 0$ and the conjugate momentum
  $\partial L / \partial \dot q$ is conserved. That is where conservation laws
  come from. An ignorable angle means angular momentum is conserved; an
  ignorable position means linear momentum is.
</Aside>

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Kinetic energy must be written in an inertial frame, and every velocity in
  it must be expressed through the generalized coordinates and their time
  derivatives. The usual error is writing the speed of a mass that sits on a
  moving cart as if the cart were at rest. Write the position vector in fixed
  ground coordinates first, differentiate that, then square it.
</Aside>
