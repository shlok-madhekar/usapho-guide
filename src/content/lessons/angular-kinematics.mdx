## Why rotation gets its own variables

Put a strip of tape near the axle of a bicycle wheel and another strip out on
the rim, then spin the wheel. In one second the outer strip races through
something like a metre of arc; the inner one barely moves. If you tried to
describe the wheel by "where its parts are", you would need a different answer
for every point on it, and none of those answers would be the same two moments
running.

But something is shared. In the time the outer strip sweeps a quarter turn, so
does the inner strip. Every particle of a rigid spinning body turns through the
*same angle* in the same time. That single number describes the whole object,
and it is why rotation gets its own set of variables. In the last lesson you
replaced a swarm of particles by one point, the
[centre of mass](/guide/center-of-mass), to talk about how a system moves as a
whole. Now you need the other half: how it turns about that point.

Angular position $\theta$ is the angle between some line painted on the body
and a fixed reference direction. Positive is usually counterclockwise; that is
a convention, and you are free to pick the other one as long as you keep it.

The unit matters more than it looks. Define the radian by the rule that an
angle of $\theta$ radians subtends an arc of length

$$s = r\theta$$

on a circle of radius $r$. A full turn wraps the whole circumference, $s = 2\pi
r$, so a full turn is $2\pi$ radians. That is the entire content of "one
revolution equals $2\pi$ rad". Three and a half revolutions is
$3.5 \times 2\pi = 21.99$ rad.

Every formula below comes out of $s = r\theta$, and $s = r\theta$ is
only true in radians. Degrees and revolutions are data formats: convert them the
moment they appear, and never carry them into a calculation.

Rpm — revolutions per minute — is the commonest one you will meet. A motor at
1800 rpm turns 1800 revolutions in 60 seconds, so

$$\omega = \frac{1800 \times 2\pi\ \text{rad}}{60\ \text{s}} = 188.5\ \text{rad/s}.$$

Going the other way, the period $T$ is the time for one full turn, so
$\omega T = 2\pi$ and $T = 2\pi/\omega$. A carousel at 0.40 rad/s has
$T = 2\pi/0.40 = 15.7$ s.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Degrees are the classic wreck. Feeding 30 into $v = \omega r$ when you meant
  30 degrees per second gives an answer 57 times too large, and it will look
  perfectly plausible. Convert revolutions, degrees and rpm to radians on the
  first line of your work, before anything else happens.
</Aside>

## Rates: angular velocity and angular acceleration

Angular velocity is how fast the angle changes,

$$\omega = \frac{d\theta}{dt},$$

measured in rad/s, and angular acceleration is how fast *that* changes,

$$\alpha = \frac{d\omega}{dt} = \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2},$$

in rad/s². These are the same two derivatives that took you from $x$ to $v$ to
$a$ in a straight line. Nothing new is being claimed. If you are handed
$\theta(t)$ you differentiate; if you are handed $\alpha(t)$ you integrate.

Sign works the way it does on a line. Counterclockwise positive means a wheel
spinning counterclockwise and slowing has $\omega > 0$ and $\alpha < 0$,
exactly as a car moving forward and braking has $v > 0$ and $a < 0$. A negative
$\alpha$ does not mean "slowing"; it means "turning more clockwise per second".
Those coincide only when $\omega$ is positive.

**Worked example 1.** A disk turns so that $\theta(t) = 2t^3 - 5t$, with
$\theta$ in radians and $t$ in seconds. Find $\omega$ at $t = 2.0$ s and
$\alpha$ at $t = 3.0$ s.

Differentiate once: $\omega = d\theta/dt = 6t^2 - 5$. At $t = 2.0$,
$\omega = 6(4) - 5 = 24 - 5 = 19$ rad/s.

Differentiate again: $\alpha = d\omega/dt = 12t$. At $t = 3.0$,
$\alpha = 12(3.0) = 36$ rad/s². Note that $\alpha$ here is not constant, so the
constant-acceleration formulas in the next section would give nonsense. Check
that before you reach for them.

## When alpha is constant

If $\alpha$ is a constant, the integrals are the ones you already did for
straight-line motion. Integrating $d\omega/dt = \alpha$ from $0$ to $t$ gives
$\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t$, where $\omega_0$ is the angular velocity at
$t = 0$. Integrating that again gives the angle turned:

$$

\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t, \qquad
\theta = \omega_0 t + \tfrac12 \alpha t^2

$$

and eliminating $t$ between them gives the timeless one,

$$\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\theta.$$

Here $\theta$ means the angle turned *since the start*, not an absolute
position. Every technique you used on the linear versions carries over
untouched: pick the equation missing the variable you neither know nor want.

A flywheel at 60 rad/s stopped at 5.0 rad/s² takes
$t = (0 - 60)/(-5.0) = 12$ s. A disk from rest at 4.0 rad/s² turns
$\theta = \tfrac12 (4.0)(5.0)^2 = 50$ rad in 5.0 s. That is all these are.

**Worked example 2.** A centrifuge starts from rest and reaches 8000 rpm after
220 revolutions. Find its angular acceleration, assumed constant.

First convert, before anything else. The final angular speed is

$$\omega = \frac{8000 \times 2\pi}{60} = \frac{50265}{60} = 837.76\ \text{rad/s},$$

and the angle turned is $\theta = 220 \times 2\pi = 1382.3$ rad.

You know $\omega_0 = 0$, $\omega$, and $\theta$; you want $\alpha$ and you do
not know or care about $t$. So use the equation without $t$:

$$

\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\theta \;\Rightarrow\;
\alpha = \frac{\omega^2}{2\theta} = \frac{(837.76)^2}{2(1382.3)}
= \frac{701,846}{2764.6} = 253.9\ \text{rad/s}^2.

$$

If you had converted 8000 rpm to anything else, or left $\theta$ in
revolutions, the number would have been wrong by a factor of $2\pi$ or 60 and
nothing in the algebra would have warned you.

## From the axle back to the rim

The whole point of $\theta$ was that it describes every point at once. To get
back to what an individual point is doing, multiply by how far out it sits.

Take a point at radius $r$. In time $dt$ the body turns $d\theta$, so the point
travels an arc $ds = r\, d\theta$. Divide by $dt$:

$$v = \frac{ds}{dt} = r\frac{d\theta}{dt} = \omega r.$$

That is its speed along its circular path. A rim point 0.35 m out on a wheel at
20 rad/s moves at $v = 20 \times 0.35 = 7.0$ m/s. Differentiate again, holding
$r$ fixed, and you get the rate at which that *speed* changes:

$$a_t = \frac{dv}{dt} = r\frac{d\omega}{dt} = \alpha r.$$

This is the **tangential acceleration** — tangential because it points along
the direction of motion. It is the piece that makes the point go faster or
slower.

But a point going in a circle is accelerating even at constant speed, because
its direction keeps turning. That is the **centripetal acceleration** you met
with circular motion, $a_c = v^2/r$, which in angular language is

$$a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{(\omega r)^2}{r} = \omega^2 r,$$

pointing straight at the axis. A point 0.20 m out on a wheel at 30 rad/s has
$a_c = (30)^2(0.20) = 900 \times 0.20 = 180$ m/s².

These two pieces are perpendicular: one along the motion, one across it. So the
total acceleration of the point has magnitude

$$a = \sqrt{a_t^2 + a_c^2} = r\sqrt{\alpha^2 + \omega^4},$$

and it is tilted away from the velocity by an angle $\phi$ with

$$\tan\phi = \frac{a_c}{a_t} = \frac{\omega^2}{\alpha}.$$

The radius cancels out of that angle. Every point on the body, near the axle or
out at the rim, has its acceleration tilted by the same $\phi$.

**Worked example 3.** A point on a wheel of radius 0.25 m has $\omega = 4.0$
rad/s and $\alpha = 8.0$ rad/s². What angle does its total acceleration make
with its velocity?

The velocity is tangential, so the angle you want is the angle between the
total acceleration and the tangential direction. The two components are

$$

a_t = \alpha r = 8.0 \times 0.25 = 2.0\ \text{m/s}^2, \qquad
a_c = \omega^2 r = 16 \times 0.25 = 4.0\ \text{m/s}^2.

$$

The centripetal part is across the velocity, the tangential part along it, so

$$

\tan\phi = \frac{a_c}{a_t} = \frac{4.0}{2.0} = 2.0,
\qquad \phi = \arctan(2.0) = 63.4^\circ.

$$

The radius did cancel, as promised: $\tan\phi = \omega^2/\alpha = 16/8 = 2$
without ever using 0.25 m.

## Ropes, spools and rolling

Whenever something flexible runs over a wheel without slipping, the contact
forces its speed to match the rim's. A cord unwinding from a spool of radius
$r$ pays out length at exactly the rate the rim moves, so $v_{\text{cord}} =
\omega r$ and $a_{\text{cord}} = \alpha r$. Pull a cord off a 0.12 m spool from
rest at a steady 2.0 m/s²: then $\alpha = a/r = 2.0/0.12 = 16.67$ rad/s², and
after 4.0 s the spool turns at $\omega = \alpha t = 16.67 \times 4.0 = 66.7$
rad/s.

A wheel rolling on the ground is the same condition in disguise. "Rolling
without slipping" means the patch of rim touching the ground is not sliding
along it — at that instant, the contact point is at rest. Work out what that
forces. The axle moves forward at $v$; relative to the axle, every rim point
moves at $\omega r$. At the bottom of the wheel the rim's motion relative to
the axle points backwards, so the ground-frame speed there is $v - \omega r$.
Setting that to zero gives the rolling condition:

$$v = \omega r, \qquad a = \alpha r.$$

Now add the two motions everywhere else. At the *top*, the rim's motion
relative to the axle points forwards, in the same direction as $v$, so

$$v_{\text{top}} = v + \omega r = 2v.$$

A wheel of radius 0.30 m rolling at 4.0 m/s has a top point moving at 8.0 m/s.
This is not a trick: the top of a rolling wheel really does move twice as fast
as the car it carries, and the bottom is momentarily stationary.

At the side, level with the axle, the relative motion is *vertical* while the
axle's motion is horizontal. They are perpendicular, so you add them as
vectors:

$$

v_{\text{side}} = \sqrt{v^2 + (\omega r)^2} = \sqrt{v^2 + v^2} = v\sqrt{2}
= 4.0 \times 1.414 = 5.66\ \text{m/s}.

$$

In general, a point whose angle from the contact point is $\psi$ has speed
$2v\sin(\psi/2)$, which gives 0 at the bottom, $v\sqrt2$ at the side and $2v$
at the top. Rolling also links turns to distance travelled: one revolution
rolls out one circumference, so 40 turns of a 0.34 m wheel covers
$40 \times 2\pi \times 0.34 = 85.5$ m.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  The bottom of a rolling wheel is instantaneously at rest. That is the whole
  content of "rolling without slipping", and every other rolling result —
  $v = \omega r$, the doubled speed at the top, the cusped path — is squeezed
  out of it.
</Aside>

## When alpha is not constant

Two kinds of problem break the constant-$\alpha$ formulas, and each has a
standard escape.

**Alpha given as a function of time.** Then you integrate. If a wheel starts
from rest with $\alpha(t) = 6t$, integrate once for the angular velocity:

$$\omega(t) = \int_0^t 6t'\,dt' = 3t^2,$$

with no constant because $\omega(0) = 0$. Integrate again for the angle:

$$\theta(t) = \int_0^t 3t'^2\,dt' = t^3.$$

At $t = 4.0$ s, $\theta = 4.0^3 = 64$ rad. Had you wrongly used
$\theta = \tfrac12\alpha t^2$ with the $\alpha$ at $t=4$, you would have got
$\tfrac12(24)(16) = 192$ rad, three times too big.

**Alpha given in terms of omega.** A rotor coasting against a drag torque
proportional to speed obeys $\alpha = -k\omega$. You cannot integrate that in
$t$ directly, because the right side keeps changing. Separate the variables:

$$

\frac{d\omega}{dt} = -k\omega \;\Rightarrow\;
\frac{d\omega}{\omega} = -k\,dt \;\Rightarrow\;
\ln\frac{\omega}{\omega_0} = -kt \;\Rightarrow\;
\omega = \omega_0 e^{-kt}.

$$

With $\omega_0 = 12$ rad/s and $k = 0.25$ s⁻¹, after 6.0 s

$$

\omega = 12 e^{-0.25 \times 6.0} = 12 e^{-1.5} = 12 \times 0.2231
= 2.68\ \text{rad/s}.

$$

It never quite reaches zero, but the total angle is still finite. To get it,
switch the variable you differentiate against. Use the chain rule to trade $t$
for $\theta$:

$$

\alpha = \frac{d\omega}{dt} = \frac{d\omega}{d\theta}\frac{d\theta}{dt}
= \omega\frac{d\omega}{d\theta}.

$$

That identity is worth memorising; it is the rotational twin of $a = v\,dv/dx$
and it is the tool for anything that depends on position rather than time.
Putting $\alpha = -k\omega$ into it,

$$

\omega\frac{d\omega}{d\theta} = -k\omega \;\Rightarrow\;
\frac{d\omega}{d\theta} = -k,

$$

so $\omega$ falls off *linearly in angle*, even though it falls off
exponentially in time. Starting at $\omega_0$ and ending at $\omega = 0$:

$$\theta_{\text{total}} = \frac{\omega_0}{k} = \frac{12}{0.25} = 48\ \text{rad}.$$

## Two things going round at once

When two objects circle the same axis, subtract their angles the way you
subtract positions in a relative-velocity problem. The angle by which the
faster one leads is $\Delta\theta = (\omega_1 - \omega_2)t$, and it laps the
slower one when that gap reaches a full turn:

$$

(\omega_1 - \omega_2)t = 2\pi \;\Rightarrow\;
t = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_1 - \omega_2}.

$$

Two runners on a track at 0.16 and 0.10 rad/s have a relative angular speed of
0.06 rad/s, so the lap happens at $t = 2\pi/0.06 = 104.7$ s. The radius of the
track, 50 m, never enters — lapping is about angle, not distance. That is a
good habit to notice: if the question is about who is where, work in angle and
leave the radius alone until the very end.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Angular kinematics has no new physics in it. Every result here is a linear
  kinematics result with $x \to \theta$, $v \to \omega$, $a \to \alpha$, plus
  the single bridge $s = r\theta$ that carries you back to metres when the
  question asks for them.
</Aside>

## Recognising the technique

You are in an angular kinematics problem when the quantities given are about
turning rather than travelling: revolutions, rpm, a period, "how many turns",
an angle in radians, a radius paired with a rate. Then:

- **Everything constant, a wheel spinning up or down uniformly.** Convert to
  radians and rad/s, then use the three constant-$\alpha$ equations, choosing
  the one that omits the variable you neither have nor want. If the problem
  gives you revolutions and asks for time, that is $\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha
  t$ after converting.
- **The answer is wanted in metres or m/s, but the data is angular** (or the
  reverse). You need $s = r\theta$, $v = \omega r$ or $a_t = \alpha r$ — one
  multiplication by the radius, at the end.
- **A question about the acceleration of a point, not of the body.** Two
  perpendicular pieces: $a_t = \alpha r$ along the motion and $a_c = \omega^2
  r$ toward the axis. Combine with Pythagoras, and use $\tan\phi = \omega^2/\alpha$
  for the direction.
- **A cord, belt, chain or a wheel on the ground, with "does not slip".** That
  phrase is the equation $v = \omega r$. Write it down first.
- **$\alpha$ given as a formula.** If it depends on $t$, integrate in $t$. If
  it depends on $\omega$ or $\theta$, separate variables, and reach for
  $\alpha = \omega\, d\omega/d\theta$ the moment the question asks for a total
  angle rather than a time.
- **Two things going round together.** Work with the difference of the angles,
  and set it to $2\pi$ for a lap.

The one mistake that costs more marks than all the others is a unit left
unconverted. Write the radian conversion on the first line, every time, even
when it feels unnecessary.
