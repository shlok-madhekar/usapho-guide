## An equation of state

$PV = nRT$ is not a law of physics in the way that $F = ma$ is. It is a
constraint linking the three variables that describe a gas, so that only two
of them are ever free. Fix any two and the third follows.

$$PV = nRT = Nk_BT$$

Use $n$ and $R = 8.31$ J/(mol·K) when you are counting moles, $N$ and
$k_B = 1.38\times10^{-23}$ J/K when you are counting molecules. They are the
same statement divided by Avogadro's number.

The practical consequence for problem solving is that any two states of a
fixed amount of gas satisfy $P_1V_1/T_1 = P_2V_2/T_2$. Most numerical
thermodynamics questions never need $R$ at all — they need this ratio.

## Temperature is energy per degree of freedom

The microscopic reading is that $k_BT$ is an energy scale. Specifically, every
independent quadratic term in a molecule's energy — each $\tfrac12 mv_x^2$,
each $\tfrac12 I\omega^2$, each $\tfrac12 kx^2$ in a vibrational mode —
carries $\tfrac12 k_BT$ on average once the system is in equilibrium. That is
equipartition, and it is what "degree of freedom" means: not a way the
molecule can move, but a quadratic energy term.

Counting for common cases:

- Monatomic: 3 translational. $f = 3$.
- Diatomic at room temperature: 3 translational, 2 rotational. $f = 5$.
- Diatomic, hot: add 2 more for the vibrational mode, since a spring
  contributes both kinetic and potential terms. $f = 7$.

Rotation about the bond axis of a diatomic does not count. Its moment of
inertia is tiny, and the energy needed to excite it is far above $k_BT$.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Equipartition is a classical result and modes freeze out when their spacing
  exceeds $k_BT$. Hydrogen behaves with $f = 3$ below about 100 K, $f = 5$ near
  room temperature, and $f = 7$ only above a few thousand kelvin. If a problem
  gives you a temperature, it is usually telling you which count to use.
</Aside>

## From counting to $\gamma$

Once you have $f$, everything else is arithmetic:

$$U = \tfrac{f}{2}nRT, \qquad C_V = \tfrac{f}{2}R, \qquad C_P = C_V + R$$

$$\gamma = \frac{C_P}{C_V} = \frac{f+2}{f}$$

So $\gamma = 5/3$ for monatomic, $7/5$ for diatomic, $4/3$ for a molecule with
six active degrees of freedom. That single number then governs every adiabatic
process, the speed of sound in the gas, and the shape of the steep curves on a
PV diagram — all of it traceable back to how many ways one molecule can hold
energy.
