## A nucleus weighs less than its parts

Weigh a helium-4 nucleus and compare it with two free protons plus two free
neutrons. The nucleus is lighter, by about 0.5% — a real, measurable
discrepancy called the **mass defect**:

$$\Delta m = Zm_p + (A - Z)m_n - m_{nucleus}$$

The missing mass left as energy when the nucleus formed. Equivalently, that
much energy must be supplied to pull it apart again, which is what "binding
energy" means:

$$E_B = \Delta m\, c^2$$

Work in atomic mass units and the conversion is $1\ \text{u} = 931.5$ MeV/$c^2$,
which turns this into arithmetic. Helium-4 comes out at 28.3 MeV.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Binding energy is not stored in the nucleus like a charged battery. It is
  energy already released. A more tightly bound nucleus has *less* mass and
  *more* binding energy, which is why the sign confuses people. If in doubt,
  ask which side of the reaction is lighter — that side is more stable.
</Aside>

## The curve

Total binding energy grows with $A$ simply because there are more nucleons, so
it says little about stability. Divide by $A$ instead and the useful quantity
appears: binding energy per nucleon, in MeV.

That curve rises steeply from hydrogen, passes 7 MeV around carbon, peaks near
8.8 MeV at iron-56 and nickel-62, then declines slowly to about 7.6 MeV at
uranium.

Two competing effects shape it. The strong force is short-ranged, so each
nucleon binds only to its neighbours — that saturates quickly and explains the
early rise and the flat top. Coulomb repulsion is long-ranged, so every proton
pushes on every other, and that term grows as $Z^2$ and drags the heavy end
down.

## Both directions release energy

Energy comes out of any reaction that moves nucleons toward the peak.

**Fission** splits a heavy nucleus into two mid-sized fragments, climbing from
7.6 to roughly 8.5 MeV per nucleon. With 236 nucleons involved that is about
200 MeV per event, mostly as kinetic energy of the fragments.

**Fusion** joins light nuclei, climbing much more steeply — deuterium plus
tritium to helium-4 releases 17.6 MeV from just five nucleons. Per nucleon,
fusion wins by a wide margin, which is why stars run on it.

Neither contradicts the other. Both are downhill on the same curve, approached
from opposite sides, and nothing releases energy by fusing past iron. That is
also why iron is the endpoint of stellar burning and why heavier elements
require a supernova to make.

For any reaction, compute $Q = (m_{initial} - m_{final})c^2$. Positive $Q$
means energy released and the reaction can proceed spontaneously as far as
energy is concerned; negative $Q$ means the energy must be supplied.
