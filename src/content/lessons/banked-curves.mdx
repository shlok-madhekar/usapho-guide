## Circular motion needs a horizontal force

A car rounding a curve of radius $r$ at speed $v$ accelerates toward the centre
of the circle, with magnitude $v^2/r$. That acceleration is horizontal, and
something has to supply it. On a flat road it is friction. Bank the road and
the normal force can do the job instead.

Tilt the surface by an angle $\theta$. The normal force is perpendicular to the
road, so it now has a horizontal component pointing inward. With no friction,
the two axes read:

$$N\sin\theta = \frac{mv^2}{r}, \qquad N\cos\theta = mg$$

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Tilting the axes to lie along the incline, as you would for a block sliding
  down a ramp. Do not. The acceleration here is horizontal, pointing at the
  centre of the circle, so use horizontal and vertical axes and let $N$ be the
  vector you decompose. Choosing axes along the acceleration is the rule; on a
  banked curve the acceleration is not along the slope.
</Aside>

## Design speed

Divide the two equations and $N$ and $m$ both vanish:

$$\tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{rg}$$

This is the **design speed** of the bank: the one speed at which the curve can
be taken with no friction at all. Highway engineers pick $\theta$ for the
expected traffic speed, so an icy banked ramp is safe at the posted speed and
only at that speed.

Go faster and the horizontal component of $N$ is not enough; you need friction
pointing down the slope to make up the difference. Go slower and you slide
*inward*, needing friction pointing up the slope. That is the useful mental
picture: friction is a correction that covers the mismatch between your actual
speed and the design speed, and it is bounded by $\mu_s N$ in either direction.
So a real banked curve has a range of safe speeds around $\sqrt{rg\tan\theta}$,
not a single value.

Note that mass dropped out. A loaded truck and a motorcycle have the same
design speed on the same curve.

## The conical pendulum

A mass on a string of length $L$ swings in a horizontal circle while the string
traces out a cone at angle $\theta$ from the vertical. The forces are gravity
and tension only, and the analysis is the same shape as the banked curve with
$T$ in place of $N$:

$$T\sin\theta = \frac{mv^2}{r}, \qquad T\cos\theta = mg$$

with $r = L\sin\theta$. Dividing gives $\tan\theta = v^2/(rg)$ again. Writing it
in terms of the period is more revealing — substitute $v = 2\pi r/T_{\text{per}}$
and the radius cancels:

$$T_{\text{per}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L\cos\theta}{g}}$$

The vertical height of the pivot above the circle, $L\cos\theta$, is what sets
the period — not the radius, not the speed, not the mass. Push the cone wider
and the mass moves faster but takes less time per revolution. As $\theta \to
90^\circ$ the required speed diverges: the string can never reach horizontal,
because a horizontal string has no vertical component to hold up the weight.
