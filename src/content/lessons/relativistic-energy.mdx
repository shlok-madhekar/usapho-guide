## Energy that does not vanish at rest

In mechanics a stationary object has no kinetic energy and that is the end of
it. Relativity says a stationary object still has energy, proportional to its
mass, and that this rest energy is the floor everything else sits on.

$$E = \gamma mc^2, \qquad E_0 = mc^2, \qquad K = (\gamma - 1)mc^2$$

Kinetic energy is total energy minus rest energy — nothing more exotic than
that. Expand $\gamma$ for small $v$ and $K$ collapses to $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, as
it must.

Momentum picks up the same factor:

$$p = \gamma m v$$

Written this way, momentum has no ceiling even though $v$ does. Push a particle
forever and $v \to c$ while $p \to \infty$. That is why accelerators are rated
in energy, not speed: past a few MeV the speed stops being interesting.

## The invariant

Combining the two definitions eliminates $\gamma$ and $v$ entirely:

$$E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2$$

Every observer computes a different $E$ and a different $p$, but the
combination $E^2 - (pc)^2$ comes out the same for all of them. It equals
$(mc^2)^2$, and mass is frame-independent. Treat this as a right triangle with
legs $pc$ and $mc^2$ and hypotenuse $E$; the whole subject fits in that picture.

Set $m = 0$ and the triangle degenerates:

$$E = pc$$

A photon has momentum despite having no mass. It cannot be slowed, only
absorbed or redshifted, and $p = E/c = h/\lambda$ is what lets light push on a
solar sail.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Do not write $K = \tfrac{1}{2}\gamma m v^2$ or define a velocity-dependent
  "relativistic mass." Both give wrong answers in collisions. Mass is a fixed
  property; the $\gamma$ belongs to energy and momentum, not to $m$.
</Aside>

## Using it

In a collision or decay, conserve total $E$ and total $\vec{p}$ separately —
they are conserved even when kinetic energy is not, and even when particle
count changes. Mass is not conserved: a pion at rest decaying into two photons
turns $mc^2$ entirely into photon energy.

Work in units of eV and eV/c and the arithmetic gets short. An electron's rest
energy is 0.511 MeV, a proton's 938 MeV. A 1 MeV electron is therefore mostly
kinetic and comfortably relativistic; a 1 MeV proton is barely moving.
