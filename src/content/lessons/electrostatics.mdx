## Gauss's law is a statement about symmetry

The flux of the electric field through a closed surface counts the charge
inside it:

$$\oint \vec E \cdot d\vec A = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}$$

This is always true and only occasionally useful. It becomes a calculation
tool when the geometry has enough symmetry that $E$ is constant over a
surface you can choose, so it comes out of the integral. Three cases cover
almost everything:

- Spherical symmetry: use a concentric sphere. Outside, $E = kQ/r^2$; inside a uniformly charged ball, $E \propto r$.
- Cylindrical symmetry: use a coaxial can. $E = \lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0 r$.
- Planar symmetry: use a pillbox. $E = \sigma/2\varepsilon_0$, independent of distance.

## Conductors

In equilibrium the field inside conducting material is zero, all excess charge
sits on surfaces, and every point of the conductor is at the same potential.
Those three facts are really one fact: if any field remained inside, charges
would still be moving and it would not be equilibrium yet.

The consequence worth internalizing is what a hollow conductor does. Put a
charge in the cavity and induced charge appears on the inner surface to cancel
its field within the metal, with the balance pushed to the outer surface. The
outside world learns the total enclosed charge and nothing else about its
position.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Gauss's law relates the flux of the field produced by *all* charges to the
  charge *enclosed*. Those are different sets. When symmetry breaks, as with an
  off-center cavity, the law still holds but no longer determines $E$ by
  itself, and superposition has to finish the job.
</Aside>
