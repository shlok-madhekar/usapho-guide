## A force that only notices things that move

Put a proton at rest in the gap of a strong magnet and nothing happens. It just
sits there. Now flick it sideways at a hundred kilometres a second and it swings
around in a tight circle, comes back, and keeps circling forever. Nothing about
the magnet changed. The only thing that changed was the proton's velocity.

That is the strange part of magnetism, and it is worth sitting with before you
touch a formula. In the circuits you have just come from, current was pushed by
a potential difference and the force on a charge pointed along the field. Here
the force depends on how fast the charge is going, and it does not point along
the field, and it does not point along the velocity either. It points off to
the side, perpendicular to both.

## The force law, and the direction

Experiment says the force on a charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec{v}$
through a magnetic field $\vec{B}$ has three properties. It is proportional to
$q$, proportional to $v$, and it vanishes when $\vec{v}$ lies along $\vec{B}$.
It is also always perpendicular to both $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{B}$. There is
exactly one product of two vectors that behaves that way, the cross product, so

$$\vec{F} = q\,\vec{v} \times \vec{B}$$

The magnitude follows from the definition of the cross product:

$$F = |q|\,v\,B\sin\theta$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{B}$. If the charge
moves straight across the field, $\theta = 90^\circ$ and you get the maximum,
$F = qvB$. If it moves along the field, $\sin 0 = 0$ and there is no force at
all. $B$ is measured in tesla: one tesla pushes a one-coulomb charge moving at
one metre per second across it with one newton of force. A tesla is a big
field. The Earth's is about $5\times10^{-5}$ T, a fridge magnet a hundredth of
a tesla, an MRI magnet a few tesla.

The direction comes from the right-hand rule. Point your fingers along
$\vec{v}$, curl them toward $\vec{B}$, and your thumb points along
$\vec{v}\times\vec{B}$. That is the force *if $q$ is positive*. For an electron
the force is the opposite way, because $q$ is negative.

Try the easy end first. A proton at $2.0\times10^{5}$ m/s crossing a $0.50$ T
field perpendicular feels

$$F = (1.6\times10^{-19})(2.0\times10^{5})(0.50) = 1.6\times10^{-14}\ \text{N}$$

Tiny in absolute terms, but the proton's mass is $1.67\times10^{-27}$ kg, so
the acceleration is about $10^{13}$ m/s$^2$. Small forces do dramatic things to
small masses.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  The angle $\theta$ in $qvB\sin\theta$ is the angle between the velocity and
  the field, not the angle to some surface or wall. An electron moving at
  $30^\circ$ to $\vec{B}$ feels $qvB\sin 30^\circ = 0.5\,qvB$, not
  $qvB\cos 30^\circ$. Draw the two vectors tail to tail before you decide which
  trig function you need.
</Aside>

## Magnetic forces do no work

Because $\vec{F}$ is always perpendicular to $\vec{v}$, the power it delivers is

$$P = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{v} = q(\vec{v}\times\vec{B})\cdot\vec{v} = 0$$

A magnetic field can never change a particle's speed or its kinetic energy. It
can only bend the path. That is one of the most useful constraints in this
whole unit. If a solution has a magnetic field speeding a particle up, the
solution is wrong. If a particle enters a field region at $1.0\times10^{6}$ m/s,
it leaves at $1.0\times10^{6}$ m/s, however long it spent inside and however
much it turned.

## Circles, periods, and the radius formula

Fire a charge into a uniform field perpendicular to the field lines. The force
has constant magnitude $qvB$ (the speed never changes, so neither does the
force) and it always points at right angles to the motion. Constant-magnitude,
always-perpendicular force is exactly the recipe for uniform circular motion.
So set the magnetic force equal to what circular motion demands:

$$qvB = \frac{mv^2}{r}$$

One power of $v$ cancels off each side, and

$$r = \frac{mv}{qB} = \frac{p}{qB}$$

Read that. Radius is proportional to momentum and inversely proportional to
field. A heavier or faster particle is harder to bend, a stronger field bends it
more. Bending a beam through a known field and measuring the curvature is how
particle detectors weigh what went through them.

The period is stranger. Going once around covers $2\pi r$ at speed $v$, so

$$T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi m}{qB}$$

The speed has vanished. A fast particle traces a big circle, a slow one traces
a small circle, and they take exactly the same time. That fixed frequency,
$f = qB/2\pi m$, is called the cyclotron frequency, and it is what lets a
cyclotron drive every orbit, from the first tiny one to the last huge one, with
a single fixed-frequency voltage.

### Worked example: a mass spectrometer

A singly charged ion starts from rest, falls through a potential difference of
$2000$ V, then enters a $0.50$ T field perpendicular to its velocity and bends
on a circle of radius $0.15$ m. What is its mass?

Two stages, so two equations. First, the electrical stage gives it energy:

$$qV = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{\frac{2qV}{m}}$$

Second, the magnetic stage sets the radius, $r = mv/qB$. You do not know $v$ and
you do not know $m$, so eliminate $v$. Substituting,

$$r = \frac{m}{qB}\sqrt{\frac{2qV}{m}} = \frac{1}{B}\sqrt{\frac{2Vm}{q}}$$

Square it and solve for the mass:

$$m = \frac{qB^2r^2}{2V}$$

Now the arithmetic. $B^2 = 0.25$ T$^2$, $r^2 = 0.0225$ m$^2$, so
$B^2r^2 = 5.625\times10^{-3}$. Then

$$m = \frac{(1.6\times10^{-19})(5.625\times10^{-3})}{2(2000)} = \frac{9.0\times10^{-22}}{4000} = 2.25\times10^{-25}\ \text{kg}$$

About 135 atomic mass units. That $m \propto B^2r^2/V$ relation is the whole
instrument: two isotopes accelerated through the same voltage arrive at radii
in the ratio $\sqrt{m}$, and if you bend them a half circle onto a plate, the
landing spots are $2(r_2 - r_1)$ apart.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  A particle that enters a slab of field and leaves before completing a circle
  has turned through an angle $\phi$ given by $\sin\phi = d/r$, where $d$ is the
  distance travelled across the slab and $r$ is the cyclotron radius. Draw the
  circle, drop the perpendicular, and it is just geometry. For a proton at
  $1.0\times10^{6}$ m/s in $0.10$ T, $r = 0.104$ m, so a $5.0$ cm slab gives
  $\sin\phi = 0.479$ and $\phi \approx 28.6^\circ$.
</Aside>

If the velocity is not perpendicular to the field, split it. The component
$v_\parallel = v\cos\theta$ along $\vec{B}$ feels no force at all and just
coasts. The component $v_\perp = v\sin\theta$ across the field circles as
before. Together they trace a helix: a circle of radius $mv_\perp/qB$ drifting
steadily along the field. The distance it advances in one full turn is the
**pitch**,

$$\text{pitch} = v_\parallel T = v\cos\theta \cdot \frac{2\pi m}{qB}$$

## Crossed fields

Put $\vec{E}$ and $\vec{B}$ perpendicular to each other and send a charge
through perpendicular to both. The electric force $qE$ pushes one way, the
magnetic force $qvB$ the other. They cancel when

$$qE = qvB \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = \frac{E}{B}$$

The charge and the mass both drop out. Every particle at exactly that speed
sails straight through whatever it is; everything slower or faster gets
deflected into a wall. That is a velocity selector, and it is the front end of
most mass spectrometers, because $r = mv/qB$ is only useful if you know $v$.

A charge released from *rest* in crossed fields does something more interesting.
It has no magnetic force at first, so $E$ accelerates it; as it picks up speed
the magnetic force grows and curves it around, eventually turning it back and
slowing it to rest again. The path is a cycloid, the curve a point on a rolling
wheel traces. Since magnetism does no work, all the kinetic energy came from
$E$, and the particle is momentarily at rest again after drifting a distance
$d$ sideways. The fastest point is halfway, and working through the geometry
gives a maximum speed of $v_{\max} = 2E/B$ — for $E = 1.0\times10^{4}$ V/m and
$B = 0.20$ T, that is $1.0\times10^{5}$ m/s.

## Wires, because a wire is just charges moving

A current is a stream of charges with a drift velocity. Take a straight
segment of length $L$ carrying current $I$. In a time $\Delta t$ the charge that
passes is $q = I\Delta t$, and it moves a distance $v\Delta t = L$. So summing
$q\vec{v}\times\vec{B}$ over all the carriers gives $qvB\sin\theta =
(I\Delta t)(L/\Delta t)B\sin\theta$, and the $\Delta t$ cancels:

$$\vec{F} = I\vec{L}\times\vec{B}, \qquad F = ILB\sin\theta$$

$\vec{L}$ points along the wire in the direction of conventional current. A
$0.40$ m wire carrying $5.0$ A perpendicular to a $0.30$ T field feels
$F = (5.0)(0.40)(0.30) = 0.60$ N. That is a force you can feel with your hand,
which is why motors are built out of current-carrying wire and not out of
individual protons.

For a bent wire you integrate $I\,d\vec{\ell}\times\vec{B}$, but in a *uniform*
field $\vec{B}$ comes outside the integral and $\int d\vec{\ell}$ depends only
on the endpoints. So a bent wire in a uniform field feels the same force as the
straight wire joining its ends. A semicircle of radius $0.10$ m carrying
$4.0$ A perpendicular to a $0.25$ T field feels the force of its $0.20$ m
diameter: $F = (4.0)(0.20)(0.25) = 0.20$ N. And a closed loop in a uniform
field feels zero net force, since its endpoints coincide.

Two parallel wires each sit in the other's field. Using the field of a long wire
that you will derive properly in the next lesson, $B = \mu_0 I/2\pi d$, the
force per unit length is

$$\frac{F}{L} = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi d}$$

Same direction, they attract; opposite, they repel. Two bus bars $5.0$ cm apart
carrying $10$ A each pull on one another with
$(4\pi\times10^{-7})(100)/(2\pi \times 0.05) = 4.0\times10^{-4}$ N per metre.

### Worked example: starting a rod against friction

A $0.20$ kg rod lies across horizontal rails $0.40$ m apart in a vertical
$0.50$ T field. The static friction coefficient is $0.30$. What current just
starts it moving?

Start with what is on the paper: the field is vertical, the rod is horizontal
and perpendicular to it, so $\vec{I}\vec{L}\times\vec{B}$ is horizontal, along
the rails. Good, that is the direction the rod would slide. Its magnitude is
$ILB$ with no $\sin\theta$ correction.

The rod is on the verge of moving when that force equals the largest static
friction the rails can supply. Nothing pushes the rod down or up, so the normal
force is just the weight, $N = mg$, and

$$ILB = \mu mg \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I = \frac{\mu m g}{LB}$$

Numbers: $\mu m g = (0.30)(0.20)(9.8) = 0.588$ N, and $LB = (0.40)(0.50) = 0.20$
T$\cdot$m. So $I = 0.588/0.20 = 2.94$ A.

## Loops: moment, torque, and small oscillations

A loop feels no net force in a uniform field, but it does feel a twist. Picture
a rectangular loop of area $A$ carrying current $I$, with its plane containing
the field. The two sides perpendicular to $\vec{B}$ feel forces $ILB$ in
opposite directions, offset by the width of the loop. Equal and opposite forces
on different lines of action are a couple: no net force, pure torque. Working
out the lever arms gives $\tau = IAB$ for that orientation, and $N$ turns
multiply it by $N$.

Define the **magnetic dipole moment** $\mu = NIA$, a vector pointing along the
normal to the loop (right hand curled the way the current flows, thumb along
$\vec{\mu}$). Then for any orientation

$$\vec{\tau} = \vec{\mu}\times\vec{B}, \qquad \tau = \mu B \sin\alpha$$

where $\alpha$ is the angle between $\vec{\mu}$ and $\vec{B}$. Maximum torque
when the loop's plane contains the field ($\alpha = 90^\circ$); zero torque when
the normal lines up with the field. The associated potential energy is
$U = -\vec{\mu}\cdot\vec{B} = -\mu B\cos\alpha$, lowest when aligned. Rotating a
$0.50$ A$\cdot$m$^2$ moment from aligned to perpendicular in a $0.20$ T field
costs $\Delta U = 0 - (-0.10) = 0.10$ J of external work.

### Worked example: a coil that oscillates

A coil of $200$ turns, area $4.0\times10^{-4}$ m$^2$, carries $0.50$ A and hangs
so it can rotate about a vertical axis in a horizontal $0.020$ T field. Its
moment of inertia is $1.2\times10^{-6}$ kg$\cdot$m$^2$. Find the period of small
oscillations about the aligned position.

The aligned position is the energy minimum, so nudge it and it swings back. The
restoring torque at a small angle $\alpha$ is

$$\tau = -\mu B\sin\alpha \approx -\mu B \alpha$$

using $\sin\alpha \approx \alpha$ for small angles. That is a restoring torque
proportional to displacement, which is angular simple harmonic motion:
$I_{\text{rot}}\ddot\alpha = -\mu B\alpha$, so the effective torsion constant is
$\kappa = \mu B$ and

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I_{\text{rot}}}{\mu B}}$$

Compute the moment first. $\mu = NIA = (200)(0.50)(4.0\times10^{-4}) =
4.0\times10^{-2}$ A$\cdot$m$^2$. Then $\mu B = (4.0\times10^{-2})(0.020) =
8.0\times10^{-4}$ N$\cdot$m per radian. So

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{1.2\times10^{-6}}{8.0\times10^{-4}}} = 2\pi\sqrt{1.5\times10^{-3}} = 2\pi(0.0387) = 0.243\ \text{s}$$

That is the pendulum formula with $\mu B$ playing the role of $mg\ell$. Whenever
you see a dipole nudged from alignment, expect this.

## Recognising the technique

Most magnetic force problems announce themselves in one of five ways.

**A charge and a speed and a field.** If the question gives you a moving charge
and asks for a force, it is $qvB\sin\theta$ and the only work is finding
$\theta$. If it asks for a radius, a period, a frequency, or a kinetic energy,
you want $r = mv/qB$ and $T = 2\pi m/qB$, and you pick whichever one contains
the quantity you were given. Energy questions go through $K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$
with $v = qBr/m$.

**Two stages, one electric and one magnetic.** A potential difference
accelerating an ion and then a field bending it means eliminate $v$ between
$qV = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ and $r = mv/qB$. Any problem with a voltage and a radius
in the same sentence is this problem.

**A velocity that is not perpendicular to the field.** Split it into parallel
and perpendicular pieces immediately. The parallel piece coasts, the
perpendicular piece circles, and the words "helix" or "pitch" confirm it.

**A wire or a rod.** Then it is $ILB\sin\theta$, and the rest is a free body
diagram of the sort you have drawn many times: balance it against gravity for a
floating wire, against $\mu N$ for friction, against $ma$ for an accelerating
rod. Two wires means $\mu_0 I_1 I_2/2\pi d$ per unit length.

**A loop, a coil, or the word "turns".** Compute $\mu = NIA$ first, always, then
reach for $\tau = \mu B\sin\alpha$, $U = -\mu B\cos\alpha$, or the oscillation
period. Do not try to add up forces on the individual sides; the dipole moment
already did that for you.

Everything here has taken the field as given. The next lesson asks where the
field came from in the first place, and gives you the tool for computing it from
the currents that make it: the [Biot-Savart law](/guide/biot-savart).
