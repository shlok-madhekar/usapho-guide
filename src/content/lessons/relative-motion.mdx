## Velocities add

Every velocity is measured relative to something. Once you say so explicitly,
the bookkeeping becomes one rule:

$$\vec{v}_{A/C} = \vec{v}_{A/B} + \vec{v}_{B/C}$$

Read the subscripts as a chain. The inner labels match and cancel, leaving the
outer pair. Reversing a subscript pair flips the sign:
$\vec{v}_{B/A} = -\vec{v}_{A/B}$.

These are vector sums, not number sums. A plane flying north at 200 km/h
through air drifting east at 50 km/h moves over the ground at
$\sqrt{200^2 + 50^2} \approx 206$ km/h, on a heading tilted east of north.
Accelerations add the same way, provided neither frame is rotating.

## Crossing a river

A swimmer moves at speed $u$ relative to the water; the river runs at $v$
across a channel of width $w$. Two different questions get two different
answers.

To cross in the least time, aim straight across. The downstream current does
nothing to the across-channel component, so $t = w/u$ no matter how fast the
river runs. You land downstream, but you land soonest.

To land directly opposite, aim upstream at angle $\theta$ with
$\sin\theta = v/u$, so the upstream component cancels the drift. The remaining
across-channel speed is $\sqrt{u^2 - v^2}$ and the crossing takes
$t = w/\sqrt{u^2 - v^2}$. If $v \ge u$, that has no solution: a swimmer slower
than the current cannot reach the point opposite at all.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Decide which velocity the given speed describes before drawing anything.
  "Swims at 2 m/s" almost always means relative to the water, and the current
  is relative to the bank. Mixing the two frames in one triangle is the error
  that quietly ruins river and wind problems.
</Aside>

## Choosing the frame

The real payoff is that a good frame can delete most of a problem.

Two objects approaching each other: work in the frame of one of them. It is at
rest, the other closes at the relative velocity, and the time to meet is just
separation divided by that speed.

Two projectiles launched simultaneously: in the frame of the first, gravity
cancels out of the relative motion entirely, since both accelerate at $-g$. The
second moves in a straight line at constant relative velocity. A question about
whether they collide becomes a question about whether a line passes through a
point.

Any inertial frame gives the same physics, so pick the one where the answer is
easiest to see, then translate back at the end.
