## Energy per unit charge

The electrostatic force is conservative, so moving a charge between two points
costs work that does not depend on the path. Divide that work out by the
charge and what remains is a property of the field alone:

$$\Delta V = \frac{\Delta U}{q} = -\int_a^b \vec E \cdot d\vec\ell$$

Only differences are physical. The zero is a convention, usually infinity for
bounded distributions, but never for an infinite line or sheet — there the
integral diverges and you must choose a finite reference point.

## Why this is the easier quantity

$V$ is a scalar. Superposition for potential means adding numbers:

$$V = \frac{kQ}{r}, \qquad V = \sum_i \frac{kq_i}{r_i}, \qquad V = \int\frac{k\,dq}{r}$$

No components, no angles, no symmetry argument needed to cancel anything.
The ring on axis, which took real work as a field integral, is immediate:
every element sits at the same distance $\sqrt{z^2+R^2}$, so
$V = kQ/\sqrt{z^2+R^2}$.

That suggests a strategy. When the field is wanted and the integral is ugly,
compute $V$ first and differentiate.

## Getting the field back

Potential loses no information, because the field is its gradient:

$$\vec E = -\nabla V, \qquad E_x = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}$$

The minus sign says $\vec E$ points downhill in $V$, which is why positive
charges accelerate toward low potential. Differentiating the ring's $V$
reproduces $kQz/(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}$ in one step.

Equipotential surfaces are the level sets of $V$, and $\vec E$ is
perpendicular to them everywhere. Where they crowd together the field is
strong. Field lines and equipotentials together are a complete picture.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  $V = 0$ and $\vec E = 0$ are independent. Midway between $+q$ and $-q$ the
  potential vanishes but the field is large; at the center of three equal
  charges the field vanishes but the potential does not. Only the *derivative*
  of $V$ controls the field, so a single value of $V$ tells you nothing.
</Aside>
