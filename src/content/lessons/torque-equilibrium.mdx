## Turning effect

A force applied to a rigid body does two things: it pushes the body, and it
tries to turn it. The turning part is torque, and it depends on where the force
is applied, not just how hard.

$$\tau = rF\sin\theta = r_\perp F$$

The second form is usually faster: drop a perpendicular from the pivot onto the
force's line of action, measure that distance, and multiply. That distance is
the lever arm. A force whose line passes straight through the pivot has zero
lever arm and zero torque, which is why pushing a door near its hinge is futile.

## Two conditions, not one

A body is in static equilibrium when it neither accelerates nor angularly
accelerates. That is two independent statements:

$$\sum \vec{F} = 0, \qquad \sum \vec{\tau} = 0$$

Forces alone are not enough. Two equal opposite forces applied at different
points sum to zero but spin the body — a couple. Both conditions must hold.

In two dimensions this gives three scalar equations: $\sum F_x = 0$,
$\sum F_y = 0$, $\sum \tau = 0$. Three equations means you can solve for at
most three unknowns, which is why a ladder against a wall with friction at both
ends is genuinely indeterminate rather than merely hard.

## Choosing the pivot

For a body in equilibrium, the net torque is zero about *every* point, not just
the real hinge. That freedom is the most useful tool in the whole topic.

Take a uniform plank of weight $W$ resting on two supports, and suppose you
want the force at the right support. Take torques about the left support. The
left force now has zero lever arm and drops out of the equation entirely, and
one line gives you the answer without ever solving a system.

Procedure: draw the free-body diagram, mark every force and where it acts, then
pick as pivot the point where the force you care least about is applied.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Gravity is not applied at the pivot or at the end of the object — for a
  uniform body it acts at the center of mass. Forgetting to include the weight
  as a torque, or placing it at the wrong point, is the single most common error
  in equilibrium problems.
</Aside>
