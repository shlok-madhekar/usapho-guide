## A coil resists change

A current produces flux, and if the current changes the flux changes, and by
Faraday's law that induces an emf in the very coil producing it. Self-inductance
is the proportionality between current and its own flux linkage,
$N\Phi = LI$, which turns Faraday's law into

$$\mathcal{E} = -L\frac{dI}{dt}$$

The minus sign is Lenz's law: the induced emf always opposes the *change*, not
the current itself. A coil carrying a steady current opposes nothing. Try to
increase the current and it pushes back; try to cut the current and it pushes
forward, sometimes hard enough to arc across an opening switch.

For a long solenoid of $n$ turns per length, cross-section $A$ and length
$\ell$, the field $\mu_0 n I$ gives

$$L = \mu_0 n^2 A \ell$$

Inductance, like capacitance, is pure geometry — no current in the formula.

## Current is the continuous variable

Where a capacitor cannot change its voltage discontinuously, an inductor cannot
change its current discontinuously. That single fact answers most LR transient
questions.

**At $t = 0^+$** after a switch closes on a previously dead circuit, the
inductor current is still zero, so it acts like a break. **As $t\to\infty$**,
nothing is changing, $dI/dt = 0$, no emf is induced, and it acts like a plain
wire. In between, an LR circuit relaxes exponentially with time constant

$$\tau = \frac{L}{R}$$

Note that $\tau$ grows with $L$ but *shrinks* with $R$ — the opposite of RC. A
larger resistance forces the current to its final value faster.

Energy stored follows from integrating $P = LI\,dI/dt$:

$$U = \tfrac12 LI^2$$

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  An inductor is not a resistor with a delay, and it never has a well-defined
  "resistance". Do not write $V = IL$ or try to combine an inductor into a
  series-parallel reduction with resistors. Its voltage depends on the slope of
  the current, so the only correct move is to solve the loop equation, or to
  use the two limiting behaviours above and interpolate exponentially.
</Aside>

## LC oscillation

Connect a charged capacitor to an inductor with no resistor. The loop rule
gives $Q/C + L\,d^2Q/dt^2 = 0$, which is the harmonic oscillator equation with

$$\omega = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$$

Energy sloshes between the electric field of the capacitor and the magnetic
field of the inductor, and the total is constant:

$$\frac{Q^2}{2C} + \tfrac12 LI^2 = \text{constant}$$

The mechanical analogy is exact and worth using. Charge plays the role of
position, current the role of velocity, $1/C$ the spring constant, and $L$ the
mass. Every result from the mass-spring problem carries over, including the
quarter-period phase lag between current and charge: the current peaks when the
capacitor is empty, just as a mass moves fastest through equilibrium.
