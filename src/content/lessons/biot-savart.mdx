## A field with no source to point at

In [magnetic force](/guide/magnetic-force) you took the field as given. Someone
handed you a $\vec{B}$, you crossed it with $q\vec{v}$ or with $I\vec{L}$, and
out came a force. Now turn the question around. Where did that field come from?

Hold a compass next to a wire carrying a big current and the needle swings. Not
towards the wire and not away from it — it lines up *around* it, tangent to a
circle whose centre is the wire. That is already strange. An electric charge
makes a field that points away from itself, so the field has an obvious source
sitting at the middle of it. A current makes a field that closes on itself with
nothing in the middle to point at. Whatever rule produces $\vec{B}$ from a
current has to build that sideways-ness in from the start.

The rule is the Biot-Savart law, and it is the magnetic twin of Coulomb's law:
chop the source into tiny pieces, work out what one piece sends to the point
you care about, add the pieces up as vectors. Everything below is that one
sentence, applied to wires bent into different shapes.

## What one piece of current sends out

Take a wire carrying current $I$ and mentally cut out a short length of it.
Call that stub $d\vec{\ell}$: a vector whose length is the length of the stub in
metres and whose direction is the direction the current flows there. The
product $I\,d\vec{\ell}$ is the source — a current element, measured in
ampere-metres.

Pick a field point $P$. Let $\vec{r}$ run from the element to $P$, let $r$ be
its length, and let $\hat{r}$ be the unit vector along it ($\hat{r} =
\vec{r}/r$, length one, direction only). Three things have to be true of the
field $d\vec{B}$ this element makes at $P$. It should grow with the current and
with the size of the stub. It should fall off with distance the way every
point-source field does, as $1/r^2$. And it must come out sideways, because the
compass says so. A cross product is the only clean way to write "sideways to
both". Put those together:

$$
d\vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\vec{\ell}\times\hat{r}}{r^2}
$$

The constant $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ T·m/A is the permeability of free
space; the $4\pi$ is packed into it so that it cancels in the tidiest answers.
The combination $\mu_0/4\pi = 1.0\times10^{-7}$ in SI units is the number you
will actually punch into a calculator, so learn it in that form.

Read the cross product carefully, because it does two separate jobs. Its
*magnitude* is $d\ell\sin\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the
element and the line to $P$. So an element sends nothing at all to a point
straight ahead of itself or straight behind it, and sends the most to a point
off to its side. Its *direction* is given by the right-hand rule: point your
fingers along the current, curl them towards $\hat{r}$, and your thumb gives
$d\vec{B}$. For a point beside a wire that means the field circles the wire.

## The straight wire

Now add elements up. Put a straight wire along a line and a field point $P$ a
perpendicular distance $d$ from it. Every element of the wire lies in the plane
of the page, and $P$ is in that plane too, so every $d\vec{\ell}\times\hat{r}$
points the same way: straight out of the page. That is the piece of luck that
makes this problem doable. The vector sum collapses into an ordinary sum of
magnitudes, and the only question left is how much each element gives.

Measure position along the wire by the angle $\theta$ from the perpendicular
through $P$, positive on one side and negative on the other. Doing the integral
(it is a standard one, and the substitution $x = d\tan\theta$ is what makes it
go) gives, for a segment whose ends sit at angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$,

$$
B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi d}\left(\sin\theta_2 - \sin\theta_1\right)
$$

Memorise this one. Nearly every straight-wire problem in the bank is this
formula used once per segment. Two special cases fall straight out. Let the
wire run forever both ways: $\theta_2 \to +90^\circ$, $\theta_1 \to -90^\circ$,
the bracket becomes $1-(-1) = 2$, and

$$
B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi d}
$$

Let it run in from infinity and stop level with $P$ (a **semi-infinite** wire):
the bracket is $1 - 0 = 1$, and you get exactly half of that, $\mu_0 I/4\pi d$.

**Worked example 1.** A long straight wire carries 5.0 A. Find $B$ at 10 cm.

Straight substitution into the infinite-wire result:

$$
B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi d} = \frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(5.0)}{2\pi(0.10)}
$$

The $\pi$ cancels against the $\pi$ upstairs, leaving $B = (2\times10^{-7})(5.0)/(0.10) = 1.0\times10^{-5}$ T.
Ten microtesla, about a fifth of the Earth's field. That grouping,
$B = 2\times10^{-7}\,I/d$, is worth keeping: it saves you from mishandling the
$\pi$'s under time pressure.

Now the same formula twice. Two long wires 0.30 m apart carry 8.0 A and 2.0 A
in the same direction. Where between them does the field vanish? Between the
wires the two fields point opposite ways, so they can cancel; outside, they
point the same way and cannot. Let $x$ be the distance from the 8.0 A wire.
Setting magnitudes equal,

$$
\frac{\mu_0 (8.0)}{2\pi x} = \frac{\mu_0 (2.0)}{2\pi (0.30 - x)}
$$

Everything common cancels, so $8.0(0.30-x) = 2.0x$, giving $2.4 = 10.0x$ and
$x = 0.24$ m. The null point sits closer to the *weaker* wire, four times
further from the 8 A wire than from the 2 A one, because the ratio of currents
is four. Check that against your intuition before you move on.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Do not add magnitudes when the directions differ. Three long wires through the
  corners of an equilateral triangle, all carrying the same current out of the
  page, produce three equal fields at the centroid, each perpendicular to its own
  radius and each rotated 120 degrees from the next. Three equal vectors at 120
  degrees sum to zero, so the field at the centroid is zero, not three times one
  wire's field. Draw the arrows before you reach for the calculator.
</Aside>

## Arcs, loops and polygons

Bend the wire into a circular arc of radius $R$ and ask for the field at the
centre of curvature. This is the easiest integral in the subject, because every
element is the same distance $R$ away and every element is perpendicular to its
own $\hat{r}$, so $\sin\theta = 1$ throughout and every contribution points the
same way. The magnitudes just add, and the total length of an arc subtending
angle $\phi$ (in radians) is $R\phi$:

$$
B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi R^2}\,(R\phi) = \frac{\mu_0 I \phi}{4\pi R}
$$

A full circle has $\phi = 2\pi$ and gives $B = \mu_0 I/2R$. So an arc gives the
fraction $\phi/2\pi$ of a full loop: a semicircle gives half, a quarter arc
gives a quarter. And straight leads that run *radially* in or out from the
centre contribute nothing at all, since they point along their own $\hat{r}$.
That is why so many problems are drawn with radial leads — it is a way of
handing you the arc alone.

**Worked example 2.** A wire carries 10 A in from far away, runs parallel to
the $x$ axis, curls round a semicircle of radius 5.0 cm, and heads back out
parallel to the $x$ axis on the other side — a hairpin. Find $B$ at the centre.

Three pieces. The semicircle: $\phi = \pi$, so

$$
B_{\text{arc}} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4R} = \frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(10)}{4(0.050)} = 6.28\times10^{-5}\ \text{T}
$$

Each straight lead is semi-infinite and lies a perpendicular distance
$R = 0.050$ m from the centre, so each gives

$$
B_{\text{lead}} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi R} = \frac{(1.0\times10^{-7})(10)}{0.050} = 2.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{T}
$$

All three point the same way (check with the right hand: the current sweeps the
same sense past the centre in all three pieces), so they add:
$6.28\times10^{-5} + 2(2.0\times10^{-5}) = 1.03\times10^{-4}$ T. Had the leads
run radially instead of parallel, the answer would have been the arc alone.

A polygon is the same trick with the finite-segment formula. For a regular
$n$-gon of side $a$, the centre is a distance $d = a/(2\tan(\pi/n))$ from each
side, and each side subtends angles $\pm\pi/n$, so each contributes
$\frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi d}\cdot 2\sin(\pi/n)$, and there are $n$ of them.

**Worked example 3.** A square loop of side 10 cm carries 6.0 A. Find $B$ at
one *corner*.

The trap here is doing the centre calculation by mistake. Sit at the corner and
look at the four sides. Two of them start at your corner and run away from you
along straight lines that pass through you: $\theta = 0$ or $180^\circ$ for
every element, so those two sides give nothing. Only the far two matter, and
each is a distance $a = 0.10$ m away. For one of them, the near end is level
with you ($\theta_1 = 0$) and the far end is at the opposite corner, at
$45^\circ$. So

$$
B_{\text{one}} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi a}\left(\sin 45^\circ - 0\right) = \frac{(1.0\times10^{-7})(6.0)}{0.10}(0.7071) = 4.24\times10^{-6}\ \text{T}
$$

The two far sides carry current in senses that give fields the same way out of
the page, so double it: $B = 8.49\times10^{-6}$ T.

## Off the centre: the loop axis

Step off the centre of a circular loop and onto its axis, a distance $z$ away.
Now the elements are not all equivalent in direction. Each one is still a
distance $\sqrt{R^2+z^2}$ from the field point and still perpendicular to its
$\hat{r}$, so each $|d\vec{B}|$ is the same, but each is tilted: it has a
component along the axis and a component perpendicular to it. Pair up any
element with the one diametrically opposite and the perpendicular components
cancel exactly, while the axial ones add. So only the axial part survives, and
the tilt factor is $R/\sqrt{R^2+z^2}$. Multiply the total magnitude
$\mu_0 I (2\pi R)/4\pi(R^2+z^2)$ by that factor:

$$
B = \frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2\left(R^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}}
$$

Set $z = 0$ and it returns $\mu_0 I/2R$, as it must. Let $z \gg R$ and the
$R^2$ under the root drops out, leaving $B \approx \mu_0 I R^2/2z^3$ — the
$1/z^3$ falloff of a magnetic dipole. For $N$ turns wound close together,
multiply by $N$: every turn does the same job.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Off the axis of a loop there is no elementary formula — that integral is
  elliptic, and no olympiad problem will ask for it. If a question about a loop
  gives you a point that is not on the axis and not the centre, you have
  misread the geometry.
</Aside>

Stack loops and you get a solenoid. Treat a length $dz$ of a solenoid with $n$
turns per metre as a loop carrying current $n I\,dz$, integrate the axial
formula over the length, and the result for a solenoid of length $L$ and radius
$R$, at its midpoint, is

$$
B = \mu_0 n I \frac{L}{\sqrt{L^2 + 4R^2}}
$$

For $L = 0.30$ m, $R = 0.020$ m, $n = 2000$ per metre and $I = 3.0$ A:
$\mu_0 n I = (4\pi\times10^{-7})(2000)(3.0) = 7.54\times10^{-3}$ T, and the
geometric factor is $0.30/\sqrt{0.09+0.0016} = 0.30/0.3027 = 0.991$. So
$B = 7.47\times10^{-3}$ T. The factor being close to one says this solenoid is
long enough to count as infinite, which is what the next lesson will assume.

## Currents that are not wires

A current is charge passing a point per second, and nothing in the Biot-Savart
law cares whether that charge is in a wire. Spin a charged object and you have
a current.

**Worked example 4.** A flat insulating disk of radius $R = 5.0$ cm carries
$q = 2.0\ \mu$C spread evenly over its face and spins at $\omega = 100$ rad/s.
Find $B$ at its centre.

You cannot use the ring answer directly, because different parts of the disk
sit at different radii. So build it from rings. The surface charge density is

$$
\sigma = \frac{q}{\pi R^2} = \frac{2.0\times10^{-6}}{\pi(0.050)^2} = 2.55\times10^{-4}\ \text{C/m}^2
$$

A ring between radius $r$ and $r+dr$ holds charge $dq = \sigma\,2\pi r\,dr$.
That charge goes round once every $2\pi/\omega$ seconds, so the current it
represents is charge over period:

$$
dI = dq\,\frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \sigma\,\omega\,r\,dr
$$

Each ring is a full loop of radius $r$, so it puts $\mu_0\,dI/2r$ at the
centre. The $r$ cancels, which is the small miracle that makes this problem
easy:

$$
dB = \frac{\mu_0 \sigma \omega r\,dr}{2r} = \frac{\mu_0\sigma\omega}{2}\,dr
$$

Every ring contributes equally, so integrating from $0$ to $R$ just multiplies
by $R$:

$$
B = \frac{\mu_0 \sigma \omega R}{2} = \frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(2.55\times10^{-4})(100)(0.050)}{2} = 8.0\times10^{-10}\ \text{T}
$$

A ring is even easier: all the charge is at one radius, so the current is
$I = q f$ with $f$ the revolutions per second, and $B = \mu_0 I/2R$. The same
reasoning handles a sheet of charge sliding sideways, which is where the
surface current density $K$ (amperes per metre of width) comes from; an
infinite sheet gives $B = \mu_0 K/2$ on each side, pointing opposite ways.

## Knowing when to reach for it

The next lesson, [Ampere's law](/guide/amperes-law), gets the same fields far
faster whenever the geometry is symmetric enough — infinite wires, infinite
solenoids, coaxial cables. So the real skill is telling the two apart.

Reach for Biot-Savart when the problem names a **shape**: an arc, a square, a
hexagon, a finite segment, a loop seen from a point on its axis, a pair of
coils. Any time there is a length or a corner or an angle in the statement, the
field will not be constant along any convenient closed loop, and Ampere's law
has nothing to grip.

Then run the same four steps every time. One: decide which pieces contribute
nothing, because a piece pointing along its own line to the field point is
free. Two: get the direction of each surviving piece with your right hand, and
draw the arrows. Three: add what is left — as scalars if the arrows agree, as
vectors if they do not. Four: check the size against a familiar anchor, such as
$\mu_0 I/2R$ for a loop's centre or $2\times10^{-7} I/d$ for a long wire. A
bent-wire answer that comes out ten times either of those is a sign you dropped
a $\pi$ or a factor of two.
