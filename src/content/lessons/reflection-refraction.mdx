Stand at the edge of a swimming pool and look at your own legs. They bend at
the waterline. Drop a coin in and the bottom looks closer than it is. Look up
from underwater and the whole sky is squeezed into a bright circle overhead,
with darkness around it. None of this is your eyes failing. It is light
changing speed.

The last lesson treated light as a wave with a frequency that shifts when the
source moves. Here you mostly get to forget the wave and follow straight lines
called *rays*. A ray is the direction energy travels. Wherever the things light
meets are much bigger than its wavelength — a pool surface, a prism, a
raindrop — rays get you the right answer with geometry instead of wave
mechanics.

## Light slows down inside matter

In vacuum light travels at $c = 3.00\times10^8$ m/s. Inside glass or water it
goes slower, because the atoms absorb and re-radiate it and that takes time.
Define the **refractive index** of a material as the ratio

$$n = \frac{c}{v}$$

where $v$ is the speed of light in that material. So $n$ is always at least 1,
vacuum has $n = 1$ exactly, and air is $n = 1.0003$, near enough to 1 that you
usually write 1. Crown glass has $n = 1.52$, so light inside it travels at
$v = c/n = (3.00\times10^8)/1.52 = 1.97\times10^8$ m/s, about two thirds of
its vacuum speed.

One thing does not change at a boundary: the frequency. The wave on the far
side is driven by the wave on the near side, crest for crest, so they must
arrive at the same rate. Since $v = f\lambda$ and $f$ is fixed while $v$ drops
by a factor $n$, the wavelength must drop by the same factor:

$$\lambda_n = \frac{\lambda_0}{n}$$

Slower speed, shorter waves, same frequency. Hold on to that; it is what makes
refraction happen at all.

## Reflection, and what mirrors do to angles

Light hitting a smooth surface bounces with the angle of reflection equal to
the angle of incidence, both measured **from the normal** — the line
perpendicular to the surface. Angles from the surface itself will wreck every
problem you ever do. Measure from the normal, always.

A flat mirror puts the image as far behind the mirror as the object is in
front. Two consequences show up constantly. If you walk toward a mirror at
$1.20$ m/s, your image walks toward the mirror at $1.20$ m/s from the other
side, so the gap between you and your image closes at $2.40$ m/s. And if you
rotate a mirror by an angle $\alpha$ while the incoming beam is held fixed, the
normal turns by $\alpha$, so the angle of incidence changes by $\alpha$ and the
angle of reflection changes by $\alpha$ as well. The reflected beam turns by
$2\alpha$. Rotate a galvanometer mirror $8.00°$ and the spot swings $16.0°$.

Two mirrors hinged at an angle $\theta$ multiply images: light can bounce off
one, then the other, then back. Counting the distinct images gives

$$N = \frac{360°}{\theta} - 1$$

when $360°/\theta$ is a whole number. Hinge them at $72.0°$ and you get
$360/72 - 1 = 5 - 1 = 4$ images.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Every angle here is measured from the normal. Every one. When a
  problem hands you an angle "from the surface" or "grazing", your first move is
  to convert: an angle $\phi$ above the surface is $90° - \phi$ from the normal.
</Aside>

## Snell's law, from wavefronts

Picture a wide flat wavefront in air heading down onto water at a slant. The
left edge of the front touches the water first and immediately slows down. The
right edge is still in air, still fast. For the short time it takes the right
edge to reach the surface, the left edge has crept forward a smaller distance.
The front pivots. That pivot is refraction.

Make it quantitative. In a time $\Delta t$ the part of the front still in air
covers $v_1\Delta t$ and the part already in water covers $v_2\Delta t$. Both
pieces share the same strip of surface, of length $L$. Geometry of the two
right triangles built on that strip gives $v_1\Delta t = L\sin\theta_1$ and
$v_2 \Delta t = L\sin\theta_2$. Divide one by the other and $L$ and $\Delta t$
both vanish:

$$\frac{\sin\theta_1}{\sin\theta_2} = \frac{v_1}{v_2} = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$$

which is usually written in the symmetric form

$$n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2$$

Read it in words: the quantity $n\sin\theta$ is the same on both sides of the
boundary. Going into a denser medium, $n$ goes up so $\sin\theta$ must come
down and the ray bends *toward* the normal. Coming out into a rarer medium it
bends *away*. At normal incidence, $\theta_1 = 0$, both sides are zero and
nothing bends at all.

### Worked example 1: a laser into a pool, and how deep it looks

A laser in air strikes a pool at $30.0°$ from the normal; water has
$n = 1.33$. Apply Snell's law directly:

$$\sin\theta_2 = \frac{n_1\sin\theta_1}{n_2} = \frac{(1.00)(0.500)}{1.33} = 0.3759$$

so $\theta_2 = \arcsin(0.3759) = 22.1°$. Bent toward the normal, as promised.

Now the harder half. The pool is $2.40$ m deep and you look straight down. Rays
from the bottom leave the water at small angles, so use the small-angle form of
Snell's law: for small $\theta$, $\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta \approx \theta$,
and $n_{\text{w}}\theta_{\text{w}} = \theta_{\text{air}}$. A ray from a point
on the floor at true depth $h$ that crosses the surface a horizontal distance
$x$ from straight-above has $\theta_{\text{w}} \approx x/h$. In air it leaves at
$\theta_{\text{air}} \approx x/h'$, where $h'$ is the depth the backward
extension points to. Putting those together, $x/h' = n_{\text{w}}(x/h)$, and
the $x$ cancels:

$$h' = \frac{h}{n}$$

So the floor appears at $h' = 2.40/1.33 = 1.80$ m. The apparent depth is the
real depth divided by the index. Stack two liquids and you apply this to each
layer separately and add: $4.00$ cm of glycerin under $6.00$ cm of water gives
$4.00/1.47 + 6.00/1.33 = 2.72 + 4.51 = 7.23$ cm.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Light at a boundary does not choose between reflecting and refracting. It does
  both, in proportions that shift with angle. Below the critical angle some
  fraction always reflects, which is why you see both the sky and the fish on a
  pond. There is one special angle, **Brewster's angle**, where the reflected
  light is completely polarised; it happens when the reflected and refracted
  rays are exactly $90°$ apart, which forces $\theta_B + \theta_2 = 90°$ and
  therefore $\tan\theta_B = n_2/n_1$. For glass with $n = 1.52$ that is
  $\arctan(1.52) = 56.7°$.
</Aside>

## When there is nowhere to refract to

Run Snell's law the other way, from dense to rare, with $n_1 > n_2$:

$$\sin\theta_2 = \frac{n_1}{n_2}\sin\theta_1$$

The prefactor is bigger than 1, so $\sin\theta_2 > \sin\theta_1$ and the ray
tips away from the normal. Keep raising $\theta_1$ and at some point the
formula demands $\sin\theta_2 > 1$. No angle has a sine above 1. There is no
refracted ray. All of the energy goes into the reflected one. This is **total
internal reflection**, and the threshold is where $\theta_2$ would be exactly
$90°$:

$$\sin\theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$$

Water to air gives $\sin\theta_c = 1/1.33 = 0.7519$, so $\theta_c = 48.8°$.
Glass to air is about $42°$, which is why a $45°$-$45°$-$90°$ prism turns a
beam through a right angle with no silver coating at all: the internal angle at
the hypotenuse is $45°$, safely past $42°$. Run that backwards and you get the
smallest index a periscope prism can have — you need $\theta_c \le 45°$, so
$n \ge 1/\sin 45° = 1.41$.

Note the direction. TIR only exists going from high index to low. There is no
critical angle on the way into glass.

That bright circle is called **Snell's window**. A lamp at depth $h$ lights a
patch of surface of radius $R = h\tan\theta_c$. At $h = 1.50$ m in water,
$\tan(48.8°) = 1.14$, so $R = 1.71$ m.

### Worked example 2: escaping through a layer of water

A glass block, $n = 1.60$, is covered by water, $n = 1.33$, with air above.
Light inside the glass hits the glass-water boundary at $\theta$. Above what
$\theta$ does none of it reach the air?

You could do two Snell's laws in a row. Do it once and you will see why you
never need to again. At the first boundary $1.60\sin\theta = 1.33\sin\theta_w$.
At the second, $1.33\sin\theta_w = 1.00\sin\theta_a$. The middle term is
identical in both, so it cancels straight out:

$$1.60\sin\theta = 1.00\sin\theta_a$$

The intermediate layer is invisible to the bookkeeping. Whenever your
boundaries are all parallel, **$n\sin\theta$ is a constant all the way
through**, and only the first and last media matter. Light stops escaping when
$\theta_a$ would exceed $90°$:

$$\sin\theta = \frac{1.00}{1.60} = 0.625 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta = 38.7°$$

The water changes nothing. The same invariant runs the mirage over a hot road,
where air near the tarmac is hot and thin, $n = 1.000230$, while air at eye
level is cooler, $n = 1.000280$. There the ray is nearly horizontal, so work
with the angle $\phi$ from the *horizontal*, for which $\sin\theta = \cos\phi$:

$$n_{\text{eye}}\cos\phi = n_{\text{road}}\cos 0° $$
$$\cos\phi = \frac{1.000230}{1.000280} = 0.99995 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \phi = 0.573°$$

A ray leaving your eye barely more than half a degree below horizontal curves
back up. You see sky on the road.

## Prisms: two refractions and a total deviation

A prism has two flat faces meeting at an **apex angle** $A$. Send a ray in at
$\theta_1$, let it refract to $\theta_2$ inside, cross to the second face where
it hits at $\theta_3$ inside, and refract out at $\theta_4$. The geometry of
the triangle formed by the ray and the two normals gives one clean relation:

$$\theta_2 + \theta_3 = A$$

and the total turning of the ray, its **deviation**, is the sum of what each
face did:

$$D = (\theta_1 - \theta_2) + (\theta_4 - \theta_3) = \theta_1 + \theta_4 - A$$

### Worked example 3: through an equilateral prism, and the minimum

Take $A = 60.0°$, $n = 1.52$, and a ray in at $\theta_1 = 40.0°$.

First face: $\sin\theta_2 = \sin 40.0°/1.52 = 0.6428/1.52 = 0.4229$, so
$\theta_2 = 25.0°$.

Inside the glass: $\theta_3 = A - \theta_2 = 60.0° - 25.0° = 35.0°$.

Second face, going glass to air: $\sin\theta_4 = 1.52\sin 35.0° =
1.52 \times 0.5736 = 0.8719$, so $\theta_4 = 60.6°$. That is the answer, and
the deviation is $40.0 + 60.6 - 60.0 = 40.6°$.

Now the thing the figure shows. Sweep $\theta_1$ and $D$ falls, bottoms out,
and climbs. At the bottom the path is symmetric, $\theta_1 = \theta_4$ and
$\theta_2 = \theta_3 = A/2$. Call the deviation there $D_m$. Then
$\theta_1 = (A + D_m)/2$, and Snell at the first face reads

$$n = \frac{\sin\!\big((A + D_m)/2\big)}{\sin(A/2)}$$

For $A = 60.0°$ and $n = 1.50$: $\sin((60 + D_m)/2) = 1.50\sin 30.0° = 0.750$,
so $(60 + D_m)/2 = 48.6°$ and $D_m = 37.2°$. Run it backwards to measure an
index: a measured $D_m = 40.0°$ gives
$n = \sin 50.0°/\sin 30.0° = 0.766/0.500 = 1.53$.

Two more things fall out of the prism relations. Because $\theta_3 = A -
\theta_2$ and $\theta_2$ can never exceed $\arcsin(1/n)$, a large enough apex
angle traps every ray: light emerges only if $A \le 2\arcsin(1/n)$. And
because $n$ is slightly larger for violet light than for red — **dispersion** —
each colour gets its own deviation, which is what spreads white light into a
band.

## Curved surfaces, and why rainbows sit at 42 degrees

Refraction at a *curved* surface is still just Snell's law, but with a normal
that points along the radius. For paraxial rays — rays close to the axis and
nearly parallel to it — the algebra collapses to

$$\frac{n_1}{s} + \frac{n_2}{s'} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R}$$

with $s$ the object distance, $s'$ the image distance, and $R$ positive when
the surface bulges toward the incoming light. Try it on a solid glass sphere of
radius $2.00$ cm, $n = 1.50$, lit from infinity. Front surface: $s = \infty$
kills the first term, so $1.50/s' = 0.50/2.00 = 0.250$ and $s' = 6.00$ cm past
the front. That is $6.00 - 4.00 = 2.00$ cm past the *back*, so for the back
surface the light is already converging and the object distance is $-2.00$ cm,
with $R = -2.00$ cm as the surface now curves away:

$$\frac{1.50}{-2.00} + \frac{1.00}{s'} = \frac{1.00 - 1.50}{-2.00} = 0.250$$

$$\frac{1.00}{s'} = 0.250 + 0.750 = 1.00 \quad\Rightarrow\quad s' = 1.00 \text{ cm}$$

The sphere focuses one centimetre beyond its own back surface.

The rainbow needs no such approximation, only careful angle counting. A ray
enters a spherical drop at incidence $\theta_1$, refracts to $\theta_2$,
reflects once off the back, and refracts out. Each refraction turns it by
$\theta_1 - \theta_2$ and the internal reflection turns it by $180° -
2\theta_2$. Total:

$$D = 2(\theta_1 - \theta_2) + 180° - 2\theta_2 = 180° + 2\theta_1 - 4\theta_2$$

Rays arrive across the whole face of the drop, so every $\theta_1$ is
represented. Light piles up where $D$ barely changes as $\theta_1$ varies —
that is why there is a bright arc rather than a smear. Set $dD/d\theta_1 = 0$.
With $\sin\theta_1 = n\sin\theta_2$, differentiating gives
$\cos\theta_1 = n\cos\theta_2 \, d\theta_2/d\theta_1$, and the stationary
condition $d\theta_2/d\theta_1 = 1/2$ turns into

$$\cos\theta_1 = \sqrt{\frac{n^2 - 1}{3}}$$

For water, $n = 1.333$: $\cos\theta_1 = \sqrt{(1.777 - 1)/3} = \sqrt{0.2590} =
0.5089$, so $\theta_1 = 59.4°$. Then $\sin\theta_2 = 0.8609/1.333 = 0.6458$ and
$\theta_2 = 40.2°$. Put them in:

$$D = 180° + 2(59.4°) - 4(40.2°) = 180° + 118.8° - 160.8° = 138.0°$$

The light comes back $138°$ from its original direction, which is $180° - 138°
= 42.0°$ away from the direction pointing straight back at the sun. That is the
radius of the rainbow. The secondary bow is the same calculation with two
internal reflections, $D = 360° + 2\theta_1 - 6\theta_2$, and lands at $50.9°$.

## Recognising which tool the problem wants

- **The word "angle" plus two materials.** Write $n_1\sin\theta_1 =
  n_2\sin\theta_2$ and check that you measured from the normal.
- **Stacked parallel layers.** Do not chain Snell's law layer by layer. Use
  $n\sin\theta$ constant and ignore everything in the middle.
- **"Above what angle", "just barely escapes", "is guided", "trapped", a
  fiber, a periscope prism.** That is a critical angle: $\sin\theta_c = n_2/n_1$
  with $n_1$ the medium the light is *in*.
- **"Looks like", "appears to be", straight down into water.** Apparent depth,
  $h' = h/n$, one layer at a time.
- **Two flat faces at an angle.** Prism relations: $\theta_2 + \theta_3 = A$
  and $D = \theta_1 + \theta_4 - A$. If the word "minimum" appears, jump
  straight to the symmetric path.
- **Two indices quoted for one piece of glass.** Dispersion: run the whole
  calculation twice and subtract the two answers.
- **"Brightest", "stationary", "the rainbow angle".** Write the deviation as a
  function of the entry angle and set its derivative to zero.
- **A mirror that moves or turns.** Angles double. A rotation $\alpha$ swings
  the beam $2\alpha$; approaching at $v$ closes the gap at $2v$.

Every one of these is the same law used with care about which angle is which.
Draw the normal before you draw the ray, and most of the difficulty goes away.
