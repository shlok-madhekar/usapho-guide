Shine a laser through a single human hair and put a screen behind it. You do not
get a shadow of the hair. You get a row of bright dots spread over half a metre,
with dark gaps between them. Something that should block light has painted a
pattern across the wall instead.

In the last lesson you traced light as rays, and the image was where the rays
crossed. That worked because the lens was enormous compared with a wavelength.
Once the obstacle gets small — a hair, a slit, a telescope aperture — rays stop
being the right description and you have to use the fact that light is a wave.
What follows is one idea used over and over: light arriving at a point by two
routes adds or cancels, depending on how far out of step the routes are.

## Two routes to the same point

Take a wall with two narrow slits in it, a distance $d$ apart, and a screen a
distance $L$ behind. One wave arrives from the left, so both slits start their
journeys in step. Two sources holding a fixed phase relationship like this are
called *coherent*; without coherence there is no pattern, just a smear.

Pick a point $P$ on the screen at angle $\theta$ off the centre line. The far
slit's journey to $P$ is longer. If $L$ is much bigger than $d$ — in any real
setup $L$ is metres and $d$ is microns — the two rays to $P$ are essentially
parallel, both tilted at $\theta$. Drop a perpendicular from the near slit onto
the far slit's ray and you cut off a right triangle with hypotenuse $d$ and
angle $\theta$. The extra distance is its short leg:

$$\Delta = d\sin\theta$$

That $\Delta$ is the *path difference*, and it controls everything. A wave that
travels one extra wavelength arrives back exactly in step with itself. So if
$\Delta$ is any whole number of wavelengths, the two waves arrive crest on
crest and add. Bright fringe:

$$d\sin\theta = m\lambda, \qquad m = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \ldots$$

The integer $m$ is the *order*: $m=0$ is the fringe straight ahead, where the
two routes are the same length, $m=1$ is the first one out on either side. If
instead $\Delta$ is half a wavelength, or one and a half, the crest of one wave
lands on the trough of the other and they cancel exactly. Dark fringe:
$d\sin\theta = (m + \tfrac12)\lambda$.

Angles in these problems are usually tiny. With $d = 0.1$ mm and
$\lambda = 550$ nm, $\sin\theta_1 = 0.0055$, so $\theta_1$ is about a third of
a degree. That lets you use $\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta \approx y/L$, where
$y$ is the distance up the screen from the centre. Substituting,
$dy/L = m\lambda$, so the $m$th fringe sits at $y_m = m\lambda L/d$ and
consecutive fringes are separated by

$$\Delta y = \frac{\lambda L}{d}$$

Read that formula. Longer wavelength, wider fringes. Bigger screen distance,
wider fringes. Bigger slit separation, *narrower* fringes — pattern and object
are inverse to each other, which comes back with gratings and telescopes.

**Example 1.** Two slits $0.100$ mm apart, $550$ nm light, screen $2.00$ m
away. Fringe spacing?

Put everything in metres: $d = 1.00\times10^{-4}$ m,
$\lambda = 5.50\times10^{-7}$ m, $L = 2.00$ m.

$$
\Delta y = \frac{(5.50\times10^{-7})(2.00)}{1.00\times10^{-4}}
 = \frac{1.10\times10^{-6}}{1.00\times10^{-4}} = 1.10\times10^{-2}\ \text{m}
$$

That is $11.0$ mm. The pattern magnifies the wavelength by the ratio $L/d$,
which is 20 000 here.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Only use $\Delta y = \lambda L/d$ when the angles are small. If a problem
  gives you $d$ only a few wavelengths wide, $\sin\theta$ is not small and you
  must go back to $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ and take a real inverse sine.
</Aside>

## How bright is the space between fringes

Problems also ask for the intensity partway between fringes. Each slit sends a
wave of amplitude $E_0$, and at angle $\theta$ the second arrives with a phase
lag $\phi$. One wavelength of path difference is one full cycle of $2\pi$, so

$$\phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\,d\sin\theta$$

Add two equal waves that are $\phi$ apart. Drawn as arrows tip to tail, they
form an isosceles triangle, and the resultant has length
$2E_0\cos(\phi/2)$. Intensity goes as amplitude squared, so

$$I = I_{\max}\cos^2\!\left(\frac{\phi}{2}\right)$$

where $I_{\max} = 4E_0^2$ is the value at the centre. Note $4$, not $2$: two
coherent beams at a maximum give four times one beam's intensity, not twice.
Energy is not being created, it has been moved out of the dark fringes.

**Example 2.** At a point on the screen the two paths differ by $\lambda/3$.
What fraction of the central intensity is there?

A path difference of $\lambda/3$ is a phase of
$\phi = 2\pi/3$. Then $\phi/2 = \pi/3$, and $\cos(\pi/3) = 0.500$. So

$$\frac{I}{I_{\max}} = (0.500)^2 = 0.250$$

A quarter. Two thirds of the way to a dark fringe in path already costs three
quarters of the brightness, because $\cos^2$ falls fastest in the middle.

The same picture handles unequal slits. If
one transmits amplitude $E_0$ and the other $\tfrac12 E_0$, the arrows no
longer cancel when they oppose. At a maximum the amplitudes add to $1.5E_0$; at
a minimum they subtract to $0.5E_0$. The intensity ratio is

$$\frac{I_{\max}}{I_{\min}} = \left(\frac{1.5}{0.5}\right)^2 = 9.0$$

Unequal slits never give true darkness. The fringes lose contrast but stay in
the same places.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Adding intensities instead of amplitudes. Coherent beams add as arrows first
  and get squared at the end; only incoherent beams (two separate lamps) add
  as intensities. If you ever find the minimum of a two-source pattern by doing
  $I_1 - I_2$ you will get the wrong answer whenever the slits are unequal.
</Aside>

## One slit is also two slits, infinitely many times

Now close one slit and widen the other to a width $a$. You still get a pattern:
the surprise the hair demonstrated. A slit of finite width is not one source but
a continuous strip of them, every point across the opening sending light onward.
That is *Huygens' principle*, and it is why light spreads into the shadow at all.

There is a trick that gets the dark fringes without calculus. Split the slit
down the middle into a top half
and a bottom half, each of width $a/2$. Pair the very top point with the point
$a/2$ below it, the next point down with the point $a/2$ below *that*, and so
on: every point in the top half has a partner in the bottom half, exactly
$a/2$ away. Each pair has a path difference of $(a/2)\sin\theta$. If that
equals $\lambda/2$, every single pair cancels, so the whole slit cancels. That
condition is $a\sin\theta = \lambda$. Splitting into four strips instead of two
gives $a\sin\theta = 2\lambda$, and so on:

$$a\sin\theta = m\lambda, \qquad m = \pm1, \pm2, \ldots$$

That equation is a trap. It has the same shape as the double-slit formula but
locates the *dark* fringes, and $m=0$ is excluded: at $\theta = 0$ every route
is the same length and everything adds, giving a broad central bright band
running from $-\lambda/a$ to $+\lambda/a$ in $\sin\theta$, flanked by much
fainter side lobes. Its half-width $\lambda/a$ again has the obstacle
downstairs: narrow the slit and the light fans out further.

**Example 3.** A $633$ nm laser lights a single slit; on a screen $2.50$ m
away the central band is $8.00$ mm wide, edge to edge. Find the slit width.

Edge to edge means from the first minimum on one side to the first on the
other, so the half-width is $y_1 = 4.00$ mm $= 4.00\times10^{-3}$ m. For the
first minimum, $m = 1$ and $\sin\theta \approx y_1/L$:

$$
a\,\frac{y_1}{L} = \lambda
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
a = \frac{\lambda L}{y_1}
= \frac{(6.33\times10^{-7})(2.50)}{4.00\times10^{-3}}
$$

$$
a = \frac{1.5825\times10^{-6}}{4.00\times10^{-3}} = 3.96\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}
= 0.396\ \text{mm}
$$

Notice $a = \lambda L / y_1$ is the same algebra as $d = \lambda L/\Delta y$.
Always check which distance the problem handed you, full width or half width,
before dividing: that factor of two is where most of the lost marks go.

## Fringes inside an envelope

Real double slits have width. Both effects happen at once, and they simply
multiply: the fine interference fringes from the separation $d$, modulated by
the broad diffraction envelope from the width $a$.

$$
I = I_0\cos^2\!\left(\frac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}\right)
\left[\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}\right]^2,
\qquad \alpha = \frac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda}
$$

The first factor you derived above. The second is what the full sum over the
slit gives, and its zeros are exactly the $a\sin\theta = m\lambda$ you found by
pairing. You rarely need it in full, but you do need what it does to the count.

Since $d > a$ always, fringes are packed closer than envelope zeros. Fringe $m$
sits at $\sin\theta = m\lambda/d$ and envelope zero $m'$ at
$\sin\theta = m'\lambda/a$, so they coincide when $m = m'(d/a)$. If $d/a$ is a
whole number those fringes land on a zero and are erased: the *missing orders*.
With $d = 4a$, orders $4, 8, 12, \ldots$ vanish.

**Example 4.** Two slits, each $0.0500$ mm wide, separated by $0.250$ mm,
lit at $550$ nm. How many interference maxima lie strictly inside the central
diffraction envelope?

Ratio first: $d/a = 0.250/0.0500 = 5.00$. The central envelope runs out to the
first zero at $\sin\theta = \lambda/a$, and the interference order there is
$m = d/a = 5$. So order $5$ is exactly on the envelope zero and is missing;
the orders strictly inside are $m = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \pm3, \pm4$.

Count them: one central, four on each side, so $1 + 8 = 9$ maxima.

The general rule: the number of fringes inside the central envelope is
$2\lceil d/a\rceil - 1$, and when $d/a$ is an exact integer the fringes at
$\pm d/a$ are missing rather than merely at the edge.

## Many slits, and why gratings are instruments

Go from two slits to $N$ equally spaced ones. The maximum condition does not
change: if adjacent slits are in step, all $N$ are, so the bright directions are
still $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$. What changes is sharpness.

With two slits, stepping off a maximum puts the pair a little out of phase and
the brightness drops slowly. With $N$ slits the same small mismatch $\delta$
between neighbours accumulates: slit $N$ is $(N-1)\delta$ behind slit 1. Draw
the $N$ arrows tip to tail, each turned by $\delta$ from the last, and they curl
into an arc. When the total turn $N\delta$ reaches $2\pi$ the arc closes into a
circle and the resultant is zero. The first dark point beside a maximum is only
$\delta = 2\pi/N$ away in phase instead of $\pi$, so peaks narrow as $1/N$ while
staying put.

Narrow peaks let you tell two close wavelengths apart. The *chromatic resolving
power* of a grating with $N$ illuminated lines used in order $m$ is

$$\frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} = mN$$

where $\Delta\lambda$ is the smallest wavelength difference you can separate.

The other grating number is the *angular dispersion*, how far apart in angle two
wavelengths emerge. Differentiate $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ with $m$ and $d$
fixed: $d\cos\theta\,d\theta = m\,d\lambda$, so

$$\frac{d\theta}{d\lambda} = \frac{m}{d\cos\theta}$$

Higher order spreads the spectrum more, and so does a finer grating.

**Example 5.** A grating with $600$ lines/mm is used in second order near
$500$ nm. Find the angular dispersion in rad/nm.

The spacing is one over the line density:
$d = 1/(600\ \text{mm}^{-1}) = 1.667\times10^{-3}$ mm
$= 1.667\times10^{-6}$ m.

Find the angle first, since $\cos\theta$ is in the answer. With $m = 2$:

$$
\sin\theta = \frac{m\lambda}{d} = \frac{2(5.00\times10^{-7})}{1.667\times10^{-6}}
= 0.600 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta = 36.9^\circ,\ \cos\theta = 0.800
$$

Now substitute:

$$
\frac{d\theta}{d\lambda} = \frac{2}{(1.667\times10^{-6})(0.800)}
= \frac{2}{1.333\times10^{-6}} = 1.50\times10^{6}\ \text{rad/m}
$$

Convert to per nanometre by multiplying by $10^{-9}$ m/nm:
$1.50\times10^{-3}$ rad/nm. So the sodium doublet, $0.59$ nm apart, comes out
about $0.9$ mrad apart here.

Two more things gratings do that show up constantly. The **highest visible
order** is set by $\sin\theta \le 1$: from $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ you need
$m \le d/\lambda$, then round down. And **orders overlap**, because
$m\lambda$ is what fixes the angle, not $\lambda$: third order of $400$ nm and
second order of $600$ nm both give $m\lambda = 1200$ nm and land on top of each
other.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Whenever a problem tells you the grating is tilted, or lit at an angle
  $\theta_i$, the path difference between neighbouring slits picks up a second
  term from the incoming side. The condition becomes
  $d(\sin\theta_m - \sin\theta_i) = m\lambda$ with both angles measured from
  the normal on the same side. Every other grating result follows from this one
  by setting $\theta_i = 0$.
</Aside>

## Apertures, telescopes and the Rayleigh criterion

A circular hole diffracts too. The pairing trick does not work cleanly on a
disc, so the maths is messier, but the result has the same shape as a slit's
with an extra numerical factor. The first dark ring sits at

$$\sin\theta \approx \theta = 1.22\,\frac{\lambda}{D}$$

for an aperture of diameter $D$. That central blob is called the Airy disc, and
it is the smallest image a perfect lens can make of a point.

Two stars closer together than this merge into one blur. The *Rayleigh
criterion* says they are just resolved when the centre of one Airy disc falls on
the first dark ring of the other, i.e. when their angular separation equals
$1.22\lambda/D$. Bigger telescopes see finer detail for this reason alone,
nothing to do with collecting more light.

**Example 6.** A binary star has an angular separation of $0.400$ arcsec. What
aperture resolves it at $550$ nm? Use $1$ rad $= 206265$ arcsec.

Convert the angle:

$$\theta = \frac{0.400}{206265} = 1.939\times10^{-6}\ \text{rad}$$

Set that equal to the criterion and solve for $D$:

$$
D = \frac{1.22\lambda}{\theta} = \frac{1.22(5.50\times10^{-7})}{1.939\times10^{-6}}
= \frac{6.71\times10^{-7}}{1.939\times10^{-6}} = 0.346\ \text{m}
$$

A 35 cm telescope. The same formula on a $3.00$ mm eye pupil at $25$ cm gives
the finest detail your eye can pick out; on Hubble's $2.40$ m mirror it gives
$2.8\times10^{-7}$ rad.

## Two setups that are secretly a double slit

Several classic problems are double slits in disguise, and spotting the
disguise is the whole solution.

**Lloyd's mirror.** A point source sits a height $h$ above a flat mirror. Light
reaches the screen directly, and also by bouncing off the mirror. The reflected
light appears to come from an image source a distance $h$ *below* the mirror,
so you have two sources separated by $d = 2h$ and every double-slit formula
applies with that $d$. One catch: reflection off a denser medium flips the
phase by $\pi$, so the bright and dark fringes swap, and the fringe *spacing*
$\lambda L/(2h)$ is unaffected. With $h = 1.00$ mm, $\lambda = 600$ nm and
$L = 2.00$ m, the spacing is
$(6.00\times10^{-7})(2.00)/(2.00\times10^{-3}) = 6.0\times10^{-4}$ m, i.e.
$0.6$ mm.

**A slab over one slit.** Put a transparent sheet of thickness $t$ and index
$n$ over one slit. Inside it the wavelength is $\lambda/n$, so the sheet fits
$n$ times as many cycles as the same thickness of vacuum. The extra path, in
vacuum terms, is $(n-1)t$; divide by $\lambda$ for the fringe shift:

$$\text{shift} = \frac{(n-1)t}{\lambda}$$

With $t = 10.0\ \mu$m, $n = 1.58$ and $\lambda = 550$ nm:
$(0.58)(1.00\times10^{-5})/(5.50\times10^{-7}) = 10.5$ fringes.

The same counting logic drives the Michelson interferometer. Moving one mirror
by $x$ changes that arm's path by $2x$, because the light goes there and back,
so the number of fringes that sweep past is $N = 2x/\lambda$. Replacing gas of
index $n$ in a cell of length $\ell$ with vacuum changes the path by
$2(n-1)\ell$, giving $N = 2(n-1)\ell/\lambda$. Every one of these is the same
sentence: count how many extra wavelengths one route gained.

## Recognising which tool a problem wants

The formulas are few; picking the right one is the skill. Work down this list.

**What length is the problem handing you?** A separation between two openings
means interference and maxima at $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$. A width of one
opening means diffraction and minima at $a\sin\theta = m\lambda$. A diameter of
a round aperture means $1.22\lambda/D$. If both a width and a separation
appear, you need the envelope picture and probably a missing-order or
fringe-count question.

**Is it asking where, or how bright?** Where means the $m\lambda$ conditions.
How bright means going back to the phase, $\phi = 2\pi\Delta/\lambda$, and
squaring an amplitude sum.

**Does the word "resolve" or "just distinguish" appear?** That is Rayleigh for
apertures, $\lambda/\Delta\lambda = mN$ for gratings. Both are about telling
two nearby things apart, and both get better as the instrument gets bigger.

**Is there a reflection, a mirror image, or a second path that is not a slit?**
Then it is a disguised double slit: find the effective separation between the
real source and its image, and use $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ with that.

**Does anything sit in the beam, or is the whole setup in a medium?** Then
count optical path, $n$ times the geometric length, and convert the difference
into fringes by dividing by $\lambda$.

Finally, sanity-check every angle. Visible wavelengths are half a micron. If a
problem gives you slits tens of microns apart, the first order is a couple of
degrees and the small-angle form is safe. If it gives you a grating with
$d$ only two or three wavelengths wide, expect angles of tens of degrees, keep
the inverse sine, and check whether the order you were asked for even exists.
