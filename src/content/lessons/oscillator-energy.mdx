## The question a stopwatch cannot answer

You have spent the last lesson on [pendulums](/guide/pendulums), finding the
period of a swing and writing the angle as a cosine in time. That machinery
answers "where is it at 2.4 seconds?" But most oscillator problems never
mention time at all. They ask how fast the bob is moving at the bottom, or how
far a block gets before it stops, or what happens to the swing when you drop a
lump of putty on it. Solving those with $x(t) = A\cos\omega t$ means finding
the phase, differentiating, and hoping the algebra closes.

There is a shortcut, and it is the single most useful tool in this unit. An
oscillator has a fixed budget of energy. It spends that budget on stretching
the spring and on moving the mass, and the split between the two changes
constantly while the total sits still. Track the budget instead of the clock
and most of these questions collapse to one line of algebra.

## A constant total, traded back and forth

Take a block of mass $m$ on a horizontal spring of stiffness $k$, and let $x$
be the displacement from the relaxed position. Two things hold energy here.
The spring holds potential energy $U = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$: that is the work you
do stretching it, since the force $kx$ grows from zero and averages $\tfrac12
kx$ over a pull of length $x$. The block holds kinetic energy $K =
\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$.

Nothing else takes a cut. Gravity and the normal force are both perpendicular
to the motion, so neither does work. With no friction, the sum is conserved:

$$E = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = \text{constant}$$

That constant is worth naming in a useful way. Evaluate it at the turning
point, where the block is momentarily at rest at $x = A$, the amplitude. There
$v = 0$, so every joule sits in the spring:

$$E = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2$$

Now evaluate the same constant at the centre, where $x = 0$ and the spring
holds nothing. Every joule is kinetic, and the block is moving at its fastest:

$$
\tfrac{1}{2}mv_{\max}^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_{\max} = A\sqrt{k/m} = \omega A
$$

The $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ is the same angular frequency from the last two
lessons, which is a decent sign the bookkeeping is right. Notice what the
energy statement does not contain: $t$. It connects a position to a speed
directly, which is exactly the connection those problems ask for.

## Reading a speed off the energy line

Set the total at a general position equal to the total at the turning point:

$$\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2$$

Multiply through by $2/m$ and use $\omega^2 = k/m$:

$$
v^2 = \frac{k}{m}\left(A^2 - x^2\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = \omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}
$$

Read that as a sentence. The speed is largest at $x=0$, falls to zero at $x =
\pm A$, and depends on $x$ only through $x^2$, so the block moves equally fast
on the way out and on the way back. The mass never appears except inside
$\omega$.

Two ratio facts follow immediately, and problems love them because they need no
numbers at all. The potential share is
$U/E = (\tfrac12 kx^2)/(\tfrac12 kA^2) = x^2/A^2$, so the kinetic share is
$K/E = 1 - x^2/A^2$. At $x = 0.60A$ the kinetic fraction is
$1 - 0.36 = 0.64$. And $K = U$ means each holds half, so
$\tfrac12 kx^2 = \tfrac12\left(\tfrac12 kA^2\right)$, giving $x^2 = A^2/2$ and

$$x = \frac{A}{\sqrt 2} \approx 0.707A$$

and watch the two pieces come out equal.</>}>

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  The total goes as the square of the amplitude, $E = \tfrac12 kA^2$. Doubling
  the swing costs four times the energy. Run it backwards and a system that has
  lost 36% of its energy has lost only 20% of its amplitude, because
  $\sqrt{0.64} = 0.8$. Amplitude ratios and energy ratios are never the same
  number.
</Aside>

**Example 1.** A 0.25 kg puck on a spring of stiffness 100 N/m swings with
amplitude 8.0 cm. How fast is it moving 4.0 cm from equilibrium?

Start with $\omega$, because $v = \omega\sqrt{A^2-x^2}$ needs it:
$\omega = \sqrt{100/0.25} = \sqrt{400} = 20$ rad/s. Then
$A^2 - x^2 = (0.080)^2 - (0.040)^2 = 0.0064 - 0.0016 = 0.0048$ m$^2$, and
$\sqrt{0.0048} = 0.0693$ m. So $v = 20 \times 0.0693 = 1.39$ m/s.

Check it against the maximum, $v_{\max} = \omega A = 20 \times 0.080 = 1.6$
m/s. Halfway out in position, the puck is still at 87% of top speed. That is
the parabola doing its work: near the centre, the spring holds almost nothing.

## Vertical springs and pendulums store it somewhere else

Hang the same block from the spring and gravity now does work as it moves. The
fix is short. Let $y$ be the displacement measured from the *hanging*
equilibrium, where the stretch is $y_0 = mg/k$. Add the spring and gravity
terms and expand:

$$
U = \tfrac{1}{2}k(y_0+y)^2 - mg\,(y_0 + y) = \tfrac{1}{2}ky^2 + \left(\tfrac{1}{2}ky_0^2 - mg\,y_0\right)
$$

The cross terms cancel because $ky_0 = mg$, and what is left is
$\tfrac12 ky^2$ plus a constant that never changes. So a vertical spring
behaves exactly like a horizontal one, provided you measure from the new
equilibrium and forget gravity entirely. A 1.5 kg mass on a 45 N/m spring
pulled 8.0 cm below its hanging position reaches
$v_{\max} = 0.080\sqrt{45/1.5} = 0.080 \times 5.477 = 0.438$ m/s, with $g$
appearing nowhere.

A pendulum keeps its potential energy in height instead. A bob on a string of
length $L$ at angle $\theta$ from vertical sits a height
$h = L(1-\cos\theta)$ above the bottom of its swing, so $U = mgL(1-\cos\theta)$.
Released from rest at $\theta_0$, energy conservation to the lowest point gives
$\tfrac12 mv^2 = mgL(1-\cos\theta_0)$, so

$$v = \sqrt{2gL\,(1-\cos\theta_0)}$$

This one is exact at any angle, small or not. For a 1.20 m string released at
$15^\circ$: $1-\cos 15^\circ = 1 - 0.9659 = 0.0341$, so
$v = \sqrt{2(9.8)(1.20)(0.0341)} = \sqrt{0.8014} = 0.895$ m/s.

**Example 2.** A bob on a 1.00 m string is released from rest at $60^\circ$
from vertical. At the lowest point, what is the ratio of string tension to
weight?

Energy alone will not answer this, because tension does no work and so never
appears in the energy equation. It gives you the speed; Newton's second law
turns the speed into a force. Do them in that order.

Speed first: $v^2 = 2gL(1-\cos 60^\circ) = 2gL(1-0.5) = gL$.

Now the bottom of the swing. The bob moves on a circle of radius $L$, so its
acceleration there points straight up, towards the pivot, with magnitude
$v^2/L$. Two vertical forces act: tension $T$ up and weight $mg$ down.

$$T - mg = \frac{mv^2}{L} = \frac{m(gL)}{L} = mg$$

So $T = 2mg$ and the ratio is exactly 2. A cleaner way to hold it: at the
bottom, $T/mg = 3 - 2\cos\theta_0$, which you can get by leaving $\theta_0$
general. At $60^\circ$ that is $3-1 = 2$.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Tension and the normal force never enter an energy equation, because they
  point perpendicular to the motion and do no work. Every problem that asks for
  a force at some point in an oscillation is a two-step problem: energy to get
  $v$, then $\sum F = ma$ with $a = v^2/r$ at that point. Writing
  $T = mg + \tfrac12 mv^2/L$, or setting $T = mg$ at the bottom, are the two
  standard ways to lose the question.
</Aside>

## Averages over a cycle

Put $x = A\cos\omega t$ into both energies, with
$v = -A\omega\sin\omega t$ and $m\omega^2 = k$:

$$
U = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2\cos^2\omega t, \qquad K = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2\sin^2\omega t
$$

Over a full cycle the average of $\cos^2$ and of $\sin^2$ is $\tfrac12$ each,
since the two averages must be equal and must add to 1. So

$$\langle U\rangle = \langle K\rangle = \tfrac{1}{4}kA^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}E$$

On average, the energy splits down the middle. For a 0.40 kg mass on a 90 N/m
spring with $A = 5.0$ cm, the mean kinetic energy is
$\tfrac14 (90)(0.050)^2 = \tfrac14 (90)(0.0025) = 0.0563$ J.

Both $\cos^2$ and $\sin^2$ run through two full periods for every one period of
the motion, so the energies slosh at $2\omega$ while the block oscillates at
$\omega$. That factor of two is behind a lot of otherwise mysterious answers.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Equal average energies do not mean the oscillator spends half its time fast.
  It crawls near the turning points and flies through the centre, so it is out
  near the extremes for more of each cycle than a glance at the averages
  suggests.
</Aside>

## When the mass changes mid-oscillation

A popular problem type drops a lump of putty on a moving block, or lifts a
rider off it, and asks for the new amplitude. Energy is not conserved in a
sticking collision, so you cannot start there. What you can do is ask, at the
instant of the change, which of $x$ and $v$ survives it — then rebuild the
energy from whichever survived.

At the **centre**, the block is moving and $x = 0$. A vertical drop of putty
adds no horizontal momentum, so horizontal momentum is conserved:
$mv = (m+M)v'$. The new state is "speed $v'$ at $x=0$", so the whole new energy
is kinetic and $\tfrac12(m+M)v'^2 = \tfrac12 kA'^2$.

At the **turning point**, $v=0$ and the spring is stretched by $A$. Adding or
removing mass there changes no stored energy at all, so the amplitude is
unchanged. Only the speed later on changes, through the new $\omega$.

**Example 3.** A 0.60 kg block oscillates on a $k = 240$ N/m spring with
amplitude 10.0 cm. A 0.30 kg lump of putty is dropped onto it and sticks. Find
the new amplitude if the drop happens at the centre, and the new maximum speed
if it happens at the turning point instead.

*Dropped at the centre.* The speed there before the drop is
$v = \omega A = \sqrt{240/0.60}\times 0.100 = \sqrt{400}\times 0.100 = 20
\times 0.100 = 2.00$ m/s. Momentum: $(0.60)(2.00) = (0.90)v'$, so
$v' = 1.20/0.90 = 1.333$ m/s. That 0.9 kg moving at 1.333 m/s carries
$\tfrac12(0.90)(1.333)^2 = 0.45 \times 1.778 = 0.800$ J. Setting
$\tfrac12(240)A'^2 = 0.800$ gives $A'^2 = 1.600/240 = 0.006667$ m$^2$ and
$A' = 0.0817$ m. The amplitude shrank, because the collision threw away energy.

*Dropped at the turning point.* Nothing is moving, so no energy is lost and no
momentum argument is needed. The stored energy is still
$\tfrac12(240)(0.100)^2 = 1.20$ J, and the amplitude is still 10.0 cm. But
that energy now has to accelerate 0.90 kg:
$\tfrac12(0.90)v_{\max}^2 = 1.20$, so $v_{\max}^2 = 2.40/0.90 = 2.667$ and
$v_{\max} = 1.63$ m/s. Compare with 2.00 m/s before: heavier means slower for
the same stored energy.

## When energy leaks away

Real oscillators run down. Two kinds of loss show up constantly, and they
behave completely differently.

**Sliding friction** takes a fixed amount per metre travelled. A kinetic
friction force $f = \mu m g$ opposing the motion removes $f\,d$ of energy over
a path length $d$, no matter how fast the block is going. That single sentence
solves a whole family of problems.

Two consequences worth deriving. First, the block stops for good once its total
energy has drained, so the total path length is
$d_{\text{tot}} = E_0/(\mu m g)$ up to the small energy still stored where it
finally stalls. Second, over one half-cycle the block travels from amplitude
$A_n$ to amplitude $A_{n+1}$, a distance $A_n + A_{n+1}$, so

$$\tfrac12 kA_n^2 - \tfrac12 kA_{n+1}^2 = \mu m g\,(A_n + A_{n+1})$$

The difference of squares factors, the common $(A_n + A_{n+1})$ cancels, and
what is left is startlingly simple:

$$A_n - A_{n+1} = \frac{2\mu m g}{k}$$

The amplitude falls by the *same amount* every half-swing. The envelope is a
straight line, not the exponential you get from air drag. And the motion stops
for good once the spring force can no longer beat static friction, which
happens inside the band $|x| < \mu m g/k$.

**Example 4.** A 0.80 kg block on a spring with $k = 160$ N/m sits on a
surface with $\mu = 0.20$, with $g = 9.8$ m/s$^2$. It is pulled 15.0 cm out and
released. How many complete cycles does it manage?

The friction force is $f = \mu m g = (0.20)(0.80)(9.8) = 1.568$ N. Two lengths
come from it. The half-width of the dead band is
$x_0 = f/k = 1.568/160 = 0.0098$ m, and the amplitude loss per half-cycle is
$2x_0 = 0.0196$ m.

So after $n$ half-swings the amplitude is $A_n = 0.150 - 0.0196n$. The block
completes a half-swing only if it can reach the far side, which needs
$A_{n+1} > 0$, that is $A_n > 0.0196$ m. Count:

$A_0 = 0.1500$, $A_1 = 0.1304$, $A_2 = 0.1108$, $A_3 = 0.0912$,
$A_4 = 0.0716$, $A_5 = 0.0520$, $A_6 = 0.0324$, $A_7 = 0.0128$ m.

$A_6 = 0.0324$ m is bigger than 0.0196 m, so the seventh half-swing happens and
lands at $A_7 = 0.0128$ m. But $A_7$ is smaller than 0.0196 m, so there is no
longer enough stored energy to carry the block all the way across, and it
stalls part-way through the eighth half-swing. Seven complete half-swings is
three and a half swings, which is **3 complete cycles**.

**Air drag** behaves differently: the loss per cycle is a fixed *fraction* of
the energy, so $E$ decays exponentially. That is what a quality factor $Q$
encodes, through $E(t) = E_0 e^{-\omega_0 t/Q}$. Over one period
$T = 2\pi/\omega_0$ the exponent becomes $-\omega_0 T/Q = -2\pi/Q$, so the
fraction lost per cycle is

$$\frac{\Delta E}{E} = 1 - e^{-2\pi/Q}$$

For $Q = 40$: $2\pi/40 = 0.1571$, $e^{-0.1571} = 0.8546$, so the fork loses
$1 - 0.8546 = 0.145$ of its energy each period. High $Q$ means a stingy
oscillator.

## When the moving thing is not one block

$K = \tfrac12 mv^2$ is a special case. Two upgrades cover most of what the
problems throw at you, and both leave every other formula here
intact — you only swap in a different effective mass.

If the oscillating object **rolls without slipping**, it stores kinetic energy
in spin as well as in translation:
$K = \tfrac12 mv^2 + \tfrac12 I\omega_{\text{spin}}^2$ with
$\omega_{\text{spin}} = v/R$, which becomes
$K = \tfrac12 m v^2 (1 + I/mR^2)$. A uniform solid cylinder has
$I = \tfrac12 mR^2$, so the bracket is $1.5$ and $K = \tfrac34 mv^2$. For a
0.60 kg cylinder on a 180 N/m spring released at 9.0 cm:
$\tfrac12(180)(0.090)^2 = 0.729$ J, and setting
$\tfrac34(0.60)v^2 = 0.729$ gives $v^2 = 0.729/0.45 = 1.62$ and $v = 1.27$
m/s. Slower than a sliding block would be, because a third of the budget went
into spin.

If **two masses share one spring** with the centre of mass at rest, the kinetic
energy written in terms of the relative speed $v_{\text{rel}}$ is
$K = \tfrac12 m_* v_{\text{rel}}^2$, where
$m_* = m_1m_2/(m_1+m_2)$ is the *reduced mass* (a different $\mu$ is already
doing duty as the friction coefficient, so it gets a star here). With 0.40 kg
and 0.60 kg, $m_* = 0.24/1.00 = 0.24$ kg, and a 250 N/m spring stretched
5.0 cm gives $\tfrac12(0.24)v_{\text{rel}}^2 = \tfrac12(250)(0.050)^2$, so
$v_{\text{rel}} = 0.050\sqrt{250/0.24} = 0.050 \times 32.27 = 1.61$ m/s.

One last idea, for the problem where the mass drifts slowly, like sand leaking
from a swinging bucket. If a parameter changes over many periods, the ratio
$E/\omega$ stays fixed. Since $E = \tfrac12 kA^2$ and $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$,
that forces $A^2 \propto \omega \propto m^{-1/2}$, so $A \propto m^{-1/4}$.
Lose three quarters of the sand and the amplitude grows by
$4^{1/4} = \sqrt2$.

## Recognising that this is the tool

Reach for energy, not for $x(t)$, when a problem shows any of these:

- It asks for a **speed at a stated position**, or a position where the speed
  takes some value, and never mentions the time. That is $v = \omega\sqrt{A^2-x^2}$.
- It asks for an **amplitude after something changed** — putty added, a rider
  removed, drag applied. Find the energy just after the change and set it equal
  to $\tfrac12 kA'^2$.
- It gives a **friction coefficient** and asks for cycles, distance, or a final
  resting place. Energy in minus $\mu m g d$ out.
- It quotes a **fraction of energy**, a ratio of energies, or a $Q$. Ratios
  never need numbers for $m$ or $k$.
- It asks for a **force** at some point in the swing. Energy first for the
  speed, then $\sum F = mv^2/r$.

Time-domain methods answer "when". Energy answers everything else, and it does
not care whether the restoring force came from a spring, from gravity on a
pendulum, or from something stranger. That last point is the opening for the
[next lesson](/guide/effective-potentials), where you stop assuming the
potential is a parabola and start finding out what oscillates near the bottom
of any well at all.
