## The rate at which work gets done

Two cranes lift the same girder to the same height. They do identical work. The
one that finishes in ten seconds instead of a hundred is the one that costs
more to build. Power is the quantity that separates them:

$$P_{\text{avg}} = \frac{W}{\Delta t}, \qquad P = \frac{dW}{dt}$$

The unit is the watt, one joule per second. The horsepower still used for
engines is about $746\text{ W}$.

Average power is a whole trip divided by a whole time. Instantaneous power is
what the machine is delivering right now, and the two can be wildly different:
a sprinter's peak output is several times the average over a race.

## P equals F dot v

Substitute $dW = \vec F \cdot d\vec r$ and divide by $dt$:

$$P = \vec F \cdot \vec v = Fv\cos\theta$$

This is the form to reach for in almost every problem. It says a force delivers
power in proportion to how fast its point of application is moving, and that a
force perpendicular to the velocity delivers none — the same reason such forces
do no work.

A useful consequence: at constant speed on a level road, the engine's output
exactly balances the losses. If drag goes as $F_d = bv^2$, then

$$P = bv^3$$

so doubling a car's top speed demands eight times the power. That cubic is why
top speeds creep up so slowly with engine size.

## When power, not force, is the limit

Beginning problems assume constant acceleration. Real engines and motors
instead have a ceiling on power, and this changes the shape of the motion.

A car of mass $m$ pulling away at maximum power $P$ satisfies $Fv = P$, so
$F = P/v$. At low speed the force is enormous — in practice tire grip caps it —
and as the car speeds up the available force falls. Setting $ma = P/v$ and
integrating gives

$$v(t) = \sqrt{\frac{2Pt}{m}}$$

Speed grows as $\sqrt{t}$, not linearly, and the acceleration decays. The same
reasoning answers "how long to reach $30\text{ m/s}$" without ever naming a
force, and it explains why the last few miles per hour take so long.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  A constant-power problem is not a constant-acceleration problem. The
  kinematic equations $v = v_0 + at$ and $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2ad$ do not apply.
  Start from $Fv = P$, write $mv\,dv/dx = P/v$ or $m\,dv/dt = P/v$, and
  integrate.
</Aside>

## Checking an answer

Power is a good sanity check on energy problems. Compute the energy a process
requires and divide by a plausible time; if a household appliance comes out
needing a megawatt, something is wrong. Working backward from a stated power
rating to the force or the time is one of the most common ways olympiad
problems disguise an energy question.
