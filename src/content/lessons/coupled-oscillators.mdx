## Two oscillators that talk to each other

A single mass on a spring has one frequency and every motion is a sine wave at
that frequency. Couple two masses and the motion stops looking periodic:
energy sloshes from one mass to the other and back, and neither trace is a
clean sine.

Take two equal masses $m$ on a line, each tied to a wall by a spring of
constant $k$, with a third spring of constant $k_c$ between them. Let $x_1$
and $x_2$ be displacements from equilibrium. The middle spring is stretched by
$x_2 - x_1$, so

$$m\ddot x_1 = -kx_1 + k_c(x_2 - x_1)$$
$$m\ddot x_2 = -kx_2 - k_c(x_2 - x_1)$$

Neither equation can be solved alone; each contains the other's coordinate.
That coupling is the whole difficulty, and the whole trick is to find
combinations of $x_1$ and $x_2$ that uncouple.

## Normal modes

A **normal mode** is a motion in which every part of the system oscillates at
the same frequency with a fixed ratio of amplitudes. The shape does not
change, only its overall size. For symmetric systems you can usually guess the
modes from the symmetry.

**Symmetric mode.** Both masses move together, $x_1 = x_2$. Then
$x_2 - x_1 = 0$, the middle spring is never stretched, and it contributes
nothing. Each mass sees only its wall spring:

$$\omega_s = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$$

**Antisymmetric mode.** The masses move oppositely, $x_2 = -x_1$. The middle
spring stretches by $2x_1$, so mass one feels $-kx_1 - 2k_c x_1$:

$$\omega_a = \sqrt{\frac{k + 2k_c}{m}}$$

The antisymmetric mode is always the faster one, because that is the mode that
deforms the coupling spring. Stronger coupling pushes the two frequencies
further apart and leaves the symmetric one untouched.

Add the two equations of motion and the coupling cancels, giving an equation
for $x_1 + x_2$ alone; subtract them and you get one for $x_1 - x_2$. Those
sums and differences are the **normal coordinates**, each a plain simple
harmonic oscillator. Guessing by symmetry and doing the algebra give the same
answer.

## Any motion is a superposition

The equations are linear, so the general solution is a sum of the two modes:

$$x_1(t) = A\cos(\omega_s t + \phi_s) + B\cos(\omega_a t + \phi_a)$$
$$x_2(t) = A\cos(\omega_s t + \phi_s) - B\cos(\omega_a t + \phi_a)$$

Four constants for four initial conditions. This is why the energy transfer is
not mysterious. Hold mass two fixed, pull mass one aside, release both: that
initial condition needs equal amounts of each mode, $A = B$. The two modes
drift out of phase, and when they are fully opposed the first mass sits still
while the second carries all the amplitude. The beat period is set by
$\omega_a - \omega_s$, so weak coupling means slow, complete energy exchange.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Count first. A system of $N$ masses moving in one dimension has $N$ degrees
  of freedom and therefore exactly $N$ normal modes. If your symmetry argument
  has produced fewer than that, you have missed one, often a zero-frequency
  mode in which the whole system translates without deforming any spring.
</Aside>

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  The symmetric and antisymmetric guesses only work when the system genuinely
  has that symmetry: equal masses and a mirror-symmetric spring arrangement.
  Make the masses unequal and the modes are still real but the amplitude ratio
  is no longer plus or minus one, and you must solve the determinant condition
  rather than guess. Check the symmetry before you use the shortcut.
</Aside>
