## Chop it into point charges

A rod or a disk is not a point charge, but every little piece of it is. Cut
the object into elements $dq$, write the field each element produces, and
integrate:

$$d\vec E = \frac{k\,dq}{r^2}\hat r, \qquad \vec E = \int d\vec E$$

The physics is done in the first line. Everything after it is setting up an
integral correctly, and there are only three decisions to make.

## The three decisions

**Choose $dq$.** Pick one variable that sweeps the object once, and express
$dq$ in it: $dq = \lambda\,dx$ for a line, $\sigma\,dA$ for a surface,
$\rho\,dV$ for a volume. For a disk the good element is a thin ring of radius
$s$, area $2\pi s\,ds$, because you already know a ring's field.

**Kill a component.** Before integrating, find the symmetry. For a point on
the axis of a ring, every element has a partner on the opposite side whose
transverse contribution cancels. Only the axial part survives, and each
element contributes $dE\cos\theta$ with $\cos\theta = z/\sqrt{z^2+R^2}$.

**Express everything in the integration variable.** $r$, $\cos\theta$, and
$dq$ must all be written in terms of the one variable, with limits that cover
the object exactly once.

## Carrying it through

For the ring, $r$ and $\cos\theta$ are the same for every element, so the
integral is trivial and $\int dq = Q$:

$$E_z = \frac{kQz}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}}$$

Stack rings to get the disk. With $dq = \sigma\,2\pi s\,ds$ and $R \to s$,

$$E_z = 2\pi k\sigma\left(1 - \frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}}\right) \xrightarrow{R\to\infty} \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$$

That limit is the infinite sheet, which Gauss's law also gives in one line —
a useful check that the setup was right.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Do not integrate $|d\vec E|$ and call it $E$. Adding magnitudes ignores
  direction and always overestimates. Project onto the surviving axis *first*,
  put the $\cos\theta$ inside the integral, then integrate a scalar.
</Aside>

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Check every result in two limits. Far away, a bounded distribution must look
  like a point charge; the ring gives $kQ/z^2$ for $z \gg R$. Close in, a
  surface must look infinite.
</Aside>
