## The magnetic counterpart of Gauss's law

Gauss's law converts a hard integral into an easy one whenever symmetry makes
the field constant over a closed surface. Ampere's law does the same trick with
a closed loop instead of a surface:

$$\oint \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{\ell} = \mu_0 I_{enc}$$

The circulation of $\vec{B}$ around any closed path equals $\mu_0$ times the
current threading that path. Only current passing through the loop counts.
Current outside contributes to $\vec{B}$ at every individual point, but its
contributions to the circulation cancel exactly.

Sign convention: curl the right hand along the direction you traverse the loop,
and the thumb gives the positive direction for enclosed current. Two equal
currents threading in opposite senses enclose zero net current.

## Choosing a loop

The law is always true and rarely useful. It becomes useful when you can pick a
path on which $\vec{B}$ is either constant and parallel to $d\vec{\ell}$, or
perpendicular to it, or zero. Then the integral collapses to $B \times
(\text{length})$ and you can solve for $B$.

Argue the direction of $\vec{B}$ from symmetry *before* choosing the path. For
a straight wire, rotational symmetry plus the cross-product structure of
Biot-Savart forces the field to be circumferential and to depend only on $r$.
A concentric circle then gives

$$B(2\pi r) = \mu_0 I \quad\Longrightarrow\quad B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$$

For a thick wire of radius $a$ with uniform current density, a loop inside
encloses only the fraction $r^2/a^2$ of the current, so $B = \mu_0 I r /
2\pi a^2$ — linear inside, falling as $1/r$ outside, continuous at the surface.

## Solenoid and toroid

For a long solenoid with $n$ turns per unit length, take a rectangular loop
with one side inside along the axis, one side outside far away where the field
is negligible, and two sides perpendicular to the field contributing nothing.
Only one side survives:

$$B = \mu_0 n I$$

Uniform, independent of position within the interior, and independent of the
solenoid's radius.

A toroid has closed field lines, so a concentric circle of radius $r$ inside
the windings is the natural path. With $N$ total turns:

$$B = \frac{\mu_0 N I}{2\pi r}$$

This one is not uniform — it falls off across the cross-section — and it is
strictly zero outside the toroid, both inside the hole and beyond the outer
edge.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  $I_{enc} = 0$ does not mean $B = 0$. It means the *circulation* vanishes.
  Directly outside a toroid, or on a loop enclosing two opposite currents, the
  integral is zero while the field at individual points need not be. You may
  conclude $B = 0$ only when a symmetry argument has already established that
  $B$ is constant in magnitude and everywhere tangent to your chosen path.
</Aside>
