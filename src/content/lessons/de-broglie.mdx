In 1927 two engineers at Bell Labs were bouncing electrons off a lump of nickel
and measuring how many came back at each angle. A bottle of liquid air broke,
air got into their vacuum tube, the nickel oxidised, and to clean it they baked
it hot enough to turn the surface into a few large crystals. When they ran the
experiment again the smooth curve they had been getting was gone. In its place
were sharp peaks at particular angles.

Sharp peaks at particular angles is what a wave does when it meets a regular
array of scatterers. It is what X-rays do to a crystal, and what light does to a
diffraction grating. But electrons are particles. They have a definite mass, you
can count them one at a time, and Millikan had measured the charge on a single
one. This lesson is about what has to be true for both of those things to hold
at once, and about the single equation that makes it quantitative.

## Running the photon relation backwards

You already know one object that is both. Light is a wave — it diffracts, it
interferes, it has a wavelength you can measure with a ruler and two slits. And
light is also lumpy: the photoelectric effect only makes sense if light arrives
in packets of energy $E = hf$, where $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s is Planck's
constant and $f$ is the frequency.

A photon carries momentum as well as energy. For anything moving at $c$,
relativity gives $E = pc$, so the momentum of a photon is $p = E/c = hf/c$. And
since $f = c/\lambda$ for a wave travelling at $c$, that becomes

$$p = \frac{h}{\lambda}$$

Read this as a sentence about a wave: short wavelength means large momentum.
Nothing in it mentions light specifically. It is a dictionary entry translating
between a wave word, $\lambda$, and a particle word, $p$.

De Broglie's proposal in 1924 was that the dictionary works in both directions.
If a thing you thought was a wave turns out to carry momentum in lumps, then a
thing you know carries momentum should have a wavelength. Solve the relation the
other way round and you get the whole content of this lesson:

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$$

That $\lambda$ is called the **de Broglie wavelength**. The claim is not that an
electron is "really" a wave or "really" a particle. It is that whenever you set
up an experiment sensitive to wavelength — a grating, a crystal, a pair of slits
— the electron will behave with *this* wavelength, the one set by its momentum.

Notice which quantity is on the bottom. It is momentum, not speed and not
energy. Every mistake people make with this formula comes from reaching for the
wrong one of those three.

## Why a baseball has no visible wavelength

Put in a thrown baseball: $m = 0.145$ kg at $v = 40.0$ m/s. Its momentum is
$p = mv = 5.80$ kg·m/s, so

$$\lambda = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{5.80} = 1.14 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{m}$$

A proton is about $10^{-15}$ m across. The baseball's wavelength is nineteen
orders of magnitude smaller than that. Diffraction only shows up when the wave
meets an obstacle or an opening comparable to its own wavelength, and there is
no aperture in the universe that small. The wave nature is not switched off for
big objects. It is present and permanently unmeasurable, which in practice is
the same thing.

Now the other extreme. An electron, $m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}$ kg, moving at
$1.00 \times 10^6$ m/s. Then $p = (9.11 \times 10^{-31})(1.00 \times 10^6) =
9.11 \times 10^{-25}$ kg·m/s and

$$\lambda = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{9.11 \times 10^{-25}} = 7.28 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m}$$

Seven ångströms. That is a few atomic diameters — precisely the scale of the
gaps between atoms in a crystal. Matter waves are hidden from you not because
they are weak but because $h$ is tiny, so you need a tiny momentum to get a
usable wavelength, and tiny momentum means a very light particle.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Do not put kinetic energy where momentum belongs. Two particles with the same
  kinetic energy have different wavelengths if their masses differ, because
  $p = \sqrt{2mK}$ carries a factor of $\sqrt{m}$. A proton and an electron at
  the same kinetic energy have wavelengths in the ratio
  $\lambda_e/\lambda_p = \sqrt{m_p/m_e} = \sqrt{1836} = 42.8$. At the same
  *speed*, the ratio is different again: $\lambda_p/\lambda_e = m_e/m_p =
  1/1836$. Read the problem to see which is held fixed.
</Aside>

## Getting the wavelength from an accelerating voltage

Most of the time you are not handed a speed. You are told an electron was
accelerated from rest through a potential difference $V$, or that a particle has
kinetic energy $K$. Convert to momentum first.

Non-relativistically, $K = p^2/2m$, so $p = \sqrt{2mK}$ and

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mK}}$$

There is a version of this that is much easier to use in your head. Multiply top
and bottom by $c$:

$$\lambda = \frac{hc}{\sqrt{2(mc^2)K}}$$

Now everything is an energy. With $hc = 1240$ eV·nm and the rest energy
$m_ec^2 = 511\,000$ eV, an electron pushed through $V$ volts has $K = eV$, i.e.
$K$ in electron-volts is numerically $V$. So

$$\lambda = \frac{1240}{\sqrt{2(511\,000)V}}\ \text{nm} = \frac{1240}{1011\sqrt{V}}\ \text{nm} = \frac{1.226}{\sqrt{V}}\ \text{nm}$$

That is the formula quoted in half the problems you will meet:
$\lambda = 1.226/\sqrt{V}$ nm, with $V$ in volts. One volt gives 1.226 nm.
A hundred volts gives 0.1226 nm, about one atomic spacing. A hundred thousand
volts — an electron microscope — gives 3.88 pm, which is why electron
microscopes see atoms and light microscopes never will.

Keep the $hc$ and $mc^2$ version in mind even for other particles. For a neutron
with $K = 0.0250$ eV (a "thermal" neutron, the kind that comes out of a
reactor), $m_nc^2 = 939.6$ MeV $= 9.396 \times 10^8$ eV, so

$$\lambda = \frac{1240}{\sqrt{2(9.396 \times 10^8)(0.0250)}} = \frac{1240}{\sqrt{4.698 \times 10^7}} = \frac{1240}{6854} = 0.181\ \text{nm}$$

Again an atomic spacing. That is not luck: it is why neutron scattering is a
standard tool for finding out where atoms sit in a solid.

## Where the peaks come from

Back to the nickel crystal. A crystal is a stack of parallel planes of atoms,
separated by a distance $d$ of a couple of ångströms. A wave coming in at a
glancing angle $\theta$ to those planes reflects a little from each plane. The
beam reflecting from the second plane travels an extra distance $2d\sin\theta$
compared with the one from the first. Those two beams reinforce each other only
when that extra distance is a whole number of wavelengths:

$$2d\sin\theta = n\lambda$$

This is **Bragg's law**, and it was already familiar from X-rays. The startling
thing Davisson and Germer found was that electrons obey it too, with the
$\lambda$ that de Broglie's formula predicts from the accelerating voltage.
Nothing else in the experiment knows about $h$.

**Worked example: an electron Bragg peak.** Electrons accelerated through
200 V reflect from planes 0.200 nm apart. Where is the first-order peak?

First the wavelength: $\lambda = 1.226/\sqrt{200} = 1.226/14.14 = 0.0867$ nm.

Then Bragg with $n = 1$:

$$\sin\theta = \frac{\lambda}{2d} = \frac{0.0867}{2(0.200)} = 0.2167$$

so $\theta = \arcsin(0.2167) = 12.5^\circ$. If you had used the $n = 2$ peak you
would need $\sin\theta = 0.433$, giving $25.7^\circ$; if $\lambda$ were ever
bigger than $2d$, no peak exists at all, because a sine cannot exceed one.

Not every diffraction problem is Bragg. If the electrons go *through* two slits
a distance $d$ apart onto a screen a distance $L$ away, the fringe spacing is
the same one you learned for light, $\Delta y = \lambda L / d$. The physics of
the interference is unchanged; only the source of $\lambda$ is new.

**Worked example: an electron double slit.** 50.0 keV electrons, slits 1.00 µm
apart, screen 1.00 m away.

$$\lambda = \frac{1.226}{\sqrt{50\,000}}\ \text{nm} = \frac{1.226}{223.6} = 5.48 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{nm} = 5.48 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{m}$$

$$\Delta y = \frac{\lambda L}{d} = \frac{(5.48 \times 10^{-12})(1.00)}{1.00 \times 10^{-6}} = 5.48 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}$$

Fringes 5.5 µm apart. Small, but a microscope can resolve that, and the
experiment has been done with electrons, with neutrons, and with whole
C$_{60}$ molecules of 720 atoms.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  At high energy the non-relativistic $p = \sqrt{2mK}$ fails, and the
  $1.226/\sqrt{V}$ formula fails with it. Use the exact relation
  $(pc)^2 = K^2 + 2K(mc^2)$, which comes straight from $E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2$
  with $E = K + mc^2$. It reduces to $pc = \sqrt{2(mc^2)K}$ when
  $K \ll mc^2$, so you can always use it and never think about which regime you
  are in.
</Aside>

**Worked example: a 1.00 MeV electron.** Its rest energy is 0.511 MeV, so this
is firmly relativistic and the shortcut formula would be badly wrong.

Total energy: $E = K + m_ec^2 = 1.00 + 0.511 = 1.511$ MeV.

$$(pc)^2 = E^2 - (m_ec^2)^2 = (1.511)^2 - (0.511)^2 = 2.283 - 0.261 = 2.022\ \text{MeV}^2$$

so $pc = 1.422$ MeV $= 1.422 \times 10^6$ eV. Then

$$\lambda = \frac{hc}{pc} = \frac{1240\ \text{eV·nm}}{1.422 \times 10^6\ \text{eV}} = 8.72 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{nm} = 0.872\ \text{pm}$$

The non-relativistic formula would have given $1.226/\sqrt{10^6} = 1.23$ pm,
about 40% too long.

## Standing waves, and why energy comes in levels

A wavelength attached to a particle explains something Bohr had only been able
to assert. In the [Bohr model](/guide/bohr-model) you were told that angular
momentum comes in units of $\hbar$, with no reason given. De Broglie gave the
reason. An electron going round a circular orbit of radius $r$ is a wave chasing
its own tail. If the circumference is not a whole number of wavelengths, the
wave comes back out of step with itself and cancels. So the surviving orbits are
the ones with

$$2\pi r = n\lambda = \frac{nh}{p}$$

Rearrange: $pr = nh/2\pi$, that is, $mvr = n\hbar$. Bohr's rule, derived rather
than decreed.

The same argument in a straight line gives you the single most useful toy model
in quantum mechanics. Trap a particle between two hard walls a distance $L$
apart. The wave must vanish at both walls, so exactly a whole number of
half-wavelengths fits in: $L = n\lambda/2$, giving $\lambda = 2L/n$ for
$n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$. Feed that back through de Broglie: $p = h/\lambda = nh/2L$,
and then $K = p^2/2m$ gives

$$E_n = \frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2} = \frac{n^2(hc)^2}{8(mc^2)L^2}$$

The energy cannot be anything you like. It comes in levels, labelled by how many
half-waves fit, and the lowest one is not zero. A confined particle always has
some kinetic energy simply because confinement forces a curve into its wave.

**Worked example: a proton in a nucleus.** Model a proton as confined to a
one-dimensional box of length $L = 5.00$ fm $= 5.00 \times 10^{-6}$ nm. Its
rest energy is $m_pc^2 = 938.3$ MeV $= 9.383 \times 10^8$ eV. The ground state
is $n = 1$:

$$E_1 = \frac{(1240)^2}{8(9.383 \times 10^8)(5.00 \times 10^{-6})^2}$$

Numerator: $1240^2 = 1.538 \times 10^6$ eV²·nm².
Denominator: $8 \times 9.383 \times 10^8 \times 2.50 \times 10^{-11} =
1.877 \times 10^{-1}$ eV·nm².
Dividing, $E_1 = 8.19 \times 10^6$ eV $= 8.19$ MeV.

Megaelectron-volts, from nothing but a box and a wavelength. That is the right
order of magnitude for nuclear binding energies, and it is why nuclear physics
is million-fold more energetic than chemistry: the box is a hundred thousand
times smaller, and $E \propto 1/L^2$.

## Uncertainty as a consequence

A wave with one exact wavelength has no location: a pure sine stretches forever.
To build something localised within a distance $\Delta x$ you have to add up
waves covering a spread of wavelengths, and through $p = h/\lambda$ that is a
spread of momenta. The sharper you make the position, the wider the spread of
momentum you needed. Quantitatively,

$$\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}, \qquad \hbar = \frac{h}{2\pi} = 1.055 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J·s}$$

For estimates, drop the factor of two and use $\Delta p \approx \hbar/\Delta x$.
This turns "how big is it" into "how much momentum must it have", and it is
often enough to get an answer within a factor of two without solving anything.

**Worked example: estimating hydrogen's ground state.** Say the electron is
confined to within a radius $r$ of the proton. Then its momentum is at least
about $p \approx \hbar / r$, so its kinetic energy is about $p^2/2m_e =
\hbar^2/(2m_er^2)$, and its electrical potential energy is $-ke^2/r$. The total:

$$E(r) = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m_er^2} - \frac{ke^2}{r}$$

Squeeze the atom and the first term blows up; let it spread and the second term
becomes feeble. Something in between is the minimum. Differentiate and set to
zero:

$$
\frac{dE}{dr} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{m_er^3} + \frac{ke^2}{r^2} = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \frac{\hbar^2}{m_eke^2}
$$

That is exactly the Bohr radius, $0.529 \times 10^{-10}$ m. Put it back in.
At that radius the kinetic term is $\tfrac{1}{2}m_ek^2e^4/\hbar^2$ and the
potential term is $-m_ek^2e^4/\hbar^2$, so

$$E_{\min} = -\frac{m_ek^2e^4}{2\hbar^2} = -13.6\ \text{eV}$$

The measured ionisation energy of hydrogen, from a one-line estimate. The atom
is the size it is because that size is the truce between confinement energy and
electrical attraction. And the same argument tells you why electrons are not
inside nuclei: confine one to $10^{-14}$ m and $pc \approx \hbar c/\Delta x =
197.3\ \text{eV·nm} / 10^{-5}\ \text{nm} = 19.7$ MeV, far more energy than any
nucleus could hold onto.

## Recognising when this is the tool

The signals are specific.

**A wavelength is asked for, or given, about something with mass.** Electron,
neutron, proton, atom, molecule, buckyball. That is $\lambda = h/p$ and nothing
else. Your only real work is getting $p$: from $mv$ if you are given speed, from
$\sqrt{2mK}$ if you are given energy or a voltage, from
$\sqrt{K^2 + 2Kmc^2}/c$ if the kinetic energy is comparable to $mc^2$.

**Angles, fringes, slits or crystal planes with particles going in.** Find
$\lambda$ first, then use whichever optics formula the geometry calls for:
$2d\sin\theta = n\lambda$ for reflection off planes, $d\sin\theta = n\lambda$
for a grating or a surface row, $\Delta y = \lambda L/d$ for two slits,
$a\sin\theta = \lambda$ for the first minimum of a single slit of width $a$.
The wave part is ordinary optics; the quantum part is one line at the start.

**A particle is described as confined, trapped, in a well, or "inside" something
of a stated size.** Fit half-waves: $\lambda = 2L/n$ and $E_n = n^2(hc)^2 /
(8mc^2L^2)$. If the question says "estimate" rather than "find", use
$\Delta p \approx \hbar/\Delta x$ instead and expect agreement to a factor of a
few.

**The word "estimate" plus a size.** That is uncertainty-principle work. Write
$p \approx \hbar/r$, build an energy, and minimise if there is a competing term.

Work in $hc = 1240$ eV·nm and $\hbar c = 197.3$ eV·nm wherever you can. They let
you stay in electron-volts and nanometres from start to finish and skip the
powers of ten that generate most wrong answers.

Next you will meet the one place where the energies get larger still, and where
particles convert into one another rather than merely diffract:
[radioactivity and nuclear decay](/guide/radioactivity).
