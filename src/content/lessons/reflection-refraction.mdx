## Bending at a boundary

The index of refraction is a ratio of speeds: $n = c/v$. Light in glass with
$n = 1.5$ travels at two thirds of $c$.

When a wavefront crosses a boundary at an angle, one edge enters the slow
medium before the other and falls behind, pivoting the front. Frequency is
fixed by the source and cannot change at a boundary, so it is the wavelength
that shrinks: $\lambda_n = \lambda_0 / n$. Matching the wavefronts along the
surface gives

$$n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2$$

with angles measured from the normal, never from the surface. Reflection obeys
the simpler $\theta_i = \theta_r$.

## Total internal reflection

Going from dense to rare, $n_1 > n_2$, Snell's law demands
$\sin\theta_2 = (n_1/n_2)\sin\theta_1 > \sin\theta_1$. The refracted ray bends
away from the normal, and at some incidence it would need $\sin\theta_2 > 1$.
There is no such angle, so nothing is transmitted. Everything reflects. The
threshold is

$$\sin\theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$$

Glass to air gives about $42°$, which is why a prism can turn light through a
right angle with no coating and why an optical fiber holds light in its core
over kilometers. Note that TIR only exists in the dense-to-rare direction —
there is no critical angle going into glass.

## What an image is

An image is a place where rays either meet or appear to have come from. Your
eye cannot tell the difference; it reconstructs a source from the directions of
arriving rays and reports whatever point they diverge from.

Rays that physically converge form a real image, and a screen there shows it.
Rays that only extrapolate backward to a point form a virtual image, and a
screen there shows nothing. The reflection behind a flat mirror is virtual: the
rays never went there. Refraction at a flat surface makes a pool look shallower
by the factor $n$ for the same reason.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  At a boundary light does not choose between reflecting and refracting — it
  does both, in proportions that change with angle. Below the critical angle
  there is always a partial reflection. Energy problems that assume all the
  light transmits will be off, sometimes badly at grazing incidence.
</Aside>
