A guitar string is about 0.65 m long and you can pull it into any shape you
like before letting go. Yet when you let go it sings one note, the same note
every time, and the note only changes if you change the string's length or how
hard it is stretched. A flute is a tube of air with holes in it, and it does
the same trick: one length of air column, one pitch. Nothing about a plucked
string or a blown pipe says in advance which frequency it should pick.

So where does the note come from? It comes from the ends. In the [previous
lesson](/guide/wave-basics) a wave ran along the string forever and never met
anything. Put two ends on the string and the wave comes back. What arrives at
any point is now two waves at once, one going each way, and almost every
frequency dies from that interference. A few survive. Those are the notes.

## Two waves on one string

Start with the rule that makes all of this work. **Superposition**: when two
waves overlap, the displacement at a point is the sum of what each wave would
have given on its own. Waves do not collide or push each other aside. They add,
pass through, and come out the other side unchanged.

Take the wave from the last lesson, $y = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$, moving in the
$+x$ direction with amplitude $A$, wave number $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ and angular
frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$. Its reflection off a far end is an identical wave
moving the other way, $y = A\sin(kx + \omega t)$. Add them:

$$
y(x,t) = A\sin(kx - \omega t) + A\sin(kx + \omega t)
$$

Use $\sin(P) + \sin(Q) = 2\sin\!\big(\tfrac{P+Q}{2}\big)\cos\!\big(\tfrac{P-Q}{2}\big)$,
with $P = kx - \omega t$ and $Q = kx + \omega t$. The half-sum is $kx$ and the
half-difference is $-\omega t$, and cosine does not care about that sign. So

$$
y(x,t) = 2A\sin(kx)\,\cos(\omega t)
$$

Look hard at what just happened. In the original waves, $x$ and $t$ were locked
together inside one bracket, which is what made the shape travel. Now they sit
in separate factors. Nothing moves along the string any more. Every point
oscillates in time as $\cos(\omega t)$, all in step, and the only thing that
changes from place to place is the size of the swing, $2A\sin(kx)$. That is a
**standing wave**, and the factorising is the whole of it.

## Nodes, antinodes, and the half-wavelength rule

The amplitude factor $2A\sin(kx)$ does two special things.

Where $\sin(kx) = 0$, the string never moves at all, no matter what $t$ is.
Those points are **nodes**. They happen when $kx = 0, \pi, 2\pi, \dots$, that
is at $x = 0, \lambda/2, \lambda, \dots$, since $k = 2\pi/\lambda$. So
**adjacent nodes are half a wavelength apart.** Not a whole one. This single
fact does more work in this topic than any formula.

Halfway between two nodes, $\sin(kx) = \pm 1$ and the point swings with
amplitude $2A$, twice what either travelling wave carried alone. Those are
**antinodes**. A node and its neighbouring antinode are a quarter wavelength
apart.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Node to node is $\lambda/2$. Node to antinode is $\lambda/4$. If a problem
  hands you a distance between two features of a standing wave, you are one
  step from $\lambda$, and then $v = f\lambda$ finishes it.
</Aside>

## A string fixed at both ends

Now add the ends. A string clamped at $x = 0$ and $x = L$ cannot move at either
clamp, so both ends have to be nodes. The $x = 0$ end is already a node in
$2A\sin(kx)$. The other end costs you something: you need $\sin(kL) = 0$, so
$kL = n\pi$ for some whole number $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$

Put $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ back in and that says $2\pi L/\lambda = n\pi$, or

$$
L = \frac{n\lambda}{2}
$$

In words: the string holds a whole number of half-wavelengths. Every other
wavelength is forbidden, because its reflection comes back out of step with
itself and cancels. Solve for the wavelength, $\lambda_n = 2L/n$, and feed it
through $f = v/\lambda$:

$$
f_n = \frac{nv}{2L}
$$

These allowed frequencies are the **harmonics**. The lowest, $n = 1$, is the
**fundamental** $f_1 = v/2L$, and every other one is a whole-number multiple of
it: $f_2 = 2f_1$, $f_3 = 3f_1$, and so on. The $n$ also counts things you can
see. Mode $n$ has $n$ loops (each loop is one half-wavelength), $n - 1$ nodes
in the middle, and $n + 1$ nodes if you count the two clamped ends.

One piece is still missing: what sets $v$? For a string it is the tension $T$
pulling it taut and the linear mass density $\mu$, the mass per metre:

$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}
$$

Tighter means faster means higher. Heavier per metre means slower means lower,
which is why the low strings on a guitar are the thick ones. Combining,

$$
f_n = \frac{n}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}
$$

## Pipes, and the harmonics a stopped pipe cannot play

Air in a pipe does the same thing, with one change in the boundary conditions.
A sound wave is a longitudinal wave, so "displacement" means how far a slab of
air slides back and forth along the pipe.

A **closed** end is a solid wall. Air there cannot slide, so a closed end is a
displacement **node**. An **open** end is free to breathe in and out against the
open air, so it is a displacement **antinode**. That is the whole rule.

A pipe open at both ends needs an antinode at each end. Antinode to antinode,
like node to node, is half a wavelength, so the condition is identical to the
string's: $L = n\lambda/2$, and

$$
f_n = \frac{nv}{2L}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots
$$

with $v$ now the speed of sound, about 343 m/s in room-temperature air.

A pipe closed at one end — a **stopped** pipe — needs a node at the closed end
and an antinode at the open one. The shortest fit is a quarter wavelength. Add
a half wavelength and it still fits; add another and it still fits. So the pipe
holds a quarter, or three quarters, or five quarters, never a half:

$$
L = \frac{(2n-1)\lambda}{4}, \qquad f_n = \frac{(2n-1)v}{4L}
$$

Two consequences, both of which problems test. First, the fundamental of a
stopped pipe is $v/4L$, half that of an open pipe the same length: stopping one
end drops the pitch an octave. Second, only the **odd** harmonics exist. After
$f_1$ comes $3f_1$, then $5f_1$. There is no $2f_1$, because that mode would
need a node at the open end.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Pressure and displacement are a quarter wavelength out of step. A
  displacement node is a **pressure** antinode: air cannot move there, so it
  gets squeezed hardest. If a diagram is labelled as a pressure wave and you
  read its zeros as the fixed ends, every mode number comes out wrong. Decide
  which quantity the picture shows before you count anything.
</Aside>

There is one more boundary pair worth naming, because it turns up on strings
too. Clamp a string at one end and tie the other to a light ring that slides
freely on a frictionless rod. The free end cannot be held down, so it is a
displacement antinode. Node at one end, antinode at the other — exactly the
stopped-pipe geometry. So $f_n = (2n-1)v/4L$ there as well, odd harmonics only.
Whenever the two ends are different in kind, you get quarter wavelengths and odd
harmonics. Whenever they match, you get half wavelengths and all harmonics.

## Three worked examples

**A violin string.** The vibrating length is $L = 0.65$ m and transverse waves
travel along it at $v = 520$ m/s. Fixed at both ends, so
$f_1 = v/2L$. The denominator is $2(0.65) = 1.30$ m, and
$520/1.30 = 400$ Hz. Its third harmonic is just $3 \times 400 = 1200$ Hz,
because a both-ends-fixed string keeps every multiple.

**A pipe tuning a string.** A pipe 0.32 m long, closed at one end, is sounded at
its fundamental with $v_{\text{sound}} = 343$ m/s. A string 0.40 m long with
$\mu = 2.5\times10^{-3}$ kg/m, fixed at both ends, resonates in its fundamental
with that tone. Find the string's tension.

Nothing here can be plugged in directly, so set up first. The only thing the
two objects share is a frequency, so get it from the pipe. Stopped pipe,
fundamental, so $f = v/4L = 343/(4 \times 0.32) = 343/1.28 = 268.0$ Hz.

Now go into the string with that number. Its fundamental is $f = v/2L$, so the
wave speed on the string must be

$$
v = 2Lf = 2(0.40)(268.0) = 214.4 \text{ m/s}
$$

That is the speed of a wave on a wire, not the speed of sound; they are
different numbers for the same note, and mixing them up is the main way this
problem is lost. Finally $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$ rearranges to $T = \mu v^2$:

$$
T = (2.5\times10^{-3})(214.4)^2 = (2.5\times10^{-3})(45960) = 114.9 \text{ N}
$$

**Counting resonances as water drains.** A tube 1.00 m long stands vertically,
full of water. A 440 Hz fork sounds over the open top while the water drains
out. Sound travels at 343 m/s. How many times do you hear it resonate?

The frequency is fixed by the fork, so the wavelength is fixed too:
$\lambda = v/f = 343/440 = 0.7795$ m. What changes is the length of air column
above the water. The water surface is a closed end (a node), the top is open
(an antinode), so the column resonates when

$$
L = \frac{(2n-1)\lambda}{4} = (2n-1)(0.1949) \text{ m}
$$

Work through the list: $n = 1$ gives 0.195 m, $n = 2$ gives 0.585 m, $n = 3$
gives 0.974 m, and $n = 4$ would need 1.364 m, which is longer than the tube.
The first three all fit inside 1.00 m, so you hear **three** resonances.

Notice the spacing of those positions: 0.390 m each time, exactly
$\lambda/2$. That is a general result and a fast way to measure the speed of
sound in a lab — find two neighbouring resonance lengths, double the gap to get
$\lambda$, and multiply by the fork's frequency.

<Aside label="Two neighbours give you everything">
  If a string fixed at both ends resonates at 420 Hz and 490 Hz with nothing in
  between, those are consecutive harmonics, so their difference **is** the
  fundamental: 70 Hz. For a stopped pipe the neighbours are $(2n-1)f_1$ and
  $(2n+1)f_1$, so the gap is $2f_1$. Resonances at 750 Hz and 1050 Hz differ by
  300 Hz, and the fundamental is 150 Hz — which checks out, since
  $750 = 5 \times 150$ and $1050 = 7 \times 150$.
</Aside>

## How to recognise one of these

The giveaway word is *resonates*, but the real signal is a **length with two
ends**: a string between bridges, an air column above water, a pipe, a wire on
a sonometer. Whenever a problem fixes a length and then asks for a frequency,
or fixes a frequency and asks for a length, it is a standing-wave problem and
the recipe never changes.

1. Decide what each end is. Fixed, clamped, closed, in water — that is a node.
   Open, free, sliding on a frictionless rod — that is an antinode.
2. Same at both ends, use $L = n\lambda/2$ and $f_n = nv/2L$, all harmonics.
   Different at the two ends, use $L = (2n-1)\lambda/4$ and
   $f_n = (2n-1)v/4L$, odd harmonics only.
3. Get $v$ from the right place. On a string, $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$. In air, it is
   the speed of sound, and if a temperature is quoted, $v$ scales as
   $\sqrt{T_{\text{kelvin}}}$, so a warm pipe sounds sharp.
4. Read what mode you are in. "Second harmonic", "nodes 0.20 m apart", "the
   next resonance above" — each of these pins down $n$ before you solve.

Where students actually lose the marks is step 1 and step 3: calling a stopped
pipe an open one, or putting the speed of sound into a string formula. Write
down the two boundary conditions before you write anything else.

So far the two waves you added had the same frequency. What if they do not
quite? Then the interference pattern cannot stand still, and what you hear is a
throb — that is [beats and interference](/guide/beats-interference).
