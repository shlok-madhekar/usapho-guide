## One equation, three variables

Put some gas in a container and you can measure three things about it: how much
space it fills, how hard it pushes on the walls, and how hot it is. You might
expect those three to be independent. They are not. Squeeze the gas without
letting heat out and it gets hotter. Warm a sealed can and the pressure climbs.
Two of the three are free; the third is then fixed for you.

The relation that fixes it is the ideal gas law — not a law of nature in the
sense that $F = ma$ is, but an *equation of state*, a statement about how one
particular kind of stuff behaves. Real gases obey it well when they are dilute
and far from condensing, which covers nearly every gas in a problem. This
lesson builds it from the molecular picture in
[kinetic theory](/guide/kinetic-theory), then uses it to invert a mercury tube,
compress a diesel cylinder, and predict the temperature three kilometres up.

## Where $PV = nRT$ comes from

The last lesson ended with a result about molecules bouncing off walls. For $N$
molecules of mass $m$ rattling around in a box of volume $V$, the pressure on
the walls is

$$
PV = \tfrac{1}{3} N m \overline{v^2},
$$

where $\overline{v^2}$ is the average of the squared speed. Rewrite the right
side to make the kinetic energy show up. Since the average translational
kinetic energy of one molecule is $\overline{K} = \tfrac12 m \overline{v^2}$,

$$
PV = \tfrac{2}{3} N \overline{K}.
$$

That is pure mechanics: no temperature anywhere. Temperature enters as a
*definition*. Two gases in thermal contact stop exchanging energy when their
molecules have the same average kinetic energy, so that average is the natural
thing to call temperature. The Kelvin scale was already fixed by then, so the
definition carries a conversion constant $k_B$:

$$
\overline{K} = \tfrac{3}{2} k_B T, \qquad k_B = 1.38\times10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}.
$$

Substitute and the $\tfrac23$ and $\tfrac32$ cancel:

$$
PV = N k_B T.
$$

Counting molecules one at a time is awkward, so chemists count in moles. One
mole is $N_A = 6.02\times10^{23}$ of them, so $N = n N_A$, and grouping
$R = N_A k_B = 8.31$ J/(mol·K) gives the form you will use most:

$$
PV = nRT.
$$

The two versions are the same statement divided by Avogadro's number. Use $n$
and $R$ when the problem talks about moles or grams, $N$ and $k_B$ when it
talks about individual molecules.

Read it in words. At fixed temperature, pressure and volume trade off inversely
— halve the volume and the pressure doubles. At fixed volume, pressure rises in
direct proportion to *absolute* temperature. And $T$ is always in kelvin, never
celsius, because the derivation ran through $\overline{K} = \tfrac32 k_B T$ and
kinetic energy cannot be negative.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Putting celsius into $PV = nRT$ is the single most common error in this
  topic, and it does not announce itself — you get a plausible-looking number
  that is wrong by a factor of a few. Convert first, every time: $T(\text{K}) =
  T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273$. The same goes for gauge pressure. A tyre gauge
  reading $2.00\times10^5$ Pa means the gas is at $2.00\times10^5 +
  1.013\times10^5 = 3.01\times10^5$ Pa absolute, and only the absolute value
  belongs in the equation.
</Aside>

## The ratio form, which is what you actually use

Most problems never need $R$. They give you a gas in one state and ask about
another, with the amount of gas unchanged. Then $nR$ is a constant you never
have to evaluate, and dividing the two states gives

$$
\frac{P_1 V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2 V_2}{T_2}.
$$

Cross out whatever is held fixed. Rigid container: $V$ cancels, so $P \propto
T$. Fixed temperature: $PV$ is constant, which is Boyle's law. Fixed pressure:
$V \propto T$.

**Example 1 — a tyre after a highway run.** A tyre of essentially fixed volume
reads a gauge pressure of $2.00\times10^5$ Pa at $280$ K. Driving warms the air
to $310$ K. Atmospheric pressure is $1.013\times10^5$ Pa. Find the new gauge
pressure.

Convert to absolute first: $P_1 = 2.00\times10^5 + 1.013\times10^5 =
3.013\times10^5$ Pa. The volume is fixed, so

$$
P_2 = P_1 \frac{T_2}{T_1} = 3.013\times10^5 \times \frac{310}{280}
= 3.013\times10^5 \times 1.1071 = 3.336\times10^5\ \text{Pa}.
$$

The question asks for gauge, so subtract the atmosphere back off:
$3.336\times10^5 - 1.013\times10^5 = 2.32\times10^5$ Pa. Had you used gauge
pressure throughout you would have got $2.21\times10^5$ Pa — close enough to
look right, and wrong.

## Pressure bookkeeping: pistons and mercury

Half the difficulty in gas problems is not the gas law at all. It is working
out what $P$ is before you use it. Two setups cover almost everything.

A **piston** in equilibrium is a free body. The gas below pushes up with
$P_{\text{gas}} A$, the atmosphere pushes down with $P_0 A$, and gravity pulls
down with $Mg$. So $P_{\text{gas}} = P_0 + Mg/A$ for a piston sitting on top of
the gas, and $P_0 - Mg/A$ if the piston hangs below it. With $M = 20.0$ kg and
$A = 0.0100$ m², the piston contributes $20.0 \times 9.8 / 0.0100 =
1.96\times10^4$ Pa, giving $1.209\times10^5$ Pa.

A **column of liquid** of height $h$ contributes $\rho g h$ to the pressure at
its bottom. Mercury problems quote lengths in cmHg and never need converting,
because the conversion cancels. What matters is only whether the mercury adds
to or subtracts from the atmospheric pressure holding the gas in.

**Example 2 — inverting a mercury-sealed tube.** A tube closed at the bottom
stands upright. It traps a $20.0$ cm air column, capped by $10.0$ cm of
mercury, open to an atmosphere of $75.0$ cmHg. Turn the tube over, closed end
now up, and find the new air column length at unchanged temperature.

In the first configuration the trapped air holds up $10.0$ cm of mercury and
the atmosphere above that:

$$
P_1 = 75.0 + 10.0 = 85.0\ \text{cmHg}.
$$

Now invert. The gas sits at the closed top with the mercury hanging below it,
and below that the open end feels the atmosphere. Work up from the open end:
start at $75.0$, then climbing $10.0$ cm of mercury *drops* the pressure by
$10.0$:

$$
P_2 = 75.0 - 10.0 = 65.0\ \text{cmHg}.
$$

The gas is at lower pressure, so it expands. The tube has uniform area, so
volume is proportional to length, and Boyle's law gives

$$
L_2 = L_1 \frac{P_1}{P_2} = 20.0 \times \frac{85.0}{65.0}
= 20.0 \times 1.3077 = 26.2\ \text{cm}.
$$

The sign of the $10.0$ is the whole problem. Ask each time: is the mercury
sitting on the gas, or hanging off it?

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  When a gas is sealed off by a valve and then released into a second tank, the
  quantity that is conserved is $n$, not $P$. Add up the moles:
  $P_1V_1/RT + P_2V_2/RT = P_fV_f/RT$, and since $T$ is common it cancels,
  leaving $P_f = (P_1V_1 + P_2V_2)/(V_1+V_2)$. For 2.00 L at
  $3.00\times10^5$ Pa joined to 3.00 L at $1.00\times10^5$ Pa, that is
  $(6.00 + 3.00)\times10^{5}\!\cdot\!\text{L} / 5.00\ \text{L} =
  1.80\times10^5$ Pa.
</Aside>

## Density, and why hot air rises

The mass of the gas is $Mn$, where $M$ is the molar mass in kg/mol, so

$$
\rho = \frac{Mn}{V} = \frac{MP}{RT}.
$$

At fixed pressure, density is inversely proportional to absolute temperature.
That line explains hot-air balloons. Heat the air in an open-bottomed envelope
and it stays at atmospheric pressure — the bottom is open, so it must — but its
density falls by the factor $T_{\text{out}}/T_{\text{in}}$.

**Example 3 — how hot must the balloon air be?** An envelope encloses
$V = 1500$ m³. Fabric, basket and passengers come to $300$ kg, not counting the
air inside. Outside air is $1.25$ kg/m³ at $288$ K. Find the lowest inside
temperature that lifts the balloon.

Buoyancy is the weight of displaced outside air, $\rho_{\text{out}} V g$. It
must support the payload plus the enclosed hot air, $(m_{\text{load}} +
\rho_{\text{in}} V) g$. The $g$ cancels:

$$
\rho_{\text{out}} V = m_{\text{load}} + \rho_{\text{in}} V
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\rho_{\text{in}} = \rho_{\text{out}} - \frac{m_{\text{load}}}{V}.
$$

Numerically $\rho_{\text{in}} = 1.25 - 300/1500 = 1.25 - 0.200 = 1.05$ kg/m³.
Same pressure and same gas inside and out means $\rho T$ is the same constant,
so

$$
T_{\text{in}} = T_{\text{out}} \frac{\rho_{\text{out}}}{\rho_{\text{in}}}
= 288 \times \frac{1.25}{1.05} = 288 \times 1.1905 = 343\ \text{K}.
$$

About 70 °C. That is why balloon burners are large.

## Equipartition: how a gas stores energy

The gas law says nothing about energy. For that you need to know how many
independent ways a molecule can hold it.

The rule is remarkable. In thermal equilibrium, every independent term in a
molecule's energy that is *quadratic* in some coordinate or velocity carries an
average of $\tfrac12 k_B T$: each $\tfrac12 m v_x^2$, each
$\tfrac12 I\omega^2$, each $\tfrac12 k x^2$ of a vibrating bond. Call the
number of such terms $f$, the degrees of freedom. Note what a degree of freedom
is — not a way of moving, but a quadratic energy term.

A single atom has three, one for motion along each of $x$, $y$, $z$. So $f = 3$
and $\overline{K} = \tfrac32 k_B T$, which is the definition of temperature you
started from. That is a consistency check, not a coincidence.

A diatomic molecule adds tumbling about the two axes perpendicular to the bond,
so $f = 5$ at room temperature. Rotation about the bond axis does not count:
the moment of inertia is minuscule and the energy to excite it is far above
$k_BT$. Heat the gas to a few thousand kelvin and the bond vibrates, adding a
kinetic and a potential term, so $f = 7$.

  <Sim id="ideal-gas-dof" />
</Figure>

Multiply through by the number of molecules. The internal energy of $n$ moles
is

$$
U = \tfrac{f}{2} n R T.
$$

Two things follow. First, $U$ depends on temperature alone, not on volume or
pressure. Second, heating at constant volume does no work, so all the heat goes
into $U$, and the molar heat capacity at constant volume is

$$
C_V = \frac{1}{n}\frac{dU}{dT} = \tfrac{f}{2} R.
$$

Heat at constant pressure instead and the gas expands, doing work
$P\,\Delta V = nR\,\Delta T$. You supply that as well as the internal energy, so

$$
C_P = C_V + R.
$$

The ratio of the two gets its own symbol and controls everything that follows:

$$
\gamma = \frac{C_P}{C_V} = \frac{f+2}{f}.
$$

Monatomic gases have $\gamma = 5/3$, diatomics at room temperature $7/5 = 1.40$,
and a molecule with six active degrees of freedom $4/3$.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Equipartition is classical, and modes freeze out when their energy spacing
  exceeds $k_BT$. Hydrogen behaves with $f = 3$ below about 100 K, $f = 5$ near
  room temperature, and $f = 7$ only above a few thousand. When a problem
  bothers to tell you the temperature or says "at room temperature", it is
  telling you which count to use.
</Aside>

For a **mixture**, add extensively. Two moles of different gases in one vessel
each contribute their own $n C_V$, so the mixture's molar $C_V$ is the
mole-weighted average. One mole of argon ($\tfrac32 R$) with one mole of
nitrogen ($\tfrac52 R$) gives $C_V = 2R$, then $C_P = 3R$, so $\gamma = 1.5$.
Pressures add the same way: each gas fills the whole volume and contributes a
**partial pressure** $p_i = n_i RT/V$, and the total is their sum. Two moles of
oxygen with three of nitrogen at $2.00\times10^5$ Pa total puts the oxygen at
$\frac{2}{5}\times 2.00\times10^5 = 8.0\times10^4$ Pa.

## Adiabatic processes

An **adiabatic** change has no heat crossing the boundary: insulated walls, or
a change too fast for heat to flow. The gas still does work, so its internal
energy pays for it.

Take a small step. With no heat term the first law reads $dU = -P\,dV$, and
$dU = nC_V\,dT$, so $nC_V\,dT = -P\,dV$. Replace $P$ with $nRT/V$ and divide
through by $nT$:

$$
C_V \frac{dT}{T} = -R\frac{dV}{V}.
$$

Both sides integrate to logarithms. Dividing by $C_V$ and using
$R/C_V = \gamma - 1$,

$$
\ln T + (\gamma-1)\ln V = \text{constant}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
T V^{\gamma-1} = \text{constant}.
$$

Substituting $T = PV/nR$ turns the same statement into $P V^{\gamma} =
\text{constant}$. Because $\gamma > 1$, an adiabat is steeper on a $PV$ diagram
than an isotherm: compressing adiabatically raises the pressure more than
compressing isothermally, because you are heating the gas as well as crowding
it.

**Example 4 — work done in an adiabatic expansion.** Two moles of a monatomic
gas expand adiabatically, cooling from $500$ K to $300$ K. How much work does
the gas do?

No heat flows, so the work comes entirely out of internal energy:
$W = -\Delta U$. For a monatomic gas $C_V = \tfrac32 R$, so

$$
W = -n C_V \Delta T = -2.00 \times \tfrac32 (8.31) \times (300 - 500).
$$

Work through it: $\tfrac32 \times 8.31 = 12.465$ J/(mol·K), times $2.00$ mol is
$24.93$ J/K, times $200$ K is $4986$ J. The gas does about $4.99$ kJ of work.
Notice you never needed the volumes, the pressures, or $\gamma$ — just the
temperature drop.

For a temperature question instead, use $TV^{\gamma-1}$. Air at $300$ K
compressed 18-fold with $\gamma = 1.40$ reaches $T_2 = 300 \times
18^{0.40}$. Since $\ln 18 = 2.890$, $0.40 \times 2.890 = 1.156$, and
$e^{1.156} = 3.178$, giving $T_2 = 953$ K. That is hot enough to ignite diesel
without a spark plug, which is the entire point of a diesel engine.

## The atmosphere: two different questions

Air gets thinner with altitude for one reason and colder for a completely
different one. Keeping them apart is worth a section.

**Pressure** falls because each layer holds up the weight of everything above
it. A slab of thickness $dz$ and density $\rho$ weighs $\rho g\,dz$ per unit
area, so $dP = -\rho g\,dz$. Substituting $\rho = MP/RT$ at uniform
temperature,

$$
\frac{dP}{P} = -\frac{Mg}{RT}\,dz
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
P(z) = P_0 e^{-Mgz/RT}.
$$

At $T = 280$ K, $M = 0.0290$ kg/mol, the exponent at $z = 3000$ m is
$-(0.0290)(9.8)(3000)/[(8.31)(280)] = -852.6/2326.8 = -0.3664$. Then
$e^{-0.3664} = 0.6932$, so $P = 1.013\times10^5 \times 0.6932 = 7.02\times10^4$
Pa. Roughly 70 percent of sea level.

**Temperature** falls for an adiabatic reason. Take a parcel of air rising
slowly enough to match the surrounding pressure but too fast to exchange heat.
It expands as the pressure drops, does work, and cools. Per mole, with no heat
in, $C_P\,dT = V\,dP$; the constant-pressure capacity appears because you are
tracking the parcel at ambient pressure. Insert $V = RT/P$ and the hydrostatic
$dP = -\rho g\,dz$ with $\rho = MP/RT$:

$$
C_P\,dT = \frac{RT}{P}\left(-\frac{MPg}{RT}\right)dz = -Mg\,dz,
$$

so the **dry adiabatic lapse rate** is

$$
-\frac{dT}{dz} = \frac{Mg}{C_P}.
$$

Everything cancelled — no temperature, no pressure, just a constant. With
$C_P = \tfrac72 R = 3.5 \times 8.31 = 29.085$ J/(mol·K),

$$
\frac{Mg}{C_P} = \frac{0.0290 \times 9.8}{29.085} = \frac{0.2842}{29.085}
= 9.771\times10^{-3}\ \text{K/m} = 9.77\ \text{K/km}.
$$

Climb a kilometre of mountain and expect about ten degrees colder. That number
comes out of a gas law and a force balance, with no meteorology at all.

**Example 5 — the heated half of an insulated cylinder.** This one is harder.
An insulated cylinder of total volume $2V_0$ is split into equal halves by a
frictionless insulating piston. Each half holds $1.00$ mol of monatomic gas at
$300$ K. A heater in the left half runs slowly until the left gas fills
$1.50V_0$. Find the final temperature on the right.

Look at the right-hand gas only. It is insulated from the walls and from the
left gas by the piston, and it is compressed slowly. So its process is
adiabatic, and $TV^{\gamma-1}$ is constant, whatever the heater is doing. Its
volume goes from $V_0$ to $2V_0 - 1.50V_0 = 0.500V_0$, a compression by a
factor of 2. With $\gamma = 5/3$, so $\gamma - 1 = 2/3$,

$$
T_R = 300 \times 2^{2/3}.
$$

Since $2^{2/3} = e^{0.6667 \times 0.6931} = e^{0.4621} = 1.5874$, the answer is
$T_R = 300 \times 1.5874 = 476$ K. The left gas is hotter still, but the
question did not ask, and you did not need it.

## Recognising which tool a problem wants

Almost every problem in this unit announces itself in one line.

If the problem gives you a gas in one state and asks about another state of the
*same* gas, reach for $P_1V_1/T_1 = P_2V_2/T_2$ and cross out whatever is held
fixed. You will not need $R$.

If it asks for moles, molecules, mass, density or molar mass, you need the full
$PV = nRT$ or $PV = Nk_BT$, and probably $\rho = MP/RT$. Anything mentioning
buoyancy or a balloon is a density problem wearing a costume.

If the words *insulated*, *adiabatic*, *no heat exchanged*, or *compressed
rapidly* appear, use $TV^{\gamma-1}$ or $PV^{\gamma}$, and get $\gamma$ by
counting degrees of freedom. If instead the problem says *rigid vessel* or
*constant pressure* and asks for heat, use $nC_V\Delta T$ or $nC_P\Delta T$ and
nothing else.

If a piston, mercury column or manometer appears, stop and do a force balance
or a liquid-column tally *before* touching the gas law. That step is where the
marks are lost.

And if the problem is about the atmosphere, decide first which question it is
asking. Pressure against height is the exponential barometric formula at fixed
temperature. Temperature against height is the lapse rate $Mg/C_P$. They use
the same hydrostatic equation and are otherwise unrelated.

The next lesson takes $\gamma$, the adiabat and the first law and strings them
into closed loops on a $PV$ diagram, which is how you get work out of heat —
and how much you are forbidden from getting. That is
[heat engines and the second law](/guide/heat-engines).
