## Where a bubble gets its colour

A soap bubble is colourless stuff. Water, a little detergent, a wall maybe two
hundred nanometres thick. Nothing in it is red or green. Yet hold one up in
white light and it is banded with colour, and the bands move as the film drains
and thins. An oil slick on a wet road does the same thing, and so does the
purple sheen on a camera lens.

The [double slit](/guide/diffraction) gave you interference between two beams
that left the same source and travelled different distances. A thin film does
exactly that, with one difference worth the whole lesson: here the two beams
are made by the film itself. Light hits the top surface, and part of it bounces
straight back. The rest goes in, crosses the film, bounces off the bottom
surface, and comes back out travelling alongside the first beam. Two waves,
same source, slightly different paths. They interfere. Which colours survive
depends on the thickness, and that is why the colour changes as the film drains.

## The extra path, measured in wavelengths

Take the light coming in straight down, at normal incidence, on a film of
thickness $t$ and refractive index $n$. Call the wavelength in vacuum $\lambda$.

The second beam crosses the film twice, down and back, so it travels an extra
distance $2t$. What matters for interference is not the distance but how many
wavelengths fit into it, and inside the film the wavelength is not $\lambda$.
The frequency of light cannot change when it enters glass — the atoms at the
surface are driven at the frequency they are hit with — but the speed drops to
$v = c/n$. Since $\lambda_n = v/f$ and $\lambda = c/f$,

$$\lambda_n = \frac{\lambda}{n}$$

Light of 600 nm in vacuum is 400 nm long inside a film of index 1.50. So the
number of wavelengths crammed into that extra $2t$ is

$$N = \frac{2t}{\lambda_n} = \frac{2nt}{\lambda}$$

The product $2nt$ is called the **optical path difference**: the geometric path
multiplied by the index, which converts it into "distance measured in vacuum
wavelengths". Almost every mistake in this topic is dropping the $n$ from that
product, or putting it in twice.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Path difference is $2t$. Optical path difference is $2nt$. You always compare
  $2nt$ against the vacuum wavelength $\lambda$, never against $\lambda/n$.
</Aside>

## The flip

If that were the whole story, a film would be bright whenever $2nt$ was a whole
number of wavelengths. It is not the whole story, and the reason is something
you have already met on a rope.

Send a pulse down a rope tied to a wall. The pulse comes back upside down. Send
it down a rope that is tied to a much lighter string, and it comes back the
right way up. The reflection inverts when the wave is meeting something harder
to move than what it came from.

Light does the same at a boundary, and "harder to move" means higher refractive
index. **A reflection off a medium of higher index inverts the wave. A
reflection off a medium of lower index does not.** An inversion is a phase shift
of half a cycle, so it is worth exactly $\lambda/2$ of extra path. People call
it the half-wave loss; this lesson will call it a flip.

Now there are two reflections to check, and each one either flips or it does
not. Write the three indices down in order — above the film, the film, below
the film — and compare across each boundary.

If the two reflections both flip, or neither does, the flips cancel and the
only thing left is the path. Constructive reflection then needs a whole number
of wavelengths:

$$2nt = m\lambda \qquad (\text{bright, even number of flips})$$

with $m = 0, 1, 2, \dots$. If exactly one of them flips, half a cycle is added
on top of the path, and the two conditions trade places:

$$2nt = \left(m + \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\lambda \qquad (\text{bright, one flip})$$

Dark is whichever one is left over. There is no formula to memorise beyond
these two lines, and memorising which is which for soap will hurt you the first
time a problem puts the film on glass instead of air. Count the flips every
time.

Soap in air is air-soap-air: $1.00 \to 1.33$ flips, $1.33 \to 1.00$ does not.
One flip. Oil on water is $1.00 \to 1.46 \to 1.33$: still one flip. A magnesium
fluoride coating on glass is $1.00 \to 1.38 \to 1.52$, rising all the way: two
flips.

## Two films you can do in your head

**Example 1. The thinnest bright soap film.** A soap film of index 1.33 hangs
in air. Green light of 550 nm hits it head on. What is the smallest nonzero
thickness that reflects it most strongly?

Air-soap-air is one flip, so bright means $2nt = (m + \frac12)\lambda$. The
smallest nonzero thickness comes from the smallest order, $m = 0$:

$$2(1.33)t = \tfrac{1}{2}(550\text{ nm}) = 275\text{ nm}$$

so $t = 275/2.66 = 103.4$ nm. Round to 103 nm. Notice the shape of the answer:
$t = \lambda/(4n)$. A quarter of the in-film wavelength, because the light
crosses it twice and a half-wave round trip is what the single flip needs.

**Example 2. The coating on a camera lens.** Glass of index 1.52 is coated with
magnesium fluoride, index 1.38, to kill the reflection at 550 nm. Find the
minimum coating thickness.

Here the indices rise at both surfaces, so two flips, and the plain condition
$2nt = m\lambda$ is the *bright* one. Dark is therefore
$2nt = (m + \frac12)\lambda$, and the minimum thickness is again the quarter
wave:

$$t = \frac{\lambda}{4n} = \frac{550\text{ nm}}{4(1.38)} = 99.6\text{ nm}$$

Same arithmetic, opposite meaning. That is the whole reason to count flips
rather than memorise.

A quarter-wave coating always sets the two reflected waves half a cycle apart,
but they only cancel *completely* if they have the same amplitude. At normal
incidence the reflected amplitude ratio at a boundary is
$r = (n_1 - n_2)/(n_1 + n_2)$. Setting the size of $r$ equal at both surfaces,
$(1 - n_c)/(1 + n_c) = (n_c - n_g)/(n_c + n_g)$, and multiplying out gives
$n_c = \sqrt{n_g}$. For $n_g = 1.52$ the ideal coating index is 1.23. No cheap
durable solid has that index, which is why real coatings use 1.38 and leave a
faint purple reflection behind.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  The flip count depends on what is on *both* sides of the film, not just the
  film. An oil film on water and the same oil film on glass obey opposite
  conditions. A rule like "soap films are bright at odd quarter waves" is true
  and will still cost you the problem the moment the film is sitting on
  something. Recount the flips from the three indices every single time.
</Aside>

## White light: several colours at once

Shine white light on a film of fixed thickness and every wavelength gets the
same $2nt$ but a different number of wavelengths out of it. Some colours land
on the bright condition, some on the dark one, most in between. The reflected
light is what is left.

This is the colour sequence you see in a draining film, running backwards.</>}>

</Figure>

At the very thin end something clean happens. Let $t \to 0$ for a soap film in
air. Then $2nt \to 0$, the path contributes nothing, and the single flip is all
that is left. The two beams are half a cycle apart for *every* wavelength at
once, so the whole spectrum cancels and the film looks black. That black band at
the top of a draining film is the last thing you see before it bursts.

The most common hard problem gives you two wavelengths that are both missing,
or both enhanced, with none in between, and asks for the thickness. Two
conditions, two unknowns: the thickness and the order.

**Example 3. Oil on water, two colours missing.** An oil film of index 1.50
floats on water, air above. In reflection, 500 nm and 600 nm are missing and
nothing between them is. Find $t$.

Air-oil-water is $1.00 \to 1.50 \to 1.33$: one flip. So dark is
$2nt = m\lambda$. The longer wavelength needs fewer wavelengths to fill the same
path, so it takes the smaller order. "No missing wavelength between them" means
the orders are consecutive. Call them $m$ for 600 nm and $m+1$ for 500 nm:

$$2nt = m(600) = (m+1)(500)$$

Expand: $600m = 500m + 500$, so $100m = 500$ and $m = 5$. Then
$2nt = 5(600) = 3000$ nm, and

$$t = \frac{3000\text{ nm}}{2(1.50)} = 1000\text{ nm}$$

Check it: $2nt/\lambda$ is 5 at 600 nm and 6 at 500 nm, both whole numbers, and
between them $3000/5.5 = 545$ nm is not a dark wavelength. Good.

The same setup with a wavelength allowed in between just changes the counting.
If 500 nm and 700 nm are missing with exactly one other dark wavelength between
them, the orders differ by 2: $m(700) = (m+2)(500)$ gives $m = 5$, so
$2nt = 3500$ nm. For oil of index 1.46 that is $t = 3500/2.92 = 1199$ nm.

## Wedges and rings

Nothing above assumed the thickness was the same everywhere. If $t$ varies
slowly across the film, apply the condition point by point: each place where
$2nt$ hits the right value is a fringe, and the fringes map out the shape of the
gap.

Two flat plates touching at one edge and held apart at the other make an air
wedge, $n = 1$. Air between glass is one flip (no flip on the way in at
glass-air, a flip at air-glass below), so dark means $2t = m\lambda$. Adjacent
dark fringes differ by one order, so the gap changes by $\lambda/2$ between
them. If the plates are separated by a wire of diameter $50.0\ \mu$m, the number
of dark fringes from the contact line to the wire is

$$\frac{2t_{\max}}{\lambda} = \frac{2(50.0\times10^{-6})}{589\times10^{-9}} = 170$$

And if you know the fringe *spacing* along the plate, you know the wedge angle:
the gap climbs by $\lambda/2$ over one spacing, so
$\theta \approx \lambda/(2 \Delta x)$ for a small angle in air, or
$\lambda/(2n\Delta x)$ if the wedge is filled.

Curve one surface instead and the fringes become circles. A plano-convex lens
resting on a flat plate leaves a gap that, for $r \ll R$, has thickness
$t = r^2/(2R)$, where $R$ is the radius of curvature. These are Newton's rings.

**Example 4. The tenth dark ring.** A lens of $R = 2.00$ m sits on a flat plate,
air in the gap, lit by 589 nm light. Find the radius of the tenth dark ring,
with the dark centre counted as $m = 0$.

One flip, so dark is $2t = m\lambda$. Substituting $t = r^2/(2R)$ gives
$r^2/R = m\lambda$, so $r = \sqrt{m\lambda R}$:

$$r = \sqrt{10 \times (589\times10^{-9}\text{ m}) \times 2.00\text{ m}} = \sqrt{1.178\times10^{-5}} = 3.43\times10^{-3}\text{ m}$$

That is 3.43 mm. Two things fall out of $r = \sqrt{m\lambda R}$ for free. The
rings crowd together as you go out, because $r$ grows like $\sqrt{m}$. And if a
liquid of index $n$ fills the gap, replace $\lambda$ by $\lambda/n$, so every
ring shrinks by $\sqrt{n}$ — a 3.00 mm ring in air becomes
$3.00/\sqrt{1.33} = 2.60$ mm under water.

The centre is only dark if exactly one of the two reflections flips there. A
lens of index 1.52 on a plate of index 1.75 with liquid of index 1.62 between
them goes $1.52 \to 1.62 \to 1.75$: two flips, and the centre is bright instead.
Then bright is $2nt = m\lambda$, the centre is $m = 0$, and the first bright
ring away from it is $m = 1$, at $r = \sqrt{\lambda R/n}$.

## Angles, and looking through instead of at

At an angle the geometry changes slightly. The beam inside the film travels
along a slant, and the reflected beam that never entered gets a head start. Work
the geometry through and the optical path difference becomes

$$\Delta = 2nt\cos\theta_t$$

where $\theta_t$ is the refracted angle *inside* the film, from
$\sin\theta_i = n\sin\theta_t$. The cosine, not the sine, and the angle inside,
not the angle you aimed at. At normal incidence $\cos\theta_t = 1$ and you
recover $2nt$. Tilting always shrinks $\Delta$, so the colour of a film shifts
toward the blue as you tip it — which is why a bubble's bands crawl when you
move your head.

Finally, whatever is not reflected is transmitted. The transmitted beams pick up
flips at different places, and the upshot is simple: **the transmitted condition
is always the opposite of the reflected one.** A film that looks dark in
reflection is at peak brightness looking through it. For a soap film in air,
reflection is dark at $2nt = m\lambda$, so transmission is maximal there:
$t = \lambda/(2n)$ at the smallest nonzero thickness, 188 nm for 500 nm light.

## How to spot one of these

Reach for thin-film interference when a problem mentions a film, coating,
slick, bubble, gap, or wedge whose thickness is comparable to a wavelength —
tens to a couple of thousand nanometres — and then asks about colour, missing
wavelengths, brightness, or darkness in reflected or transmitted light. Three
indices in a stack is the giveaway.

Then run the same four steps every time. One: write the three indices in order
and count the flips, which fixes whether $m\lambda$ or $(m+\frac12)\lambda$ is
the bright side. Two: write the optical path as $2nt$, or $2nt\cos\theta_t$ off
normal, with $n$ the index of the *film*. Three: if the geometry varies, put in
the thickness law — $t = r^2/(2R)$ for rings, $t = x\theta$ for a wedge. Four:
if two wavelengths are given, set the two conditions equal and solve for the
order first, then the thickness. The order is always an integer, and if your
algebra gives a non-integer you have the flip count or the ordering of the
wavelengths backwards.
