## Momentum is what force changes over time

Newton's second law is more honestly written as a statement about momentum:

$$\vec F_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\vec p}{dt}, \qquad \vec p = m\vec v$$

The familiar $F = ma$ is the special case where the mass is constant. Keep the
momentum form and rockets, falling chains, and conveyor belts loading sand all
stay in reach.

Momentum is a vector, so it has one equation per axis. Its units,
$\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$, are the same as those of impulse — no coincidence.

## Impulse is the time integral of force

Multiply through by $dt$ and integrate:

$$\vec J = \int \vec F\, dt = \Delta \vec p$$

That is the impulse-momentum theorem, and it is the time-domain twin of the
work-energy theorem. Where the work-energy theorem integrates force over
distance and gives a scalar change in $K$, this integrates force over time and
gives a vector change in $\vec p$. Problems that mention a duration, a contact
time, or an average force during an impact belong to this one.

On a graph of force versus time, the impulse is the **area under the curve**.
Real collision forces spike into a sharp hump lasting milliseconds; you rarely
know the shape, but the area is what you need, and an average force defined by
$\bar F \Delta t = \Delta p$ captures it exactly.

## Stretch the time and the force drops

For a fixed change in momentum, $\bar F$ and $\Delta t$ trade off inversely:

$$\bar F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}$$

A car crash brings you from $30\text{ m/s}$ to rest whether or not the car has a
crumple zone. What the crumple zone changes is the time. Stopping in
$0.01\text{ s}$ against a rigid dashboard means a hundred times the force of
stopping in $1\text{ s}$ against an inflating airbag. The same logic covers
bending your knees on landing, catching an egg by drawing your hand back, and
padding on a gymnastics floor. None of them reduce the momentum you must shed;
all of them lengthen the shedding.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Impulse is a vector, and a reversal counts double. A ball hitting a wall at
  $5\text{ m/s}$ and rebounding at $5\text{ m/s}$ has $|\Delta p| = 2mv$, not
  zero and not $mv$. Choose a positive direction and write signed components
  before doing any arithmetic.
</Aside>

## Why momentum survives collisions

Internal forces between two colliding bodies are equal and opposite at every
instant, so they contribute equal and opposite impulses and cancel from the
total. Only external forces can change a system's momentum. During a short
impact, gravity and friction have almost no time to act, which is why total
momentum is conserved through a collision even when the system is not truly
isolated.
