## Vectors carry the physics

Olympiad physics is done in components. Any vector $\vec A$ resolves as
$A_x = A\cos\theta,\ A_y = A\sin\theta$, and two products do all the work:

$$\vec A \cdot \vec B = AB\cos\theta \qquad |\vec A \times \vec B| = AB\sin\theta$$

- Dot product: projections, work, checking perpendicularity
- Cross product: torque, angular momentum, magnetic force
- $\vec A \cdot \vec B = 0$ means perpendicular; $\vec A \times \vec B = \vec 0$ means parallel

## The calculus you actually need

F=ma officially avoids calculus but rewards it constantly. You need
derivatives of polynomials and trig, the chain rule, and basic integrals. The
single most-used trick is small-angle expansion:

$$\sin\theta \approx \theta, \qquad \cos\theta \approx 1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2}, \qquad (1+x)^n \approx 1 + nx$$

<Callout label="Pro tip">
  When a problem says "small oscillations" or "slightly displaced," it is
  telling you to Taylor-expand. Learn to hear that dog whistle.
</Callout>

## Solve it here

<Problem
  id="np-vec-1"
  number={1}
  title="Perpendicular push"
  difficulty="Easy"
  answer={12}
  unit="J"
>
  A force $\vec F = (3, 4)$ N acts on an object that moves along
  $\vec d = (4, 0)$ m. How much work does the force do, in joules?

  <Hint>
    Work is the dot product $W = \vec F \cdot \vec d$.
  </Hint>
  <Solution>
    $$W = F_x d_x + F_y d_y = (3)(4) + (4)(0) = 12 \text{ J}$$

    Only the component of force along the motion does work.
  </Solution>
</Problem>
