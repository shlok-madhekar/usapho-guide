## A force that depends on velocity

The electric force on a charge points along the field. The magnetic force does
not point along anything obvious — it is perpendicular to both the velocity and
the field:

$$\vec{F} = q\vec{v} \times \vec{B}$$

Its magnitude is $qvB\sin\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec{v}$
and $\vec{B}$. A charge sitting still feels nothing. A charge moving parallel
to the field feels nothing. The force is largest when the motion is
perpendicular to the field.

## No work, ever

Because $\vec{F}$ is perpendicular to $\vec{v}$, the power delivered is

$$P = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{v} = q(\vec{v}\times\vec{B})\cdot\vec{v} = 0$$

A magnetic field cannot change a particle's speed or kinetic energy. It can
only bend the trajectory. This is a strong constraint and a good check: if a
solution has a magnetic field speeding a particle up, something is wrong.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  "Magnetic fields do no work" is a statement about point charges, not about
  magnets. A magnet lifting a paperclip clearly transfers energy. The
  resolution is that the energy comes from the internal structure of the
  current loops and dipoles involved, not from the $q\vec{v}\times\vec{B}$ term
  on a free charge. Use the no-work rule for particle-in-a-field problems and
  do not try to stretch it into an argument about magnets and induction.
</Aside>

## Circular motion and the cyclotron radius

Fire a charge perpendicular to a uniform field. The force has constant
magnitude and stays perpendicular to the velocity, which is exactly the
condition for uniform circular motion. Set the magnetic force equal to the
centripetal requirement:

$$qvB = \frac{mv^2}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \frac{mv}{qB} = \frac{p}{qB}$$

The radius is proportional to momentum, which is why bending a beam through a
known field and measuring the curvature is how detectors identify particles.

The period is more striking:

$$T = \frac{2\pi m}{qB}$$

Speed has dropped out. Faster particles trace larger circles in exactly the
same time, which is what lets a cyclotron drive every orbit with a single fixed
frequency.

If the velocity has a component along $\vec{B}$, that component is untouched.
The result is a helix: circular in the plane perpendicular to the field,
uniform along it.

## Force on a wire

A wire is a stream of moving charges. Summing $q\vec{v}\times\vec{B}$ over the
carriers in a length $\vec{L}$ gives

$$\vec{F} = I\vec{L}\times\vec{B}$$

For a curved wire, integrate $I\,d\vec{\ell}\times\vec{B}$. In a uniform field
the integral of $d\vec{\ell}$ depends only on the endpoints, so any wire shape
feels the same force as the straight segment joining its ends — and a closed
loop in a uniform field feels zero net force, though generally a nonzero
torque.
