## The procedure

Newton's second law is easy. Applying it to the wrong set of forces is the
mistake, and a free body diagram is the discipline that prevents it. Four
steps, in order:

1. Choose one body and draw it alone.
2. Draw every force acting **on** it. Never forces it exerts on something else.
3. Choose axes, usually with one along the acceleration.
4. Write $\sum F = ma$ per axis.

$$\sum F_x = ma_x, \qquad \sum F_y = ma_y$$

## The forces you will meet

Weight is $mg$, always straight down. The normal force is perpendicular to the
contact surface and is whatever magnitude prevents interpenetration, which is
frequently not $mg$. Tension is uniform along a massless rope and changes at
any pulley with mass.

Friction deserves its own note, because it comes in two kinds with different
grammar:

$$f_s \le \mu_s N, \qquad f_k = \mu_k N$$

Kinetic friction is an equation. Static friction is an inequality: it takes
whatever value is needed to prevent sliding, up to a ceiling.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Writing $f = \mu_s N$ before checking whether the object is actually on the
  verge of slipping is the most common error on this topic. If the object is
  sitting still and nothing says "about to slip", static friction is an
  unknown to solve for, not a number to plug in.
</Aside>

## Systems and constraints

When two bodies are connected, they share a constraint: a rope over a pulley
means equal speeds, blocks in contact mean equal accelerations. Writing the
second law for each body separately and then adding the equations usually
eliminates the internal force you did not want to know anyway.
