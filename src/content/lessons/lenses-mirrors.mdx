Hold a magnifying glass over a page and the letters swell. Move it further out and
they suddenly flip upside down. Nothing about the glass changed. Only the distance
did.

That flip is the whole subject. A lens does one thing: it takes the rays spraying
out from a point on an object and bends them so they meet again at some other
point. A small move of the object can throw that meeting point from 30 cm behind
the lens out to infinity and then around to the wrong side entirely. What follows
is how to predict it, draw it, and chain several lenses and mirrors together
without losing the signs.

## Why the rays meet at all

You already have the one law this rests on. In [reflection and
refraction](/guide/reflection-refraction) you saw that a ray crossing into a
denser medium bends toward the normal by $n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2$, and
that a mirror sends a ray back at the angle it arrived.

Now picture a point source sitting on the axis of a piece of glass whose faces are
curved. A ray that leaves along the axis hits both faces head-on and goes straight
through. A ray that leaves at a steeper angle hits the faces further from the
centre, where they are more slanted, so it gets bent more. That is the trick: the
further off-axis the ray strikes, the harder the glass turns it. If the curvature
is chosen well, the extra bending exactly compensates for the extra angle, and
every ray from that one source point arrives at one common point on the far side.

The compensation is only exact for rays that stay close to the axis, where
$\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta \approx \theta$. Those are called *paraxial* rays,
and every formula below is a paraxial formula. Rays far off-axis miss the meeting
point slightly, a blur called spherical aberration, which this lesson ignores.

Call the distance from the lens to the object $d_o$, and the distance to the point
where the rays reconverge $d_i$. Two facts pin down the relation between them.
First, an object infinitely far away sends in rays that are parallel, and those
converge at a special distance called the *focal length* $f$. Second, light does
not care which way it travels: if an object at $d_o$ images at $d_i$, then an
object at $d_i$ images at $d_o$. A relation symmetric in $d_o$ and $d_i$ that
reduces to $d_i = f$ when $d_o \to \infty$ is

$$\frac{1}{d_o} + \frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{f}$$

and that is the thin lens equation. The derivation from Snell's law comes later in
this lesson, when you build it out of single curved surfaces. In words: the
reciprocals add. If the object is far, $1/d_o$ is nearly nothing and $d_i \approx
f$. As the object walks in toward the focal point, $1/d_o$ grows until it eats the
whole of $1/f$, and $1/d_i$ hits zero — the image runs off to infinity. Push
closer still and $1/d_i$ goes negative.

## What a negative distance means

A negative $d_i$ is not an error. It means the rays leaving the lens are still
spreading apart, and only their backward extensions meet. Your eye judges a bundle
only by where it seems to come from, so it sees an image hanging on the *same* side
as the object. That is a **virtual** image: you can look at it, you cannot catch it
on a screen. A positive $d_i$ gives a **real** image, made of light that actually
goes there, and a screen at that spot lights up.

Fix these conventions now and write them at the top of every problem:

- $d_o > 0$ for an object in front of the element, where light actually comes from.
- $d_i > 0$ for a real image: behind a lens, in front of a mirror.
- $f > 0$ for a converging lens or a concave mirror; $f < 0$ for a diverging lens
  or a convex mirror.
- $R > 0$ when the centre of curvature lies on the side the light leaves toward.

Size comes from the ray that goes straight through the middle of the lens. It
arrives at height $h_o$ at distance $d_o$ and leaves at height $h_i$ at distance
$d_i$ on the far side, so similar triangles give $h_i/h_o = -d_i/d_o$. That ratio
is the **magnification**:

$$m = \frac{h_i}{h_o} = -\frac{d_i}{d_o}$$

The minus sign is the flip. A real image from a single converging lens always has
$d_i > 0$ and $d_o > 0$, so $m < 0$, so it is upside down. Every time.

## Two examples, plug-in then set-up

**A candle and a lens.** A converging lens has $f = 20.0$ cm and a candle stands
$60.0$ cm in front of it. Where is the image, and how big?

$$\frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{20.0} - \frac{1}{60.0} = \frac{3 - 1}{60.0} = \frac{2}{60.0}$$

so $d_i = 30.0$ cm, positive, so it is real and $30.0$ cm behind the lens. The
magnification is $m = -30.0/60.0 = -0.500$, so the flame appears inverted at half
size. If the candle is $5.0$ cm tall the image is $2.5$ cm tall.

**How close can a projector get?** A converging lens with $f = 12.0$ cm throws a
real image on a screen. What is the smallest possible object-to-screen distance?

Nothing is given to plug in, so set it up. The total distance is $D = d_o + d_i$.
Solve the lens equation for $d_i$:

$$d_i = \frac{d_o f}{d_o - f}, \qquad D = d_o + \frac{d_o f}{d_o - f} = \frac{d_o^2}{d_o - f}$$

Minimise $D$ by setting the derivative to zero. With $u = d_o$,

$$\frac{dD}{du} = \frac{2u(u - f) - u^2}{(u - f)^2} = \frac{u^2 - 2uf}{(u-f)^2} = 0$$

which needs $u = 2f$. Then $d_o = 24.0$ cm, $d_i = (24.0)(12.0)/(12.0) = 24.0$ cm,
and $D = 48.0$ cm, which is $4f$. Object and image sit symmetrically at $2f$ and
the image is the same size as the object.

The same rearrangement explains a lab staple. Writing the total as
$d_o^2 - D d_o + Df = 0$ shows that if $D > 4f$ there are *two* lens positions
giving a sharp image on one screen, separated by $d = \sqrt{D^2 - 4Df}$. Invert
that for the displacement method: $f = (D^2 - d^2)/(4D)$, so $D = 100$ cm with
$d = 40.0$ cm gives $f = 8400/400 = 21.0$ cm.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Measuring $d_o$ from the focal point instead of from the lens. The lens equation
  wants distances from the lens. If a problem hands you the distance $x$ from the
  object to the front focus, either add $f$ first, or switch to Newton's form
  $x x' = f^2$, where $x'$ is measured from the back focus. For $f = 15.0$ cm and
  $x = 5.00$ cm: $x' = 225/5.00 = 45.0$ cm from the back focus, which is
  $45.0 + 15.0 = 60.0$ cm from the lens. Mixing the two conventions in one line is
  the most common way to lose a problem here.
</Aside>

## Mirrors run on the same equation

A concave mirror takes parallel rays and sends them through a point too, so it has
a focal length and obeys the same relation. Its focal length is not independent of
its shape: a ray parallel to the axis at small height $h$ strikes a sphere of
radius $R$ at an angle $h/R$ to the local normal, reflects through twice that
angle, and therefore crosses the axis a distance $h/(2h/R) = R/2$ from the mirror.
So

$$f = \frac{R}{2}, \qquad \frac{1}{d_o} + \frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{2}{R}$$

A shaving mirror ground to $R = 40.0$ cm has $f = 20.0$ cm. A convex mirror has its
centre of curvature behind it, so $R$ and $f$ are negative and it can only make
small upright virtual images, which is what a car's mirror does. For
$R = -4.00$ m, $f = -2.00$ m, and a truck at $12.0$ m gives
$1/d_i = -1/2.00 - 1/12.0 = -7/12.0$, so $d_i = -1.71$ m and
$m = -(-1.71)/12.0 = +0.143$.

  <Sim id="lenses-mirrors-mirror-lab" />
</Figure>

Rates come out of the same equation by differentiating. Hold $f$ fixed and
differentiate $1/d_o + 1/d_i = 1/f$ with respect to time:

$$
-\frac{1}{d_o^2}\frac{d d_o}{dt} - \frac{1}{d_i^2}\frac{d d_i}{dt} = 0
\;\Rightarrow\; \frac{d d_i}{dt} = -\left(\frac{d_i}{d_o}\right)^{2}\frac{d d_o}{dt} = -m^2 \frac{d d_o}{dt}
$$

For a concave mirror with $f = 10.0$ cm and an object at $30.0$ cm, $d_i = 15.0$ cm
and $m = -0.500$, so an object approaching at $2.00$ cm/s drives the image at
$(0.500)^2 (2.00) = 0.500$ cm/s. Image speed is not object speed, and the factor is
the square of the magnification.

## Where the focal length comes from

Everything above took $f$ as given. To compute it, go back to one curved surface
separating index $n_1$ from index $n_2$. Applying Snell's law in the paraxial
approximation to a ray striking at height $h$ on a surface of radius $R$ gives

$$\frac{n_1}{d_o} + \frac{n_2}{d_i} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R}$$

Note that the indices ride along with their own side's distance, and that the right
side plays the role of $1/f$. Take a bug $60.0$ cm in front of a fish tank wall of
radius $R = +10.0$ cm bulging toward the air, with water at $n = 1.33$ behind it:

$$\frac{1.00}{60.0} + \frac{1.33}{d_i} = \frac{0.33}{10.0} = 0.0330$$

so $1.33/d_i = 0.0330 - 0.0167 = 0.0163$ and $d_i = 81.4$ cm inside the water.

  <Sim id="lenses-mirrors-surface" />
</Figure>

A thin lens is two such surfaces back to back. Apply the surface equation at the
front face, feed its image in as the object for the back face, let the glass
thickness go to zero, and the intermediate distance cancels, leaving the
**lensmaker's equation**:

$$\frac{1}{f} = \left(\frac{n_{\text{lens}}}{n_{\text{medium}}} - 1\right)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right)$$

For a biconvex lens of $n = 1.50$ in air with $R_1 = +20.0$ cm and
$R_2 = -30.0$ cm: $1/f = (0.500)(1/20.0 + 1/30.0) = (0.500)(0.0833) = 0.0417$, so
$f = 24.0$ cm. A plano-convex lens has one infinite radius, so one term drops: with
$n = 1.60$ and $R = 10.0$ cm, $1/f = (0.600)(1/10.0)$ and $f = 16.7$ cm.

The medium term matters. Dunk a $n = 1.50$ lens of focal length $20.0$ cm in water.
The bracket falls from $0.500$ to $1.50/1.33 - 1 = 0.128$, so $f$ grows by the
factor $0.500/0.128 = 3.91$, to $78.2$ cm. A glass lens in water is a feeble lens.

**A pin and a glass ball.** A solid glass sphere, $R = 5.00$ cm and $n = 1.50$,
sits in air with a pin $20.0$ cm in front. Where is the final image? Do the
surfaces one at a time. Front surface, $R = +5.00$ cm:

$$\frac{1.00}{20.0} + \frac{1.50}{d_i} = \frac{0.50}{5.00} = 0.100$$

giving $1.50/d_i = 0.0500$ and $d_i = 30.0$ cm past the front surface. The sphere is
only $10.0$ cm thick, so those rays are still converging when they reach the back
face and would have met $20.0$ cm beyond it. An object the light has not reached yet
is a **virtual object**, and it goes in with a negative distance: $d_o = -20.0$ cm.
At the back surface light runs glass to air, so $n_1 = 1.50$, $n_2 = 1.00$, and the
centre of curvature is now on the incoming side, so $R = -5.00$ cm:

$$\frac{1.50}{-20.0} + \frac{1.00}{d_i} = \frac{1.00 - 1.50}{-5.00} = 0.100$$

so $1/d_i = 0.100 + 0.0750 = 0.175$ and $d_i = 5.71$ cm beyond the back of the
sphere.

## Chains: one element at a time

That is the general method and it never changes. **The image from element one is
the object for element two.** Measure the new object distance from element two, let
it go negative when element one would have imaged behind element two, and multiply
the magnifications: $m_{\text{total}} = m_1 m_2$.

Two converging lenses, $f_1 = 10.0$ cm and $f_2 = 15.0$ cm, $40.0$ cm apart, object
$15.0$ cm in front of the first. Lens one:
$1/d_i = 1/10.0 - 1/15.0 = 1/30.0$, so $d_i = 30.0$ cm. That image is $30.0$ cm past
lens one, hence $40.0 - 30.0 = 10.0$ cm in front of lens two, a perfectly ordinary
real object. Lens two: $1/d_i = 1/15.0 - 1/10.0 = -1/30.0$, so $d_i = -30.0$ cm, a
virtual image $30.0$ cm in front of lens two.

When the two lenses are close together you can collapse them into one effective
focal length. Running the same chain symbolically for a separation $d$ gives

$$\frac{1}{F} = \frac{1}{f_1} + \frac{1}{f_2} - \frac{d}{f_1 f_2}$$

For $f_1 = 10.0$ cm, $f_2 = 20.0$ cm, $d = 5.00$ cm:
$1/F = 0.100 + 0.0500 - 0.0250 = 0.125$, so $F = 8.00$ cm. At $d = 0$ this is the
powers adding, as promised.

A mirror in the chain works the same way, with the light turned around. A lens
with its back face silvered acts as one mirror whose power is the mirror's plus
*twice* the lens's, because the light crosses the glass going in and again coming
out: $1/F = 2/f_{\text{lens}} + 1/f_{\text{mirror}}$. For a
plano-convex lens of $n = 1.50$ and $R = 20.0$ cm, the lensmaker's equation gives
$f_{\text{lens}} = 40.0$ cm, and the silvered flat face is a plane mirror with
$1/f_{\text{mirror}} = 0$. So $1/F = 2/40.0 = 0.0500$ and $F = 20.0$ cm.

## Instruments: what magnification means there

For a telescope, image size on a screen is meaningless. What matters is the
**angular** magnification: the angle the image subtends at your eye divided by the
angle the object subtended without the instrument.

With a magnifier, the best your unaided eye can do is bring the object to its near
point $N = 25.0$ cm, giving angle $h/N$. Put the object at the focal point instead
and the image goes to infinity, subtending $h/f$. So $M = N/f$, and a $f = 5.00$ cm
loupe is a $5.0\times$ magnifier.

A telescope in normal adjustment has the two focal points coincident, so a distant
object subtending $\theta$ makes an intermediate image of height $f_o\theta$, which
the eyepiece presents at angle $f_o\theta/f_e$. So $M = f_o/f_e$: a $100$ cm
objective with a $2.50$ cm eyepiece gives $40.0\times$. A Galilean telescope uses a
negative eyepiece and the same formula in magnitude, $50.0/10.0 = 5.0$.

Rack the eyepiece out so the final image lands at the near point instead and the
eyepiece object distance changes. With $f_e = 6.00$ cm and $d_i = -25.0$ cm:
$1/d_o = 1/6.00 + 1/25.0 = 0.2067$, so $d_o = 4.839$ cm. The angular magnification
is $f_o/d_o = 90.0/4.839 = 18.6$, a little more than the $15.0$ of normal
adjustment.

A microscope multiplies a linear magnification by an angular one: the objective
makes a real image at the far end of the tube, magnified by $L/f_o$ for tube length
$L$, and the eyepiece views it as a magnifier at $N/f_e$. With
$f_o = 0.800$ cm, $f_e = 2.00$ cm, $L = 16.0$ cm and $N = 25.0$ cm:
$(16.0/0.800)(25.0/2.00) = (20.0)(12.5) = 250$.

## Recognising it

The signal is an object, an element with a curvature or a focal length, and a
question about where or how big. Then the decision tree is short.

One thin lens or one mirror, with $f$ given: the thin lens equation and $m =
-d_i/d_o$, and read the signs off the answers rather than guessing at the start.

A radius given instead of a focal length: $f = R/2$ if it is a mirror, the
lensmaker's equation if it is a lens, and the single-surface equation if the light
stops inside the glass rather than coming out the other side. That last case is the
tell for a glass sphere, a fish tank, or a bug in amber.

More than one element, or a silvered surface: chain them, one at a time, image
becomes object, and expect at least one negative distance. Distances from focal
points rather than from the lens: Newton's form. A rate of change: differentiate and
pick up the factor $m^2$. Angles rather than heights, or an eye at the end: angular
magnification, and ask where the final image is meant to sit, at infinity or at the
near point.

Every one of those is the same equation wearing a different hat. The trouble is
never the algebra, it is the signs, and the only cure is writing the convention
down before the first line.
