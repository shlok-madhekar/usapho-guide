In the last lesson you found the field of a charged rod, a ring and a disc by
chopping the charge into pieces, writing $d\vec E$ for each piece, and adding
the pieces up. It works. It is also slow, and for a solid ball of charge the
integral is genuinely unpleasant.

Now picture a ball of plastic, 5 cm across, with charge spread evenly through
it. You want the field 15 cm from the centre. The honest way is a triple
integral over the volume. The answer, when it finally comes out, is
$kQ/r^2$ — exactly what you would get if all the charge sat at a single point
in the middle. That is too clean to be a coincidence. Something is making the
details of the distribution irrelevant, and if you can find out what, you can
skip the integral entirely.

## Flux: counting how much field crosses a surface

Start with something simpler than a ball. Take a uniform field $\vec E$ — every
arrow the same length, all pointing the same way — and hold a flat panel of
area $A$ in it. Define the **electric flux** through the panel as

$$\Phi_E = E A \cos\theta$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the field and the **normal** to the panel,
the direction sticking straight out of its face. If you hold the panel square-on
to the field, $\theta = 0$, $\cos\theta = 1$, and the flux is $EA$: the maximum.
Turn it edge-on and $\theta = 90^\circ$, so the flux is zero — the field slides
past without crossing. The units are $\mathrm{N\cdot m^2/C}$.

The picture to keep is field lines threading a hoop. Flux counts lines through
the surface. Tilting the hoop shrinks the opening the lines see, and $\cos\theta$
is exactly that shrinking factor.

<Figure caption={<><b>Figure 1.</b> A flat panel in a uniform field. Drag the tilt
angle and watch the flux fall as the panel turns edge-on. Notice the flux hits
zero at ninety degrees even though the field is as strong as ever.</>}>
  <Sim id="gauss-law-flux" />
</Figure>

Real surfaces are curved and real fields are not uniform, so you cut the surface
into patches small enough that each is flat and the field across it is constant,
then add:

$$\Phi_E = \oint \vec E \cdot d\vec A$$

The circle on the integral means the surface is **closed** — it seals off a
volume, like a balloon, with no edges. For a closed surface everyone agrees to
point the normal outward, so flux going out counts positive and flux coming in
counts negative.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Flux is a number, not a vector. It can be positive, negative or zero, and its
  sign on a closed surface tells you whether field is net leaving or net
  entering.
</Aside>

## Where the law comes from

Put a single point charge $q$ at the origin and wrap it in a sphere of radius
$r$ centred on it. Two things are true on that sphere and both matter. The field
has magnitude $E = kq/r^2$ everywhere on it, the same number at every point,
because every point is the same distance from the charge. And the field points
radially outward, which is exactly the direction of the outward normal, so
$\cos\theta = 1$ everywhere.

That makes the integral trivial. $E$ is constant, so it comes out front, and
what is left is just the total area of a sphere, $4\pi r^2$:

$$\Phi_E = \frac{kq}{r^2}\cdot 4\pi r^2 = 4\pi k q$$

The $r^2$ cancelled. The flux does not care how big you made the sphere: push it
out twice as far and the field is four times weaker but the area is four times
larger. Writing Coulomb's constant as $k = 1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$, the $4\pi$
cancels too and you are left with

$$\oint \vec E \cdot d\vec A = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}$$

This is **Gauss's law**. $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F/m}$ is
the permittivity of free space. $Q_{\text{enc}}$ is the total charge sealed
inside the surface — inside, nothing else.

Two extensions turn this from a statement about spheres into a general law.
First, the sphere was not special: distort it into any shape you like and the
field lines that left through the sphere still have to leave through the new
surface, since lines from a point charge do not stop or turn around in empty
space. Second, fields add, so flux adds. A collection of charges gives the sum
of their individual fluxes, and any charge sitting *outside* the surface
contributes zero — its lines go in one side and out the other, cancelling.

That last point is the one to hold on to. The flux depends only on the enclosed
charge. The field $\vec E$ in the integral is the field of *everything*, near
and far. Those two facts live together comfortably and confuse almost everyone
at first.

## Worked example 1: flux with no integral in sight

A uniform field of $250\ \mathrm{N/C}$ points horizontally. A flat panel
$0.40\ \mathrm{m}$ by $0.30\ \mathrm{m}$ is tilted so its normal makes $60^\circ$
with the field. Find the flux.

Area first: $A = 0.40 \times 0.30 = 0.12\ \mathrm{m^2}$. Then

$$\Phi_E = EA\cos\theta = 250 \times 0.12 \times \cos 60^\circ = 250 \times 0.12 \times 0.5 = 15\ \mathrm{N\cdot m^2/C}$$

Now a closed-surface version. A sealed instrument housing is probed and the net
outward flux through it is $-6.20\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{N\cdot m^2/C}$. What is
the charge inside? Rearranging Gauss's law, $Q_{\text{enc}} = \varepsilon_0 \Phi_E$:

$$Q_{\text{enc}} = (8.85\times10^{-12})(-6.20\times10^{5}) = -5.49\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{C}$$

Negative flux, negative charge. You never had to know where in the housing the
charge sat, or how it was shaped.

Symmetry can hand you flux fractions for free. A point charge $q$ at the centre
of a cube sends total flux $q/\varepsilon_0$ out, and the six faces are
identical, so each takes one sixth. For $q = 4.50\ \mu\mathrm{C}$:

$$\Phi_{\text{face}} = \frac{4.50\times10^{-6}}{6 \times 8.85\times10^{-12}} = \frac{4.50\times10^{-6}}{5.31\times10^{-11}} = 8.47\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{N\cdot m^2/C}$$

Put the charge at a *corner* instead and only an eighth of its field enters the
box at all, so the box catches $q/(8\varepsilon_0)$. The three faces touching
that corner are edge-on to the charge and take nothing; the other three split the
eighth equally, giving $q/(24\varepsilon_0)$ each. For $3.0\ \mu\mathrm{C}$ that
is $1.41\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{N\cdot m^2/C}$. Half a sphere catches half:
a hemispherical dome over a charge on a table passes $q/(2\varepsilon_0)$.

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  Gauss's law gives you flux from enclosed charge, not the other way round.
  Knowing the flux through one face of a lopsided box tells you nothing about
  the charge unless symmetry lets you say what fraction of the total that face
  caught. Symmetry is what makes the fraction knowable.
</Aside>

## The three symmetries that make it a calculation tool

Gauss's law is always true and usually useless for finding $E$, because $E$ sits
inside an integral you cannot undo. It becomes a tool only when you can choose a
closed surface — a **Gaussian surface**, imaginary, drawn wherever you please —
on which two conditions hold: $E$ has the same magnitude everywhere on it, and
$\vec E$ is either perpendicular to it (so $\cos\theta = 1$) or parallel to it
(so the flux through that part is zero). Then $\oint \vec E \cdot d\vec A = EA$
and you can divide.

Exactly three charge arrangements give you that, and between them they cover
almost every problem you will meet.

<Figure caption={<><b>Figure 2.</b> The three Gaussian surfaces. Note the flat ends
of the cylinder and the curved side of the pillbox carry no flux at all, which is
why only one area ever appears in the algebra.</>}>
  <Sim id="gauss-law-surfaces" />
</Figure>

**Spherical symmetry** — a ball, a shell, or any charge density that depends
only on distance from a centre. Use a concentric sphere of radius $r$, area
$4\pi r^2$:

$$E \cdot 4\pi r^2 = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} = \frac{k Q_{\text{enc}}}{r^2}$$

Outside everything, $Q_{\text{enc}}$ is the whole charge and the field is that of
a point charge at the centre — the coincidence from the opening, explained.

**Cylindrical symmetry** — a long straight line, rod, or beam. Use a coaxial can
of radius $r$ and length $L$. The flat ends are parallel to the field so they
contribute nothing, and the curved side has area $2\pi r L$:

$$E \cdot 2\pi r L = \frac{\lambda L}{\varepsilon_0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = \frac{2k\lambda}{r}$$

where $\lambda$ is charge per unit length. The $L$ cancels, as it must, since an
infinite wire has no special place along it. Note the $1/r$, not $1/r^2$.

**Planar symmetry** — a large flat sheet. Use a **pillbox**: a short cylinder
poking through the sheet with one flat face on each side. The curved side is
parallel to the field and carries nothing; each flat face of area $A$ carries
$EA$, so the total is $2EA$, and the enclosed charge is $\sigma A$ where $\sigma$
is charge per unit area:

$$2EA = \frac{\sigma A}{\varepsilon_0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$$

No $r$ at all. The field of a big sheet is the same one millimetre away and one
metre away. That is what a thundercloud base is: for air to break down at
$3.0\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{N/C}$ you need
$\sigma = 2\varepsilon_0 E = 2(8.85\times10^{-12})(3.0\times10^{6}) = 5.3\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{C/m^2}$.

## Worked example 2: inside and outside a ball of charge

A plastic ball of radius $R = 5.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ carries $Q = 8.0\ \mathrm{nC}$
spread uniformly through its volume. Find the field at $r = 15\ \mathrm{cm}$ and
at $r = 2.0\ \mathrm{cm}$.

Outside is quick. The Gaussian sphere at $r = 0.15\ \mathrm{m}$ encloses all
$8.0\ \mathrm{nC}$:

$$E = \frac{kQ}{r^2} = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(8.0\times10^{-9})}{(0.15)^2} = \frac{71.9}{0.0225} = 3.2\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{N/C}$$

Inside is where the thinking is. A Gaussian sphere at $r = 0.020\ \mathrm{m}$ does
not enclose all the charge — only the part within $0.020\ \mathrm{m}$ of the
centre. Because the density is uniform, the enclosed charge is the total times
the fraction of the volume you have grabbed:

$$Q_{\text{enc}} = Q\,\frac{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3}{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3} = Q\frac{r^3}{R^3}$$

Feed that back in:

$$E = \frac{k Q r^3 / R^3}{r^2} = \frac{kQ}{R^3}\,r$$

The field inside grows *linearly* with $r$: zero at the centre, maximum at the
surface. Numerically, $r^3/R^3 = (0.020/0.050)^3 = 0.4^3 = 0.064$, so
$Q_{\text{enc}} = 8.0 \times 0.064 = 0.512\ \mathrm{nC}$, and

$$E = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(0.512\times10^{-9})}{(0.020)^2} = \frac{4.60}{4.0\times10^{-4}} = 1.15\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{N/C}$$

Stronger inside than at 15 cm, even though less charge is doing the work — you
are much closer. The two formulas agree at $r = R$, as they must.

<Figure caption={<><b>Figure 3.</b> A uniformly charged ball. Drag the radius of the
Gaussian sphere and read the enclosed charge and field. Watch the graph: a straight
line up to the surface, then an inverse-square fall. The kink at the surface is the
whole lesson in one picture.</>}>
  <Sim id="gauss-law-sphere" />
</Figure>

This linear inside-field has a famous consequence. Bore a tunnel through a
diameter and drop a charge $-e$ into it. The force is
$F = -eE = -(keQ/R^3)\,r$ — proportional to displacement and pointing back to
the centre. That is Hooke's law, so the charge oscillates with
$\omega = \sqrt{keQ/(m R^3)}$ and frequency $f = \omega/2\pi$. The rule from
springs transfers unchanged; only the meaning of the stiffness has changed.

## Worked example 3: conductors, cavities and shells

A conductor in equilibrium has $\vec E = 0$ inside the metal. It has to: if any
field survived, the free charges would still be moving, and you would not have
equilibrium. Draw a Gaussian surface anywhere inside the metal and the flux is
zero, so the enclosed charge is zero. Excess charge can only live on surfaces.

Now the cavity. A neutral metal block has a hole in it, and you drop
$+7.5\ \mathrm{nC}$ inside the hole. Draw your Gaussian surface in the metal,
wrapping the cavity. Flux zero, so enclosed charge zero, so the cavity wall must
carry exactly $-7.5\ \mathrm{nC}$. The block was neutral, so $+7.5\ \mathrm{nC}$
is pushed to the outer surface. It does not matter that the charge sits
off-centre in the cavity; the argument never used its position.

Here is a fuller version. A $4.0\ \mathrm{nC}$ ball of radius $3.0\ \mathrm{cm}$
sits inside a metal shell carrying net $6.0\ \mathrm{nC}$, inner radius
$8.0\ \mathrm{cm}$, outer radius $10\ \mathrm{cm}$. Find $E$ at
$r = 25\ \mathrm{cm}$. A sphere at $0.25\ \mathrm{m}$ encloses everything:
$4.0 + 6.0 = 10.0\ \mathrm{nC}$. So

$$E = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(10.0\times10^{-9})}{(0.25)^2} = \frac{89.9}{0.0625} = 1.44\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{N/C}$$

The shell's inner surface carries $-4.0\ \mathrm{nC}$ and its outer surface
$+10.0\ \mathrm{nC}$, but for a point outside, only the total mattered.

which is what forces the induced charges to be what they are.</>}>
  <Sim id="gauss-law-shell" />

The same reasoning runs coaxially. A rod of radius $1.0\ \mathrm{mm}$ carrying
$\lambda = 10\ \mathrm{nC/m}$ inside a grounded tube of inner radius
$5.0\ \mathrm{mm}$: in the gap the field is $2k\lambda/r$, largest where $r$ is
smallest, which is right at the rod's surface. So
$E_{\max} = 2(8.99\times10^{9})(10\times10^{-9})/(1.0\times10^{-3}) = 1.80\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{N/C}$.
Breakdown always starts at the thin conductor.

## Worked example 4: when the density is not uniform

A sphere of radius $R = 15\ \mathrm{cm}$ has $\rho(r) = \rho_0(1 - r/R)$: dense
at the centre, fading to nothing at the rim. The field rises from zero, peaks
somewhere, then falls off as $1/r^2$ outside. Where is the peak?

Uniform-density shortcuts are gone, so build $Q_{\text{enc}}$ honestly. Slice the
interior into thin shells of radius $s$ and thickness $ds$, each of volume
$4\pi s^2\,ds$:

$$Q_{\text{enc}}(r) = \int_0^r \rho_0\left(1 - \frac{s}{R}\right) 4\pi s^2\,ds = 4\pi\rho_0\left(\frac{r^3}{3} - \frac{r^4}{4R}\right)$$

Now Gauss's law, dividing by $4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2$:

$$E(r) = \frac{\rho_0}{\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{r}{3} - \frac{r^2}{4R}\right)$$

To maximise, differentiate and set to zero:

$$\frac{dE}{dr} = \frac{\rho_0}{\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{3} - \frac{r}{2R}\right) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \frac{2R}{3}$$

With $R = 0.15\ \mathrm{m}$ that is $r = 0.10\ \mathrm{m}$ — inside the sphere,
not at its surface. Whenever the density falls off fast enough, the outer shells
add less charge than the growing $r^2$ costs you, and the field turns over early.

The same integral handles any radial $\rho(r)$. Try $\rho = C/r$: then
$Q_{\text{enc}} = \int_0^r (C/s)4\pi s^2 ds = 2\pi C r^2$, and
$E = 2\pi C r^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2) = C/(2\varepsilon_0)$, independent of
$r$. A constant field everywhere inside. To get
$1.00\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{N/C}$ you need
$C = 2\varepsilon_0 E = 1.77\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{C/m^2}$.

## When symmetry breaks: superposition rescues you

An off-centre hole in a charged ball has no symmetry at all, so no Gaussian
surface helps. Superposition does. A ball with a spherical cavity is the same
thing as a full ball of density $+\rho$ plus a smaller ball of density $-\rho$
filling the hole. Each piece is spherically symmetric about its own centre, and
inside a uniform ball the field at displacement $\vec r$ from its centre is
$\vec E = \rho\vec r/(3\varepsilon_0)$ — just rewrite $kQ r/R^3$ using
$Q = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3\rho$ and watch $R$ cancel.

Add the two at a point in the cavity, with $\vec d$ the vector from the big
centre to the small one:

$$\vec E = \frac{\rho \vec r_1}{3\varepsilon_0} - \frac{\rho \vec r_2}{3\varepsilon_0} = \frac{\rho(\vec r_1 - \vec r_2)}{3\varepsilon_0} = \frac{\rho \vec d}{3\varepsilon_0}$$

The point you chose dropped out. The field inside the cavity is uniform. For
$\rho = 2.5\ \mu\mathrm{C/m^3}$ and $d = 3.0\ \mathrm{cm}$,
$E = (2.5\times10^{-6})(0.030)/(3 \times 8.85\times10^{-12}) = 2.8\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{N/C}$.
Two overlapping spheres of opposite density work the identical way, giving a
uniform field $\rho d/(3\varepsilon_0)$ throughout the lens of overlap.

## How to recognise a Gauss's law problem

Reach for Gauss's law when you see one of these:

The problem says **flux**, or gives you a closed surface and asks what is inside.
That is Gauss's law used as a bookkeeping statement, and no field calculation is
needed at all.

The charge has one of the **three symmetries** and the question asks for a field.
Words like *sphere*, *ball*, *shell*, *long straight wire*, *beam*, *coaxial*,
*large sheet*, *wide slab*, *parallel plates* are the tell. Draw the matching
surface, ask what charge is inside it, and divide by the area.

The question asks for the field **inside** a charged object. Direct integration
inside a volume is brutal; Gauss's law is one line. The phrase "at a radius
smaller than $R$" should make you reach for it immediately.

There is a **conductor** anywhere in the problem. $E = 0$ inside metal plus
Gauss's law determines every induced charge in the setup.

And reach for superposition instead when the symmetry is broken but the pieces
are symmetric: cavities, overlapping spheres, two sheets, a wire beside a plane.

The one thing Gauss's law will not give you is the field of a finite rod or a
ring off-axis. No surface has constant $E$ on it there, so you go back to the
integration of the previous lesson. Knowing which tool a problem wants is most
of the skill.

Next you will stop tracking the field vector and start tracking a single number
per point in space, which turns most of these vector problems into arithmetic:
[electric potential](/guide/electric-potential).
