A baseball leaves a pitcher's hand at 40 m/s and a catcher stops it dead. For
the few milliseconds the ball is squashing into the leather, the force between
them is enormous, wildly non-constant, and completely unknown. You cannot write
$F = ma$ for that, because you do not know $F$ at any instant, and $a$ changes
from zero to something huge and back again in the time it takes a fly to beat
its wings.

And yet a catcher can tell you exactly how hard the catch felt, and you can
predict it. The trick is to stop asking what the force is at each instant and
ask instead what the force does *in total, over the whole contact*. That total
turns out to be an easy number even when the force is a mess. This lesson is
about that number.

## The honest form of Newton's second law

In [Power](/guide/power) you tracked how fast energy moved. Here you track
something else that a force delivers: motion itself, weighted by mass.

Define the **momentum** of an object of mass $m$ moving with velocity
$\vec v$ as

$$\vec p = m\vec v$$

It is a vector, pointing wherever the velocity points, and its units are
$\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}$. A 72 kg runner at 6.5 m/s carries
$72 \times 6.5 = 468\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}$.

Now look at what Newton actually wrote. Not $F = ma$, but

$$\vec F_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\vec p}{dt}$$

The net force on something is the rate at which its momentum changes. If the
mass is constant this collapses to the familiar version, since
$d(m\vec v)/dt = m\, d\vec v/dt = m\vec a$. But keep the momentum form and you
keep problems where the mass is *not* constant: a rocket throwing fuel out the
back, a chain landing on a scale, a jet of water hitting a wall. Those all
break $F = ma$ and none of them break $\vec F = d\vec p/dt$.

One more thing worth having in your pocket. Kinetic energy is
$K = \tfrac12 mv^2$ and $p = mv$, so $p^2 = m^2v^2 = 2m\left(\tfrac12 mv^2\right)$,
giving

$$p = \sqrt{2mK}$$

A 2.0 kg object with 36 J of kinetic energy therefore has
$p = \sqrt{2 \times 2.0 \times 36} = \sqrt{144} = 12\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}$.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  Momentum is a vector; kinetic energy is a scalar. Two objects with equal and
  opposite momenta have zero total momentum but plenty of total kinetic energy.
  That difference is what makes the two conservation laws say different things
  about a collision.
</Aside>

## Impulse is the area under the force-time curve

Take $\vec F_{\text{net}} = d\vec p/dt$, multiply both sides by $dt$, and add
up every instant from the start of the contact to the end:

$$\vec J \equiv \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \vec F\, dt = \vec p_2 - \vec p_1 = \Delta\vec p$$

That is the **impulse-momentum theorem**, and $\vec J$ is called the
**impulse**. In words: the total accumulated force-times-time is exactly the
change in momentum, and nothing else about the force matters. The spike can be
any shape at all. Only the area under it counts.

It has the same relationship to $\vec F = d\vec p/dt$ that the work-energy
theorem has to $F = ma$: one integrates force over *distance* and gives you a
scalar change in energy, the other integrates force over *time* and gives you a
vector change in momentum. Which one a problem wants is usually decided by
whether it hands you a distance or a duration.

If the force is constant, the integral is a rectangle and $\vec J = \vec F\Delta t$.
A 40 N force acting for 0.25 s delivers $40 \times 0.25 = 10\ \mathrm{N\cdot s}$.
And $\mathrm{N\cdot s}$ is the same unit as $\mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}$, since
$1\ \mathrm{N} = 1\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m/s^2}$. That is not a coincidence; it is the
theorem.

If the force is not constant, you still want the area, and you can define an
**average force** $\bar F$ as the constant force that would give the same area:

$$\bar F\, \Delta t = \Delta p$$

**Worked example 1.** A collision force is modelled as
$F(t) = F_0\sin(\pi t/T)$ with $F_0 = 900\ \mathrm{N}$ and $T = 0.040\ \mathrm{s}$,
acting from $t = 0$ to $t = T$. Find the total impulse.

The force is not constant, so integrate:

$$

J = \int_0^{T} F_0\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right) dt
   = F_0\left[-\frac{T}{\pi}\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right)\right]_0^{T}
   = \frac{F_0 T}{\pi}\left(\cos 0 - \cos\pi\right) = \frac{2F_0T}{\pi}

$$

Now the numbers:

$$J = \frac{2 \times 900 \times 0.040}{\pi} = \frac{72.0}{3.14159} = 22.9\ \mathrm{N\cdot s}$$

Notice the average force that hides in there: $\bar F = J/T = 22.9/0.040 = 573\ \mathrm{N}$,
which is $2/\pi \approx 0.637$ of the peak. A half-sine hump is about two-thirds
as effective as a square pulse of the same height.

## Stretch the time and the force drops

Rearrange $\bar F \Delta t = \Delta p$ into

$$\bar F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}$$

and read it as a bargain you are allowed to strike. The change in momentum is
usually fixed by the physics of the situation: a falling egg arrives with the
speed it arrives with, and it has to end at rest. You cannot argue with
$\Delta p$. What you *can* control is $\Delta t$. Double the stopping time and
you halve the force.

This is every piece of safety equipment ever built. Airbags, crumple zones,
crash mats, bending your knees on landing, drawing your hand back as you catch.
None of them reduce the momentum you have to get rid of. All of them lengthen
the time over which you get rid of it.

</Figure>

**Worked example 2.** A 0.20 kg ball hits a wall horizontally at 8.0 m/s and
rebounds at 6.0 m/s. Contact lasts 0.015 s. Find the impulse on the ball and
the average force.

First thing on blank paper: a positive direction. Take *towards the wall* as
positive. Then $v_1 = +8.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $v_2 = -6.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$, because
the ball comes back.

$$

p_1 = 0.20 \times 8.0 = 1.6\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}, \qquad
p_2 = 0.20 \times (-6.0) = -1.2\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}

$$

$$J = \Delta p = p_2 - p_1 = -1.2 - 1.6 = -2.8\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}$$

Magnitude 2.8 N·s, directed away from the wall. The signs did the work: if you
had subtracted the speeds instead of the velocities you would have got
$0.20 \times 2.0 = 0.4\ \mathrm{N\cdot s}$, seven times too small. The average
force is

$$\bar F = \frac{2.8}{0.015} = 187\ \mathrm{N}$$

<Aside label="Where this goes wrong" warn>
  A reversal counts double, and this is the single most common momentum error.
  A ball that hits a wall at $v$ and bounces back at $v$ has
  $|\Delta p| = 2mv$, not zero and not $mv$. A ball that sticks has
  $|\Delta p| = mv$. So the bouncing ball pushes twice as hard on the wall as
  the sticking one, even though it "ends up going the same speed it arrived".
  Write signed components before you touch a calculator.
</Aside>

## Every force acting during the interval counts

The theorem is about the *net* force. If something other than the impact is
pushing during the contact time, it contributes its own impulse and you have to
include it.

**Worked example 3.** A 0.055 kg egg falls 1.2 m and is brought to rest by a
pillow over 0.15 s. What average force does the pillow exert? Use
$g = 9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.

Two stages. First the fall, to find the arrival speed:

$$v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 1.2} = \sqrt{23.52} = 4.850\ \mathrm{m/s}$$

Now the stop. Take upward as positive, so the egg arrives with
$v_1 = -4.850\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and ends at $v_2 = 0$:

$$\Delta p = m(v_2 - v_1) = 0.055 \times (0 - (-4.850)) = +0.2668\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m/s}$$

During those 0.15 s, two forces act: the pillow pushing up with $\bar N$, and
gravity pulling down with $mg = 0.055 \times 9.8 = 0.539\ \mathrm{N}$. The net
impulse is what equals $\Delta p$:

$$(\bar N - mg)\,\Delta t = \Delta p$$

$$

\bar N = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} + mg = \frac{0.2668}{0.15} + 0.539
 = 1.778 + 0.539 = 2.32\ \mathrm{N}

$$

Dropping the gravity term would have given 1.78 N, about 23 % low. Whether that
matters depends on how short the stop is: for a 0.002 s impact against concrete,
the weight term is invisible next to a force of hundreds of newtons, and that is
exactly why you are allowed to ignore gravity during a genuine collision. Here
the stop is slow enough that gravity gets a word in.

## When mass arrives instead of speeding up

Now use the form of the law that $F = ma$ cannot reach. Suppose a stream of
material arrives steadily at a surface: water from a hose, sand onto a
conveyor, air pushed down by a drone. Nothing is accelerating in the ordinary
sense. Instead, in every second, a mass $dm$ has its velocity changed by
$\Delta v$. The momentum delivered per second is

$$F = \frac{dp}{dt} = \frac{dm}{dt}\,\Delta v$$

Read $dm/dt$ as "kilograms arriving per second" and $\Delta v$ as "how much each
kilogram's velocity changes". Multiply. That is the force.

Water at 4.0 kg/s hitting a wall at 15 m/s and dribbling down gives
$4.0 \times 15 = 60\ \mathrm{N}$. Water at 6.0 kg/s at 10 m/s turned completely
around by a curved vane changes each kilogram's velocity by
$10 - (-10) = 20\ \mathrm{m/s}$, so the force is
$6.0 \times 20 = 120\ \mathrm{N}$.

A drone hovering needs thrust equal to its weight, so
$\dot m \times 18 = 32\ \mathrm{N}$ gives $\dot m = 1.78\ \mathrm{kg/s}$ of air
pushed down. A rocket engine is the same sum run backwards: ejecting exhaust at
rate $\dot m$ with speed $u$ relative to the rocket produces thrust
$F = \dot m u$, so 3.0 kg/s at 2500 m/s is 7500 N.

**Worked example 4.** A uniform chain of linear density
$\lambda = 1.5\ \mathrm{kg/m}$ is released from rest with its lower end just
touching a scale. What does the scale read when 0.80 m has landed?

This one takes a moment. The scale supports two separate things.

The first is the *weight of the chain already lying on it*: a length
$x = 0.80\ \mathrm{m}$ of chain, mass $\lambda x = 1.5 \times 0.80 = 1.2\ \mathrm{kg}$,
weighing $1.2 \times 9.8 = 11.76\ \mathrm{N}$.

The second is the force needed to *stop the links that are landing right now*.
The chain fell freely through $x = 0.80$ m, so the arriving links have speed
$v = \sqrt{2gx} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 0.80} = 3.960\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Chain
arrives at a rate $dm/dt = \lambda v$ kilograms per second, and each kilogram
has its speed cut from $v$ to zero. So the stopping force is

$$

F_{\text{stop}} = \frac{dm}{dt}\,v = \lambda v \cdot v = \lambda v^2
 = 1.5 \times (3.960)^2 = 1.5 \times 15.68 = 23.52\ \mathrm{N}

$$

Since $v^2 = 2gx$, that term is exactly $2\lambda g x$, twice the weight of the
landed part. The scale reads

$$N = \lambda g x + 2\lambda g x = 3\lambda g x = 3 \times 11.76 = 35.3\ \mathrm{N}$$

Three times the weight of what is lying there. The same structure solves the
rope pulled off a heap: if you drag rope of density 0.90 kg/m horizontally at a
constant 2.0 m/s, you set $\lambda v = 1.8$ kg of rope into motion each second
and each kilogram goes from 0 to 2.0 m/s, so
$F = \lambda v^2 = 0.90 \times 4.0 = 3.6\ \mathrm{N}$.

## Rockets: when the mass leaves

A rocket in deep space throws mass backwards to push itself forwards. In a
short time $dt$ it ejects mass $-dm$ (negative because the rocket is losing it)
at speed $u$ relative to itself. Total momentum is conserved, so the momentum
the exhaust carries backwards equals the momentum the rocket gains forwards:

$$m\,dv = -u\,dm \quad\Longrightarrow\quad dv = -u\,\frac{dm}{m}$$

Integrate from the initial mass $m_0$ to the final mass $m_f$:

$$\Delta v = -u\int_{m_0}^{m_f}\frac{dm}{m} = u\ln\!\frac{m_0}{m_f}$$

That is the rocket equation. What it says is brutal: speed grows only with the
*logarithm* of the mass ratio, so doubling your fuel does not double your speed.
A 2000 kg rocket burning down to 800 kg with $u = 2800$ m/s, starting from rest,
reaches

$$

v = 2800 \times \ln(2000/800) = 2800 \times \ln 2.5 = 2800 \times 0.9163
 = 2566\ \mathrm{m/s}

$$

Launching from the ground, gravity spends the whole burn taking momentum back,
delivering an impulse $-mg\,dt$ that costs you $g\,t_{\text{burn}}$ of speed:

$$v = u\ln\!\frac{m_0}{m_f} - g\,t_{\text{burn}}$$

For 5000 kg down to 2000 kg over 50 s with $u = 2400$ m/s:
$2400 \times \ln 2.5 = 2400 \times 0.9163 = 2199.1\ \mathrm{m/s}$, minus
$9.8 \times 50 = 490\ \mathrm{m/s}$, leaving $1709\ \mathrm{m/s}$.

<Aside label="Worth remembering">
  In the rocket equation $u$ is the exhaust speed <i>relative to the rocket</i>,
  not relative to the ground, and the mass ratio is initial over final. If your
  answer comes out negative you have the ratio upside down.
</Aside>

## How to tell this is an impulse problem

Reach for impulse and momentum when you see any of these:

- **A duration, a contact time, or an average force.** "Stopped over 0.08 s",
  "the bat is in contact for 1.2 ms". Energy methods want a distance; this one
  wants a time.
- **A force given as a function of time**, or a force-time graph. The question
  is asking you for an area.
- **You are not told the force and do not want to know it.** Collisions,
  catches, kicks, explosions. You know the before and the after, and the middle
  is a mess you are allowed to skip.
- **Mass per second.** "kg/s", "20 balls per second", "burns fuel at a constant
  rate", a chain landing, a belt being loaded. That is $F = \dot m\,\Delta v$.
- **Something reverses.** The moment a problem says "rebounds" or "is turned
  around", it is testing whether you will write the minus sign.

And a last check on any answer: momentum is a vector, so every one of these
should have been done axis by axis with a chosen positive direction. If you only
ever worked with speeds, go back and look for a sign you dropped.
